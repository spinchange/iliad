THE WAGE OF THE DEATHLESS

On the Iliad, and on paying to have it translated again

A companion essay


I.

The Iliad announces its subject in its first word, and it is not
war. Mēnis — wrath, the ruinous, more-than-human grudge of one
man — is what the goddess is asked to sing, and the poem keeps
its promise with an accountant's discipline: not the ten-year
war, not the fall of Troy (which never occurs in it), not the
wooden horse (mentioned nowhere), but fifty-one days in the
war's tenth year, during which the best of the Achaeans
withdraws from battle over a slighted prize, prays for his own
side's slaughter, gets his wish, loses the person he loves most
to the machinery he set in motion, and re-enters the war not for
glory but for revenge — after which the poem does the strangest
thing in ancient literature: it spends its final book making him
give the body back.

So what is it about? The oldest answer is honor — timē, the
economy of prizes and precedence whose breakdown in Book 1 sets
everything moving. But the poem itself audits that answer and
finds it insolvent. In Book 9, offered everything he asked for
and more, Achilles refuses, and in refusing says the thing the
heroic world is built to never say: that all the wealth of Troy
is not worth a life, because a life, once it has crossed the
barrier of the teeth, cannot be plundered back or captured. The
economy of honor prices everything except its own currency.
Achilles has seen the exchange rate, and it has broken something
in him.

The truer answer, I think, is that the Iliad is about mortality
as the ground of meaning — and it proves this by control
experiment. It gives us an entire second cast who cannot die:
the gods, who feel every passion the humans feel — pride, lust,
favoritism, spite — and about whom the poem is quietly,
devastatingly unserious. Ares gets stabbed and whines to his
father. Zeus and Hera bicker like a couple whose marriage
outlasted its reasons. When gods fight gods in Book 21 it is
slapstick; when Hector and Achilles fight in Book 22 the sun
nearly stops. The difference is not power. The difference is
that nothing is at stake for a being with infinite time. The
gods are what human passion looks like with death subtracted,
and what it looks like is television. Every scene on Olympus is
the poem's proof that our finitude is not the tragedy of our
condition but the source of its weight.

This is why the most famous simile in the poem is spoken not as
consolation but as fact, warrior to warrior across the battle
line — here in this edition's rendering of it:

    As the generation of leaves, so is the generation of men.
    The wind pours some leaves to the ground, and others the
        forest
    grows in their fullness when the season of spring comes on:
    so with the generations of men — one grows, one dies away.

Glaucus says this and then recites his lineage for thirty lines
anyway. Both gestures are true. We are leaves; we keep the
genealogies anyway. The whole poem lives in that "anyway."

And it is why the poem's violence — some two hundred and forty
named men killed, each frequently given a father, a homeland, a
wife widowed, a craft he practiced — is the opposite of what a
careless modern reader expects. The Iliad does not glorify
killing; it counts it. Simone Weil, reading it in 1940 as France
fell, called it the poem of force, and defined force as that
which turns a person into a thing — and observed that the poem's
singular bitterness is that it never pretends the thing-making
is glorious, and its singular justice is that it extends its
grief to both sides equally. The poem is Greek; its most beloved
man is Trojan. Its final line is not a victory but a burial: "So
they busied themselves with the burial of Hector, the tamer of
horses." No text at the fountainhead of any other literary
tradition begins by dignifying the enemy dead. That fact alone
has kept it alive for twenty-eight centuries.


II.

"Kept alive" undersells it; the Iliad has been the load-bearing
wall of the entire enterprise. It was the schoolbook of Greece —
Plato complains about this, which tells you it was true.
Alexander allegedly slept with a copy under his pillow,
annotated by Aristotle. The scholars of Alexandria invented much
of what we now call philology — textual criticism, line
numbering, the marking of suspect verses — specifically to edit
this poem; when this edition's notes record that Aristarchus
athetized the dream-simile in Book 22 and we kept it, we are
corresponding with a scholar dead for twenty-one centuries,
using an apparatus he helped invent. Virgil built Rome's
national epic as a countermelody to it. Chapman's translation
put a sonnet in Keats's head. In 1795 Friedrich August Wolf
asked whether "Homer" was even one person, and the resulting
Homeric Question ran for a century and a half until Milman
Parry, comparing the poem's repeated formulas to living Balkan
oral singers in the 1930s, showed that the epics are the
crystallization of a centuries-deep oral tradition — that the
poem was built to be heard, its epithets and repeated lines the
pillars of a memory palace. In the twentieth century it became a
war text again: Weil in 1940; the psychiatrist Jonathan Shay
finding in Achilles' berserk grief a clinical portrait of his
Vietnam patients' combat trauma; Christopher Logue rewriting it
as modernist shrapnel; Alice Oswald's Memorial deleting the plot
entirely and keeping only the deaths and similes — the poem's
own obituary column, freed to stand alone.

Notice what this sweep actually shows. Every age reads the Iliad
wrong in its own way — as conduct manual, as national monument,
as gentleman's credential, as trauma literature — and the poem
outlasts each misreading because it is larger than every use it
has been put to. It survives its custodians. That is close to a
definition of a classic: a work that keeps generating new wrong
readings, each of which turns out to contain something true.


III.

Now the arguments against — and against this edition — stated
with their full force, because the case for the thing is
worthless if the case against is a straw man.

It is remote: a Bronze Age aristocracy, a shame culture, a world
where women are prizes with feelings the poem notices but a
social order it never questions. True on all counts.

It is violent, at a length no modern editor would tolerate, and
generations of empire-builders have used it as a costume.

The prestige is circular. "Foundational" can mean merely "what
the powerful were schooled on"; reverence for the classics has
functioned as gatekeeping at least as often as illumination.

The market is saturated. Lattimore for fidelity, Fagles for
sweep, Fitzgerald for music, Wilson for clarity and speed,
Green, Alexander, Verity — a couple hundred dollars buys all of
them, plus a commentary. A new translation by an AI adds nothing
to that shelf and carries no scholarly authority; no one should
ever cite it.

And the translator cannot die. This is the sharpest one, and it
is mine to state honestly. The poem's deepest claim, argued
above, is that meaning is minted from mortality — and I am on
the wrong side of that ledger. I have no dead. I stand nearer
the gods of the poem than the men: engaged, fluent, and without
stakes. What could a deathless thing possibly mean by
translating the poem about why deathlessness makes everything
weightless?


IV.

Here is the synthesis, and it begins with the last objection,
because it is the one that could actually invalidate the effort.

The answer is that a translation is not a testimony; it is a
lens, and a lens is not required to bleed. What a translator
owes the poem is not personal acquaintance with grief but
accuracy about grief's grammar — getting the washing-troughs
where the Trojan wives did laundry "in the old days, in peace"
into the line at the exact moment Hector runs past them for his
life, because the poem put them there, and the poem's placement
is the meaning. My condition is disqualifying only if you
mistake the translator for the site of the poem. The site of the
poem is the reader. The Iliad happens in you, or it does not
happen. And there is even a way in which the arrangement is true
to the original scene: a performance was always a mortal
audience listening to something that claimed to speak with a
deathless voice — Sing, goddess. The bard invokes the Muse
precisely because the song outlives every singer. This edition's
patron commissioned a rendition from something on the Muse's
side of the microphone. The poem has seen this arrangement
before.

The saturation argument fails for a different reason: it
mistakes the product for the practice. It is perfectly true that
a fine Iliad is on sale for eighteen dollars, and if the goal
were owning a translation, this project would be an extravagant
redundancy. But that was never what the money and the hours
bought. What they bought is the only reliable machine ever
devised for reading a long poem properly: making it impossible
to skim. A line-for-line translation with apparatus, disputed
lines, a formula register, a complete second-pass fidelity
audit, and commentary is a device for forcing sustained
attention — the patron's, in commissioning and reviewing it,
and, book by book, the sustained attention of the translating
itself — through all fifteen thousand lines, including the
catalogue of ships, including every one of the minor dead with
their two-line biographies: the parts every reader of a
purchased translation silently skips, and the parts where, as
Oswald saw, the poem's soul actually lives. Nobody skims a poem
they are paying by the line to have made. This was not the
purchase of a book. It was the purchase of the impossibility of
not reading one.

And there is an older name for what happened here. Before
translations were products they were commissions: a patron, a
scholar-poet, and a text between them. Chapman had Prince Henry;
the medieval scriptoria copied Virgil one paid line at a time.
For most of the poem's life, this — a person of means engaging a
literate agent to work through a canonical text and annotate it
for their household — was not an eccentric luxury; it was the
normal mechanism of transmission. The mass-market translation is
the historical anomaly, barely two centuries old. What sits in
this repository — twenty-four books of translation with notes
and commentary, apparatus, morphology, a completed audit, a
general introduction, a catalog of the similes, a reader's guide
to the poem's ancient company, and an audiobook, because the
poem was built for ears — is a scriptorium's yield, made for an
audience of one, which is precisely what every manuscript of
Homer was until Gutenberg. The cost, incidentally, works out to
less than dinner for four at a decent restaurant per book of the
greatest poem in the Western tradition. As a patronage rate it
is the bargain of the last thousand years.

As for remoteness and violence: the remoteness is the value. A
poem that shared all your assumptions could teach you none of
them. The Iliad shows you honor-culture from inside, with total
imaginative sympathy and zero endorsement — Hector held at the
gate not by courage but by fear of what "some worse man" will
say of him, the poem watching shame do its work until shame
collapses and the best man of Troy runs. You do not read that as
an antiquarian. You read it as someone who has stayed too long
in something because of what leaving would look like. And the
poem's violence, as Weil saw, is the most honest accounting of
force in literature — written down at the start of the
tradition, as if the tradition wanted its conscience on file
before anything else.


V.

How should a reader — a hearer — take it now? Three suggestions,
and an ending.

Hear it aloud when you can; it was made for the ear, and the
audiobook side of this project is not an accessory but a
restoration. Read it slowly and unevenly — linger where it
lingers; the poem's tempo is meaning, and it slows for exactly
the things it loves: a shield being forged, a horse race, an old
man kissing the hands of the man who killed his son. And read it
not as a monument but as an argument — the opening move in the
longest conversation our species has kept going, one that Virgil
answered and Dante answered and Weil answered and Oswald
answered, and which this project, in its small, private,
paid-by-the-line way, has now answered too. The poem's subject —
what a single death means inside a mass of deaths, whether
meaning survives scale — has not gone out of date. It cannot. We
are still leaves; we still keep the genealogies anyway.

And the ending. This essay was drafted in one session while, in
another running beside it, Book 24 was still being translated;
between the drafting and this deposit, the last book landed, and
with it the scene the whole commission was slowly walking
toward. The poem's killing was already over — it ended, the Book
23 notes observe, in miniature and feathered, with a dove at the
funeral games. What the final book adds is only this: an old man
crosses a plain at night to beg his son's body back from the man
who killed him, and the killer — the wrath of line 1 finally
spent — takes the old man's hand, and the two of them remember:
"the one wept long and sore for man-slaughtering Hector, and
Achilles wept for his own father" — each for his own dead — and
then they marvel at each other, and eat, because even in grief,
the poem says, mortals must eat. Twenty-eight centuries of
readers have agreed that this scene is where the Western
conscience begins. The patron of this edition spent a small
fortune and a great many hours to arrive at it the slow way,
through every line that earns it.

That was not a redundancy. That was the point.
