BOOK 9

The Embassy to Achilles: Agamemnon's gifts, three speeches, and three
refusals — each smaller than the last.

TRANSLATOR'S COMMENTARY

Book 9 is the poem's hinge and its greatest sustained writing: the offer
that should end the wrath, and the answer that reveals the wrath has
outgrown its cause. The book's architecture is a series of descents. The
gift-catalogue is spoken three times — by Agamemnon with its poison pill
("let him yield to me by as much as I am more kingly," 158-161), by
Odysseus verbatim but with the poison silently cut (note [20]), and back
through Achilles' contempt. And Achilles' three answers step downward:
to Odysseus, "tomorrow I sail"; to Phoenix, "at dawn we will decide";
to Ajax, "I fight when Hector reaches my own ships" — which is, of
course, exactly what he will do. Odysseus then reports only the first
answer (note [41]), and Diomedes closes the book with the truest
sentence in it: he will fight "whenever the heart in his chest commands
him, and the god rouses him."

All three curated disputes for this book are handled in place. The dual
verbs of 182-198 (disputed #5) are translated as duals — "the two of
them went" — with the crux laid out in note [15]. The "gates of Hades"
lines (312-313, disputed #6) and their contested referent are note
[21]. And the famous four lines in which Phoenix contemplated killing
his father (458-461, disputed #7) are physically absent from this
project's Greek text, as from the printed Monro-Allen vulgate — the
numbering jumps 457 to 462 — athetized by Aristarchus for their content
and preserved only through Plutarch; the translation reproduces the
gap, and note [32] supplies the missing lines so the reader loses
nothing but the illusion that the text was ever whole.

Watch also: Achilles heard singing "the fames of men" — the poem's only
image of epic performed, by its own hero, on a lyre looted from
Andromache's city (note [16]); the demolition of the honor-economy at
318-321; the two fates and "imperishable fame" (410-416); the Litai
allegory (note [34]); and the Meleager paradigm, at whose center sleeps
a wife named Cleopatra — Patroclus's name reversed — while Patroclus
sits listening (note [35]). Register notes: 18-25 reuse the Book 2
English verbatim, now spoken in earnest; ἄττα "old father" joins the
vocatives; δμῳαί "slave-women" appears twice more under the policy.

   1  So the Trojans kept their watches. But the Achaeans
   2  were held by unearthly Panic, the companion of icy Rout,[1]
   3  and all their best were stricken with grief past bearing.
   4  As two winds stir up the sea where the fish swarm,
   5  the North Wind and the West Wind, blowing out of Thrace,
   6  arriving suddenly: and all at once the dark wave
   7  crests, and heaps the seaweed out along the tide-line:
   8  so the heart was torn in the chests of the Achaeans.
   9  And Atreus' son, stricken to the heart with great grief,
  10  went to and fro, bidding the clear-voiced heralds
  11  summon every man by name to assembly,
  12  but not to shout; and he himself worked among the first.
  13  So they sat in assembly, dejected; and Agamemnon
  14  stood up, letting fall tears like a spring of dark water[2]
  15  that pours its black stream down a goat-proof cliff.
  16  So, groaning heavily, he spoke his words to the Argives:
  17  "Friends, leaders and lords of the Argives,
  18  Zeus, Cronos' great son, has bound me fast in heavy blindness —[3]
  19  the hard god: he promised me once, he bowed his head,
  20  that I should sack well-walled Ilion before returning;
  21  now he has planned a cruel deceit, and orders me
  22  back to Argos in disgrace, with half my people lost.
  23  Such, it seems, must be the pleasure of overmastering Zeus,
  24  who has undone the crowns of many cities
  25  and will undo more yet: his power is the greatest.
  26  Come then — as I say it, let us all obey:
  27  let us flee with the ships to our dear fatherland.
  28  We shall not take wide-wayed Troy now."
  29  So he spoke, and they all went still in silence.
  30  A long while the sons of the Achaeans sat mute, dejected;
  31  and at long last Diomedes, good at the war-cry, spoke:
  32  "Son of Atreus, it is you I will fight first — your folly —[4]
  33  where it is right, lord: in assembly. And do not be angered.
  34  My courage you insulted once before the Danaans:
  35  you called me unwarlike, without strength. And all this
  36  the Argives know, the young men and the old.
  37  To you the son of crooked-counseled Cronos gave by halves:
  38  with the scepter he gave you honor beyond all men,
  39  but courage he did not give you — and that is the greatest power.
  40  Strange man — do you really suppose the sons of the Achaeans
  41  are as unwarlike and strengthless as you say?
  42  If your own heart is set on going home,
  43  go: the way lies open, and your ships stand by the sea,
  44  the great fleet that followed you from Mycenae.
  45  But the other long-haired Achaeans will stay
  46  until we sack Troy. And if they too —
  47  well, let them flee with their ships to their dear fatherland;
  48  the two of us, Sthenelus and I, will fight on until we find
  49  the appointed end of Ilion: for we came here with the god."
  50  So he spoke, and all the sons of the Achaeans shouted,
  51  marveling at the word of Diomedes, breaker of horses.
  52  And the horseman Nestor stood up and spoke among them:
  53  "Tydeus' son, in war you are strong beyond others,
  54  and in counsel you are best of all your age-mates.
  55  Not a man of all the Achaeans will fault your word
  56  or speak against it. But you have not reached the end of words.
  57  You are young indeed — you could be my own son,
  58  the latest-born; and yet you speak good sense
  59  to the kings of the Argives, for you have spoken rightly.
  60  Come then, I — who claim to be older than you —
  61  will speak out and go through everything; and no one
  62  will slight my word, not even the lord Agamemnon.
  63  Clanless, lawless, hearthless: that is the man[5]
  64  who is in love with war among his own people — the horror of it.
  65  But for now, let us yield to black night
  66  and make our supper ready. Let the sentries
  67  post themselves along the dug ditch outside the wall —
  68  that is my charge to the young men. And then,
  69  son of Atreus, you lead: for you are most the king.
  70  Give the elders a feast: it becomes you; it is no disgrace.[6]
  71  Your huts are full of wine, which the Achaeans' ships
  72  bring over the wide sea from Thrace daily;
  73  all hosting is yours to give: you are king over many.
  74  With many gathered, you will heed the man who counsels
  75  the best counsel; and sorely do all the Achaeans need
  76  good counsel and shrewd — with the enemy this close to the ships,
  77  burning their fires past counting. Who could rejoice at that?
  78  This night will break the army, or will save it."
  79  So he spoke, and they listened well to him and obeyed.
  80  Out went the sentries in their armor, mustered
  81  around Nestor's son Thrasymedes, shepherd of the people,
  82  around Ascalaphus and Ialmenus, sons of Ares,
  83  around Meriones, and Aphareus, and Deïpyrus,
  84  and around Creon's son, brilliant Lycomedes.
  85  Seven were the captains of the sentries, and with each went
  86  a hundred young men carrying their long spears;
  87  and they went and took post between the ditch and wall,
  88  and there lit fires, and each man set about his supper.
  89  And Atreus' son led the gathered elders of the Achaeans
  90  to his hut, and set before them a feast to fill the heart;
  91  and they put out their hands to the good things lying ready.
  92  And when they had put away desire for eating and drinking,
  93  the old man first of all began to weave his counsel —
  94  Nestor, whose plan had shown itself best before as well.
  95  With good will toward them he spoke and addressed them:
  96  "Most glorious son of Atreus, lord of men Agamemnon,
  97  with you I will end, and with you begin: because you are
  98  lord of many peoples, and Zeus has put into your hands[7]
  99  the scepter and the ordinances, to take counsel for them.
 100  Therefore you above all must speak a word and listen,
 101  and act for another too, whenever his heart bids a man
 102  speak for the good: whatever he begins will hang on you.
 103  And I will say how it seems to me to be best.
 104  No other man will think a better thought than this —
 105  the thought I think, and thought long since and still,
 106  from that day, Zeus-born, when you went and took the girl
 107  Briseis out of the hut of Achilles in his anger —
 108  in no way by our judgment. I myself
 109  argued long against it; but you, yielding
 110  to your great-hearted spirit, dishonored the best of men,
 111  a man the immortals honor: for you took and keep his prize.
 112  Even now — even late — let us consider how to make amends
 113  and win him over with soothing gifts and with honeyed words."
 114  Then answered him the lord of men Agamemnon:
 115  "Old man, that was no false telling of my blindness.[8]
 116  Blinded I was — I do not deny it myself. Worth many
 117  hosts of men is the man whom Zeus loves in his heart:
 118  as now he has honored this man, and broken the Achaean host.
 119  But since I was blinded, and followed my wretched heart,
 120  I am willing to make amends, and to give ransom past counting.
 121  Before you all I will name the glorious gifts:[9]
 122  seven tripods untouched by fire, ten talents of gold,
 123  twenty gleaming cauldrons, and twelve horses,
 124  strong-built, prize-winners, who have taken races with their speed.
 125  No man who owned all they have won for me
 126  would be poor in goods, or lacking in precious gold —
 127  all the prizes those single-hoofed horses have carried home.
 128  And I will give seven women skilled in blameless work,
 129  women of Lesbos — the ones I chose when he himself
 130  took well-built Lesbos, who in beauty outdid the tribes of women.
 131  These I will give; and among them will be the one I took away:
 132  the daughter of Briseus. And I will swear the great oath besides:
 133  I never went up into her bed, I never lay with her,[10]
 134  as is the way of human kind, of men and women.
 135  All this shall be his at once. And if hereafter
 136  the gods grant us to sack the great city of Priam,
 137  let him heap his ship with gold and bronze
 138  when he comes in, when we Achaeans divide the spoil,
 139  and let him choose out twenty Trojan women for himself,
 140  the loveliest there are after Argive Helen.
 141  And if we come home to Achaean Argos, udder of the plowland,
 142  he can be my son-in-law: I will honor him the equal of Orestes,
 143  my late-born boy, reared in high plenty.
 144  I have three daughters in my strong-built hall,
 145  Chrysothemis, Laodice, and Iphianassa:[11]
 146  of these let him lead the one he chooses, without bride-price,
 147  to the house of Peleus; and I will add wedding-gifts
 148  in plenty — more than any man yet gave with his daughter.
 149  And I will give him seven well-peopled citadels:
 150  Cardamyle, and Enope, and grassy Hire,
 151  sacred Pherae, and Antheia of the deep meadows,
 152  beautiful Aipeia, and Pedasus of the vines —[12]
 153  all near the sea, on the edge of sandy Pylos,
 154  and in them live men rich in flocks and rich in cattle,
 155  who will honor him like a god with their gifts
 156  and render him, under his scepter, their shining dues.
 157  All this I will accomplish for him, if he leaves off his anger.
 158  Let him be broken. Hades is the one unappeasable,[13]
 159  the untamable — and for that, of all gods, mortals hate him most.
 160  And let him yield place to me, by as much as I am more kingly,
 161  by as much as I claim to be the elder-born."
 162  Then answered him the Gerenian horseman Nestor:
 163  "Most glorious son of Atreus, lord of men Agamemnon,
 164  the gifts you offer lord Achilles now — no man could scorn them.
 165  Come then, let us send chosen men, to go
 166  with all speed to the hut of Peleus' son Achilles —
 167  or come: those my eye falls on, let them consent.
 168  Phoenix first, dear to Zeus — let him lead;[14]
 169  and then great Ajax, and brilliant Odysseus;
 170  and of the heralds, Odius and Eurybates go with them.
 171  Now bring water for our hands, and call the holy silence,
 172  that we may pray to Zeus, Cronos' son, for his pity."
 173  So he spoke, and the word he spoke was pleasing to them all.
 174  At once the heralds poured water over their hands,
 175  the young men filled the mixing-bowls brim-high with drink
 176  and served all, pouring first drops in each cup for the god;
 177  and when they had poured, and drunk what their hearts would,
 178  they set out from the hut of Atreus' son Agamemnon.
 179  And the Gerenian horseman Nestor charged them well,
 180  with a look at each in turn, at Odysseus above all:
 181  try hard to win over the blameless son of Peleus.
 182  So the two of them went along the shore of the loud-roaring sea,[15]
 183  praying hard to the holder and shaker of the earth
 184  that they might easily bend the great mind of Aeacus' grandson;
 185  and the two came to the huts and the ships of the Myrmidons.
 186  They found him delighting his heart with the clear-toned lyre,
 187  beautiful, richly worked, with a crossbar of silver on it,
 188  which he had taken from the spoils when he wrecked Eëtion's city:
 189  with this he was pleasuring his heart, and singing the fames of men.[16]
 190  And Patroclus alone sat facing him, in silence,
 191  waiting for Aeacus' grandson to leave off singing.
 192  So the two of them came forward, brilliant Odysseus leading,
 193  and stood before him; and Achilles sprang up amazed,
 194  lyre still in hand, leaving the seat where he had sat;
 195  and Patroclus likewise rose when he saw the men.
 196  And greeting the two of them, swift-footed Achilles said:
 197  "Welcome! Truly dear men have come — the need must be great —
 198  you who even in my anger are dearest to me of the Achaeans."[17]
 199  So speaking, brilliant Achilles led them forward
 200  and seated them on chairs with coverings of purple,
 201  and quickly said to Patroclus, who stood near:
 202  "Set out a bigger mixing-bowl, son of Menoetius;
 203  mix the wine stronger; and make ready a cup for each man:[18]
 204  for the men beneath my roof are the men I love best."
 205  So he spoke, and Patroclus obeyed his dear companion.
 206  He threw down a great chopping-block in the light of the fire
 207  and laid on it the chine of a sheep and of a fat goat,
 208  and the back of a great hog, rich with lard.
 209  Automedon held them for him, and brilliant Achilles carved,
 210  and sliced the meat with skill, and threaded it on spits;
 211  and the son of Menoetius, a man like a god, built up the fire.
 212  And when the fire had burned down and the flame died,
 213  he leveled the embers and stretched the spits above them,
 214  and sprinkled the holy salt, resting the spits on their stones.
 215  And when he had roasted it all and heaped it on platters,
 216  Patroclus took bread and set it out on the table
 217  in beautiful baskets, and Achilles served the meat.
 218  He himself sat down facing godlike Odysseus
 219  against the far wall, and told his companion Patroclus
 220  to sacrifice to the gods; and he threw the offerings in the fire.
 221  And they put out their hands to the good things lying ready.
 222  And when they had put away desire for eating and drinking,
 223  Ajax nodded to Phoenix. But brilliant Odysseus caught it,
 224  and filling a cup with wine he pledged Achilles:
 225  "Your health, Achilles! Of the fair-shared feast we have no lack,
 226  whether in the hut of Agamemnon, Atreus' son,
 227  or here and now: plenty to fill the heart
 228  is set before us. But the works of the lovely feast are not
 229  our business now. We are looking at grief too great, Zeus-nurtured;
 230  we are afraid. It stands in doubt whether we save
 231  the well-benched ships or lose them — unless you clothe yourself in valor.
 232  For close to the ships and the wall they have made their camp,
 233  the high-hearted Trojans and their far-famed allies,
 234  with fires past counting through their host; and they say
 235  nothing will hold them now from falling on the black ships.
 236  And Zeus, Cronos' son, shows them signs on the right,
 237  flashing his lightning; and Hector, glorying in his strength,
 238  rages terribly, trusting in Zeus, and gives way to nothing,
 239  neither man nor god: the strong madness has entered him.
 240  He prays for the brilliant Dawn to show herself quickly,
 241  for he vows he will hew off the ships' stern-horns,
 242  burn the hulls themselves with ravening fire, and cut down
 243  the Achaeans beside them, dazed in the smoke.
 244  This is the fear my heart dreads terribly — that the gods
 245  may make his threats good, and it prove our portion
 246  to die at Troy, far from horse-pasturing Argos.
 247  Up, then! — if you mean, even at this late hour, to save
 248  the wearied sons of the Achaeans from the Trojan onslaught.
 249  It will be your own grief later; and there is no finding
 250  a cure for harm once done. Far sooner, then:
 251  think how to ward the evil day from the Danaans.
 252  Dear friend — surely your father Peleus charged you[19]
 253  the day he sent you out of Phthia to Agamemnon:
 254  'My child, the victory Athena and Hera
 255  will give, if they choose; but the great-hearted spirit —
 256  rein that in in your own chest. Friendliness is better.
 257  Break off strife, the deviser of evil, so the Argives
 258  may honor you the more, the young men and the old.'
 259  So the old man charged you; and you forget. Yet even now
 260  stop; let go the anger that grieves the heart. Agamemnon
 261  offers you worthy gifts, if you leave off your anger.
 262  Come, listen to me, and I will count out for you
 263  all the gifts Agamemnon promised in his huts:[20]
 264  seven tripods untouched by fire, ten talents of gold,
 265  twenty gleaming cauldrons, and twelve horses,
 266  strong-built, prize-winners, who have taken races with their speed.
 267  No man who owned all they have won
 268  would be poor in goods, or lacking in precious gold —
 269  all the prizes Agamemnon's horses have carried home.
 270  And he will give seven women skilled in blameless work,
 271  women of Lesbos — the ones he chose when you yourself
 272  took well-built Lesbos, who in beauty outdid the tribes of women.
 273  These he will give; and among them will be the one he took away:
 274  the daughter of Briseus. And he will swear the great oath besides:
 275  he never went up into her bed, he never lay with her,
 276  as is the way, lord, of men and women.
 277  All this shall be yours at once. And if hereafter
 278  the gods grant us to sack the great city of Priam,
 279  you may heap your ship with gold and bronze
 280  when you come in, when we Achaeans divide the spoil,
 281  and choose out twenty Trojan women for yourself,
 282  the loveliest there are after Argive Helen.
 283  And if we come home to Achaean Argos, udder of the plowland,
 284  you can be his son-in-law: he will honor you the equal of Orestes,
 285  his late-born boy, reared in high plenty.
 286  He has three daughters in his strong-built hall,
 287  Chrysothemis, Laodice, and Iphianassa:
 288  of these you may lead the one you choose, without bride-price,
 289  to the house of Peleus; and he will add wedding-gifts
 290  in plenty — more than any man yet gave with his daughter.
 291  And he will give you seven well-peopled citadels:
 292  Cardamyle, and Enope, and grassy Hire,
 293  sacred Pherae, and Antheia of the deep meadows,
 294  beautiful Aipeia, and Pedasus of the vines —
 295  all near the sea, on the edge of sandy Pylos,
 296  and in them live men rich in flocks and rich in cattle,
 297  who will honor you like a god with their gifts
 298  and render you, under your scepter, their shining dues.
 299  All this he will accomplish for you, if you leave off your anger.
 300  But if Atreus' son is grown too hateful to your heart,
 301  he and his gifts together — then pity all the rest,
 302  the Achaeans of every rank, worn down through the camp, who will honor you
 303  like a god. Truly, you would win great glory in their eyes:
 304  for now you might take Hector's very self — he would come
 305  in close to you in his deadly madness, since he says no man
 306  is his equal of all the Danaans the ships brought here."
 307  Answering him spoke swift-footed Achilles:
 308  "Zeus-sprung son of Laertes, Odysseus of many devices:
 309  I must speak my word to you outright, without mercy —
 310  exactly as I think it, and as it will be accomplished —
 311  so you will not sit by me and coo at me, one after another.
 312  For hateful to me as the gates of Hades is the man[21]
 313  who hides one thing in his mind and says another.
 314  So I will speak it as it seems to me best.
 315  Neither Atreus' son Agamemnon will persuade me, I think,
 316  nor the other Danaans — since there was no thanks, it seems,
 317  for battling the enemy without respite, forever.
 318  The portion is equal for the man who hangs back and the man who fights hard;[22]
 319  in one honor stand the coward and the brave;
 320  death comes alike to the do-nothing and the man of many deeds.
 321  And nothing is laid up for me because I suffered
 322  the pains of it, forever staking my life in the fighting.
 323  As a bird carries to her unfledged chicks[23]
 324  every morsel she takes, and it goes hard with herself,
 325  so I have lain awake through many an unsleeping night
 326  and fought through bloody days of battle,
 327  contending with men for the sake of their women.
 328  Twelve cities of men I have sacked from my ships,[24]
 329  and eleven by land, I tell you, across deep-soiled Troy:
 330  from all of them I took out treasure, much and good,
 331  and brought it all and gave it to Agamemnon,
 332  Atreus' son — who waited behind by the swift ships,
 333  took it in, doled out a little, and kept the most.
 334  What he gave the chief men and the kings as prizes
 335  stands untouched with them; from me alone of the Achaeans
 336  he took — and he keeps my bed-dear wife. Let him lie beside her[25]
 337  and take his pleasure! But why must the Argives make war
 338  on the Trojans? Why did Atreus' son gather an army
 339  and lead it here? Was it not for lovely-haired Helen?
 340  Are the sons of Atreus alone among mortal men
 341  the ones who love their wives? — since any man who is good
 342  and sensible loves his own and cares for her,
 343  as I loved that one from my heart, spear-won though she was.
 344  Now, since he took my prize out of my hands and cheated me,
 345  let him not try me: I know him too well. He will not persuade me.
 346  No, Odysseus — with you and the other kings
 347  let him think how to beat the enemy fire from the ships.
 348  Truly he has labored greatly with me gone:
 349  he has even built a wall, and driven a ditch along it,
 350  wide and great, and planted stakes within it —
 351  and even so he cannot hold the strength of man-slaughtering
 352  Hector! While I was fighting among the Achaeans,
 353  Hector had no wish to raise battle away from his wall;
 354  he came only as far as the Scaean Gates and the oak.[26]
 355  There once he stood up to me alone — and barely escaped my charge.
 356  But now, since I have no wish to fight brilliant Hector,
 357  tomorrow I will sacrifice to Zeus and all the gods,
 358  load my ships well, and drag them down to the sea —
 359  and you will see, if you care to, if such things interest you,
 360  my ships at earliest light sailing the Hellespont
 361  where the fish swarm, and in them men rowing with a will;
 362  and if the famed earth-shaker grants fair passage,
 363  on the third day I should reach deep-soiled Phthia.
 364  I have great possessions there, left when I made my luckless
 365  journey here; and more from here — gold, red bronze,
 366  fair-belted women, grey iron — I will carry home,
 367  all that fell to me by lot. But my prize — the man who gave it,
 368  the lord Agamemnon, Atreus' son, in his insolence
 369  took it back. Tell him all of it, just as I charge you,
 370  openly — so the other Achaeans too may be angry,
 371  if he hopes to cheat some other Danaan yet,
 372  clothed as he always is in shamelessness. And yet —
 373  dog though he is — he would not dare look me in the face.
 374  I will not join him in counsel, and not in action:
 375  he cheated me outright, he wronged me. Never again
 376  will he trick me with words. Once is enough. Let him go
 377  to ruin at his ease: Zeus the counselor has taken his wits.
 378  His gifts are hateful to me; I count him at a hair's worth.[27]
 379  Not if he gave me ten times as much, and twenty times,
 380  all he has now and more from elsewhere besides;
 381  not all the revenue of Orchomenus, or of Thebes —
 382  Egyptian Thebes, where the houses hold the greatest treasures,
 383  the city of a hundred gates, and through each gate
 384  two hundred men drive out with horses and chariots;
 385  not if he gave me gifts as many as the sand and dust —
 386  not even so would Agamemnon persuade my heart
 387  until he has paid me back all the outrage that galls it.
 388  And the daughter of Agamemnon, Atreus' son — I will not
 389  marry her: not if she rivaled golden Aphrodite in beauty
 390  and matched the works of grey-eyed Athena.
 391  Not even so will I marry her. Let him choose another Achaean,
 392  one who suits him — someone more kingly than I.
 393  For if the gods preserve me, and I come home,
 394  Peleus himself will find me a wife.[28]
 395  There are Achaean girls enough through Hellas and through Phthia,
 396  daughters of chieftains, men who guard their citadels:
 397  of these I will make whichever one I choose my own dear wife.
 398  There — that is where my proud heart kept urging me:
 399  to take a wedded wife, a fitting bride,
 400  and enjoy the possessions old Peleus won.
 401  For not worth my life is all they say was stored[29]
 402  in Ilion, that well-peopled citadel,
 403  before, in peacetime, before the sons of the Achaeans came —
 404  nor all the stone threshold of the Archer holds within it,
 405  Phoebus Apollo's, in rocky Pytho.
 406  Cattle can be lifted, and fat sheep;
 407  tripods can be won, and the tawny heads of horses:
 408  but a man's life cannot be lifted back, cannot be caught,
 409  once it has crossed the fence of his teeth.
 410  For my mother tells me — Thetis, the goddess of the silver feet —[30]
 411  that two fates carry me toward the end of death:
 412  if I stay here and fight around the Trojans' city,
 413  my homecoming is lost — but my fame will be imperishable;
 414  and if I come home to my dear fatherland,
 415  my noble fame is lost — but my life will be long,
 416  and the end of death will not be quick to find me.
 417  And to the rest of you I would give this counsel:
 418  sail home — since you will never now reach the end
 419  of steep Ilion: wide-seeing Zeus has held
 420  his hand over her, and her people have taken heart.
 421  So go; and carry my message to the chief men
 422  of the Achaeans — that is the honor-portion of the old —
 423  so they may devise in their minds some better plan
 424  to save the ships, and the Achaean host
 425  beside the hollow ships: since this one — this they thought of —
 426  will not serve, while my anger stands. But Phoenix —
 427  let him stay and sleep here with us,
 428  so he may follow in my ships to our dear fatherland
 429  tomorrow, if he wishes. I will not take him by force."
 430  So he spoke, and they all went still in silence,
 431  marveling at his word: he had refused with great power.
 432  At long last the old horseman Phoenix spoke,[31]
 433  bursting into tears, in terrible fear for the ships:
 434  "If it is homecoming you now ponder, shining Achilles,
 435  and you will not lift ruinous fire from the swift ships
 436  at all, because anger has fallen on your heart —
 437  how then could I be left here without you, dear child,
 438  alone? It was to you the old horseman Peleus sent me
 439  the day he sent you out of Phthia to Agamemnon,
 440  a child still, knowing nothing yet of leveling war,
 441  nothing of assemblies, where men become preeminent.
 442  For that he sent me: to teach you all these things —
 443  to be a speaker of words, and a doer of deeds.
 444  After all that, dear child, I would not choose to be left
 445  without you — no, not if a god himself should promise
 446  to scrape old age from me and set me young again,
 447  as I was when I first left Hellas of the beautiful women,
 448  fleeing a quarrel with my father, Amyntor, Ormenus' son.[32]
 449  He was enraged with me over his lovely-haired concubine:
 450  he loved her, and dishonored his own wife,
 451  my mother; and my mother begged me endlessly by my knees
 452  to lie with the concubine first, so she would loathe the old man.
 453  I obeyed her, and did it. And my father, guessing at once,
 454  cursed me long and hard, and called up the hateful Erinyes:
 455  that never should a dear son born of me
 456  be set upon his knees. And the gods fulfilled his curses —
 457  Zeus of the world below, and dread Persephone.
      [Lines 458-461 are absent from the printed text; see note 32.]
 462  Then the heart in my chest could no longer be held at all
 463  to linger in the halls of my angry father.
 464  Kinsmen and cousins in plenty, gathered around me,
 465  held me there in the halls with their begging:
 466  many a fat sheep and rolling-gaited crook-horned ox
 467  they slaughtered; many a hog rich in lard
 468  was stretched to singe across the flame of Hephaestus,
 469  and much of the old man's wine was drunk from the jars.
 470  Nine nights together they kept their watch around me;
 471  they took the guard in turns, and the fires never died —
 472  one under the colonnade of the strong-fenced court,
 473  one in the porch, before my bedchamber doors.
 474  But when the tenth dark night came on me,
 475  then I burst the close-fitted doors of my chamber
 476  and got out, and vaulted the courtyard fence
 477  lightly, unseen by the watchmen and the slave-women.
 478  Then I fled far away through Hellas of the wide spaces
 479  and came to deep-soiled Phthia, mother of flocks,
 480  to lord Peleus. And he received me with a ready heart,
 481  and loved me as a father loves his son,
 482  his only son, late-born, heir to great possessions;
 483  and he made me rich, and gave me a numerous people:
 484  I lived at the edge of Phthia, ruling the Dolopes.
 485  And I made you what you are, Achilles like the gods,[33]
 486  loving you from my heart: you would not go with another
 487  to the feast, or eat in the halls,
 488  until I had sat you on my knees and cut
 489  the first bit of meat for you, and held the wine to your lips.
 490  Many a time you soaked the tunic on my chest,
 491  spitting up wine in your troublesome babyhood.
 492  So I suffered much for you, and toiled much,
 493  minding always that the gods would bring no child of mine
 494  to birth; no — I made you my child, godlike Achilles,
 495  so that someday you might ward unseemly ruin from me.
 496  Achilles — break your great spirit. It does not become you
 497  to keep a pitiless heart. Even the gods themselves can bend,
 498  and theirs is the greater excellence, honor, and strength:
 499  with burnt offerings and gentle vows,
 500  with libation and savor, men turn even them
 501  by their prayers, when a man has transgressed and gone wrong.
 502  For Prayers, too, are daughters of great Zeus —[34]
 503  lame, and wrinkled, and eyes turned aside,
 504  who make it their care to follow after Blindness.
 505  But Blindness is strong and sound of foot, and far outruns them
 506  all, and gets in first over all the earth
 507  tripping mankind; and they come healing after.
 508  Now the man who respects the daughters of Zeus when they approach,
 509  him they greatly bless, and hear him when he prays;
 510  but when a man denies them and stiffly refuses,
 511  they go to Zeus, Cronos' son, and pray
 512  that Blindness go with that man, till he trips, and pays in full.
 513  So you too, Achilles — grant the daughters of Zeus
 514  their due of honor, which bends the will of others, and good men too.
 515  If Atreus' son were not bringing gifts, and naming more
 516  to come — if he raged on furiously still —
 517  I would not be the one to tell you: throw your wrath away
 518  and defend the Argives, however much they need it.
 519  But now he gives much at once, and has promised more hereafter,
 520  and has sent out the best men to entreat you,
 521  chosen through the Achaean host, the men who are dearest
 522  to you yourself of the Argives. Do not shame their words
 523  or their coming; till now, no one could blame your anger.
 524  So it was with the men before us too — the fames we hear of,
 525  heroes of old, when furious anger came on one of them:
 526  they could be won by gifts, and won over by words.
 527  I remember this deed of old — no new thing —
 528  how it was; and I will tell it among you, friends, all of you.
 529  The Curetes were fighting, and the battle-biding Aetolians,[35]
 530  around the city of Calydon, and killing one another —
 531  the Aetolians in defense of lovely Calydon,
 532  the Curetes raging to sack it in war.
 533  For golden-throned Artemis had raised that evil among them,
 534  in anger that Oeneus brought no harvest-offering
 535  from his orchard slope: the other gods feasted on hecatombs,
 536  and to her alone, great Zeus's daughter, he offered nothing —
 537  he forgot, or he did not think of it: a great blindness of heart.
 538  Enraged, the child of Zeus who showers arrows
 539  roused against him a wild boar, white-tusked ravager,
 540  who did great damage, haunting Oeneus' orchard land,
 541  tearing up tall trees and hurling them to the ground,
 542  roots and all, apple-blossom and all.
 543  Him the son of Oeneus killed — Meleager —
 544  when he had gathered huntsmen out of many cities,
 545  and hounds: for the boar was not brought down by few men,
 546  so huge he was; and he set many on the pyre of grief.
 547  But around the carcass the goddess raised a great clamor and battle-cry,
 548  over the boar's head and the bristling hide,
 549  between the Curetes and the great-hearted Aetolians.
 550  Now as long as Meleager dear to Ares fought,
 551  so long it went hard with the Curetes; they could not
 552  hold their ground outside the wall, though they were many.
 553  But when anger entered Meleager — anger that swells
 554  in the chests of others too, even the close-thinking —
 555  enraged at his own mother Althaea, he lay idle
 556  beside his wedded wife, beautiful Cleopatra,
 557  daughter of Marpessa of the beautiful ankles, Euenus' child,
 558  and of Idas, strongest of men on the earth
 559  in his time — who even lifted his bow against the lord
 560  Phoebus Apollo, for the sake of his lovely-ankled bride.
 561  Her, in those days, in their halls her father and lady mother
 562  called by the name Alcyone — halcyon — because
 563  her own mother, with the mourning halcyon's fate upon her,
 564  wept when Phoebus Apollo the worker-from-afar snatched her away.
 565  By her Meleager lay, digesting his heart-paining anger,
 566  enraged at his mother's curses: for she prayed the gods
 567  against him, in her grief for a brother's killing —
 568  over and over she beat the earth that feeds so many with her hands,
 569  calling on Hades and dread Persephone,
 570  crouched on her knees, her bosom wet with tears,
 571  to give her son death; and out of Erebus an Erinys
 572  who walks in mist heard her, with her unappeasable heart.
 573  Soon the din of the enemy rose around the gates, the thud
 574  of towers battered; and the Aetolian elders begged him,
 575  sending the gods' chosen priests:
 576  come out and defend us — and they promised a great gift:
 577  where the plain of lovely Calydon was fattest,
 578  there they bade him choose a beautiful precinct,
 579  fifty fields broad, half of it vineyard,
 580  half open plowland, cut from the plain.
 581  And the old horseman Oeneus begged him over and over,
 582  standing at the threshold of his high-roofed chamber,
 583  shaking the bolted doors, entreating his son;
 584  over and over his sisters and his lady mother
 585  begged him — he refused the more — and his companions,
 586  the truest and dearest of them all:
 587  but not even so could they move the heart in his chest —
 588  until the chamber itself was under thick bombardment, and the Curetes
 589  were scaling the towers and firing the great city.
 590  Then at last his fair-belted wife begged Meleager,
 591  wailing, and counted out for him all
 592  the griefs that come to people whose city is taken:
 593  the men killed, the town burned down to ash,
 594  the children led away by strangers, and the deep-belted women.
 595  And his heart was stirred as he heard those terrible things:
 596  he went out, and clothed his body in shining armor.
 597  So he beat the evil day back from the Aetolians,
 598  yielding to his own heart. And they no longer made good
 599  the gifts, the many gracious gifts; yet he warded off the evil all the same.
 600  But you — do not think that way in your heart; do not let a god
 601  turn you that way, dear boy. It would be worse
 602  to defend the ships already burning. While there are gifts,
 603  come: the Achaeans will honor you like a god.
 604  But if without gifts you enter man-destroying war,
 605  you will not be honored the same, though you beat the war away."
 606  Answering him spoke swift-footed Achilles:
 607  "Phoenix, old father, aged one, Zeus-nurtured: I have no need[36]
 608  of that honor. I judge that I am honored by the portion of Zeus,
 609  which will keep me here by the beaked ships as long as breath
 610  stays in my chest and my knees have their spring.
 611  And I will tell you another thing — put it away in your heart:
 612  do not blur my heart with weeping and grieving
 613  to gratify the hero son of Atreus. You must not
 614  love him, or my love for you may turn to hatred.
 615  It is right for you to wound the man who wounds me.
 616  Be king equally with me; take half my honor.
 617  These men will carry the message; you stay here and sleep
 618  in a soft bed; and with the showing of Dawn
 619  we will decide whether to go to our own country, or to stay."
 620  He spoke, and with his brows signed silently to Patroclus
 621  to spread a thick bed for Phoenix, so the others
 622  might think the sooner of leaving the hut. And Ajax,
 623  Telamon's godlike son, spoke his word among them:
 624  "Zeus-sprung son of Laertes, Odysseus of many devices —[37]
 625  let us go. I do not think the end of this errand
 626  will be reached by this road. We must report the word,
 627  unwelcome as it is, to the Danaans with all speed —
 628  they sit waiting for it now. But Achilles
 629  has made the great-hearted spirit in his chest a savage thing —
 630  the hard man! — and cares nothing for the love of his companions,
 631  the love we honored him with by the ships beyond all others.
 632  Pitiless! And yet a man accepts blood-price
 633  even from his brother's killer, even for his own dead son;
 634  and the killer stays in his own country, having paid the great price,
 635  and the kinsman's heart and proud spirit are held in check
 636  once he has taken the price. But you — the gods have set
 637  in your chest an anger unending, and evil, over a girl —
 638  one girl! — while we now offer you seven, far the best,
 639  and much else besides. Put a gracious spirit in you;
 640  respect your own roof: we are under it,
 641  out of the whole Danaan host; and beyond all others we have longed
 642  to be nearest to you and dearest of all the Achaeans."
 643  Answering him spoke swift-footed Achilles:
 644  "Ajax, Zeus-sprung son of Telamon, marshal of the people:
 645  everything you have said is very nearly after my own heart.
 646  But my heart swells with rage whenever I remember
 647  those things — how Atreus' son treated me like dirt among the Argives,[38]
 648  as if I were some vagrant without honor.
 649  But you two go, and carry back my message:
 650  I will not think of bloody war again
 651  until brilliant Hector, war-minded Priam's son,
 652  comes killing Argives right up to the huts and ships
 653  of the Myrmidons, and blackens the ships with fire.
 654  At my hut, though — at my black ship — I think
 655  Hector will be stopped, for all his hunger for battle."
 656  So he spoke; and each of them took a two-handled cup,
 657  poured the libation, and went back along the ships, Odysseus leading.
 658  And Patroclus told his companions and the slave-women
 659  to spread a thick bed for Phoenix with all speed;
 660  and they obeyed, and spread the bed as he commanded —
 661  fleeces, a rug, and a fine flare of linen.
 662  There the old man lay down, and waited for brilliant Dawn.
 663  And Achilles slept in the innermost corner of the strong-built hut,
 664  and beside him lay a woman he had brought from Lesbos:[39]
 665  Phorbas's daughter, fair-cheeked Diomede.
 666  And Patroclus lay down on the other side, and by him too
 667  lay fair-belted Iphis, whom brilliant Achilles gave him
 668  when he took steep Scyros, Enyeus' citadel.
 669  Now when the envoys were back in the huts of Atreus' son,
 670  the sons of the Achaeans, springing up on every side,
 671  pledged them with golden cups, and pressed their questions;
 672  and the lord of men Agamemnon asked first:
 673  "Come, tell me, Odysseus of the many tales, great glory of the Achaeans:
 674  is he willing to beat the enemy fire from the ships,
 675  or did he refuse — does anger still hold that great-hearted spirit?"
 676  Then answered him much-enduring brilliant Odysseus:
 677  "Most glorious son of Atreus, lord of men Agamemnon:
 678  that man has no will to quench his anger — he is filled
 679  fuller yet with fury; he refuses you, and your gifts.
 680  He bids you take counsel yourself, among the Argives,
 681  how you may save the ships and the Achaean host;
 682  and he himself threatens, with the showing of Dawn,[40]
 683  to drag his well-benched, well-curved ships to the sea.
 684  And he said he would counsel the others too
 685  to sail for home — since you will never now reach the end
 686  of steep Ilion: wide-seeing Zeus has held
 687  his hand over her, and her people have taken heart.
 688  So he spoke. And here are these to confirm it, who went with me:
 689  Ajax, and the two heralds, wise men both.
 690  But old Phoenix has laid himself down there — as Achilles bade —
 691  so he may follow in the ships to his dear fatherland
 692  tomorrow, if he wishes; he will not take him by force."
 693  So he spoke, and they all went still in silence,
 694  marveling at his word: he had spoken with great power.
 695  A long while the sons of the Achaeans sat mute, dejected;
 696  and at long last Diomedes, good at the war-cry, spoke:
 697  "Most glorious son of Atreus, lord of men Agamemnon:[41]
 698  would you had never begged the blameless son of Peleus,
 699  offering gifts past counting. He is proud at the best of times,
 700  and now you have driven him deeper into pride by far.
 701  No — let us leave him be: whether he goes
 702  or stays; he will fight again whenever
 703  the heart in his chest commands him, and the god rouses him.
 704  Come then — as I say it, let us all obey:
 705  sleep now, when you have taken your hearts' content
 706  of bread and wine — for in them are fury and valor;
 707  and when the beautiful rosy-fingered Dawn appears,
 708  marshal the men and the horses quickly before the ships,
 709  urging them on — and fight yourself among the first."
 710  So he spoke, and all the kings approved,
 711  marveling at the word of Diomedes, breaker of horses.
 712  Then they poured libation, and went each man to his hut,
 713  and there they lay down, and took the gift of sleep.

Notes

[1] (9.1-8) Panic (Φύζα) "companion of icy Rout" — the war-demons of
    4.440 gain another member; and the two-winds simile makes the army's
    heart a sea worked by opposed gales: the book's whole subject —
    a mind torn two ways — announced in weather.

[2] (9.14-15) Agamemnon weeps "like a spring of dark water" pouring down
    a cliff — the exact simile that will describe Patroclus weeping for
    the dying Achaeans at 16.3-4. The poem opens its two great
    turning-points, the embassy and the Patrocleia, with the same tears.
    (Echo note; English will repeat verbatim there.)

[3] (9.17-28) Agamemnon repeats his own test-speech of 2.110-118 and
    139-141 word for word (the English repeats the Book 2 rendering) —
    but this time he means it. The counterfeit despair of Book 2 has
    become real, and no Odysseus rises to stop the flight: only Diomedes.

[4] (9.32-49) Diomedes' answer — the payoff planted at 4.401-402, where
    he took Agamemnon's insult in silence "where it is right" now claims
    the assembly's license: "it is you I will fight first — your folly."
    The scepter/courage antithesis (37-39) dismantles Agamemnon's
    authority with surgical courtesy. Aristarchus athetized 44.

[5] (9.63-64) "Clanless, lawless, hearthless is the man who is in love
    with war among his own people" — Nestor's anathema on civil strife,
    the most quoted political sentence in the poem after 2.204; the
    triple alpha-privative (ἀφρήτωρ ἀθέμιστος ἀνέστιος) strips the
    stasis-lover of every circle of belonging.

[6] (9.70-72) Wine-ships from Thrace "daily" — with 7.467-475 (Lemnos),
    the poem's second glimpse of the supply-economy behind the war.

[7] (9.97-99) "Zeus has put into your hands the scepter and the
    ordinances" — Odysseus's crowd-control theology of 2.205-206, now
    recited to the king as obligation rather than of him as privilege:
    with the scepter comes the duty to listen.

[8] (9.115-120) "Blinded I was — I do not deny it" (ἀασάμην): Agamemnon
    at last speaks the ἄτη-word of 1.412 and 2.111 in the first person.
    A line quoted by Athenaeus adds after 119: "or maddened with wine,
    or the gods themselves harmed me" (apparatus, Line 119) — an ancient
    plus-verse softening the confession that the vulgate leaves bare.
    His fullest self-exculpation waits for 19.86-138.

[9] (9.121-157) The catalogue of gifts: seven tripods, ten talents,
    twenty cauldrons, twelve racehorses, seven women of Lesbos, Briseis
    with an oath, and on the far side of victory a ship's hold of
    treasure, twenty Trojan women, a royal marriage "without bride-price"
    (146 — the suitor's payment waived and reversed: Agamemnon dowers
    the groom), and seven cities. It is a king's ransom — and every item
    denominates honor in exactly the currency Achilles is about to
    declare bankrupt.

[10] (9.132-134) The oath that he never touched Briseis — offered
     unprompted, and revealing what the quarrel's language of "prizes"
     had buried: that this is also about a woman's bed, and that
     Agamemnon knows it. He will swear it publicly at 19.258-265.

[11] (9.144-145) Chrysothemis, Laodice, Iphianassa: no Electra, and an
     Iphianassa alive and marriageable in the war's tenth year — the
     Iliad appears not to know (or pointedly ignores) the sacrifice of
     Iphigenia at Aulis. The tragedians' house of Atreus is not yet
     this poem's.

[12] (9.149-156) The seven cities stand on the Messenian coast, "on the
     edge of sandy Pylos" — inside Nestor's sphere, not Agamemnon's:
     ancient critics already asked by what right he gives them away.
     Royal gift-geography is aspirational; the point is the scale.

[13] (9.158-161) The poison pill: "Let him be broken (δμηθήτω)...
     and let him yield place to me, by as much as I am more kingly."
     The gift-list ends by re-enacting the original offense — the gifts
     are to purchase not reconciliation but submission. Odysseus will
     cut these lines in the retelling (note [20]).

[14] (9.168-170) The envoys: Phoenix — Achilles' own tutor, somehow
     present in Agamemnon's council — to lead; then Ajax and Odysseus,
     with two heralds (this Odius is not the Halizone captain of 2.856).
     The oddity of Phoenix's presence and the dual verbs that follow
     (note [15]) have fed two centuries of analysis about whether his
     role was a later expansion. The poem's own logic is plain: it
     sends Achilles' three loves — his teacher, his bluntest peer, and
     the army's best voice.

[15] (9.182-198) The famous duals (disputed_lines.md #5): "the two of
     them went... the two came... greeting the two of them" — the Greek
     uses strict dual verbs for a party of three envoys plus two
     heralds. Ancient solutions (Aristarchus: the duals cover only the
     two principals; others: Phoenix went ahead) and modern ones (an
     older two-man embassy, with Phoenix stitched in later; or the
     heralds as the pair) all leave a seam showing. The translation
     renders every dual visibly as "the two," per project policy, so
     the crux is present in English rather than smoothed away.

[16] (9.186-191) Achilles singing "the fames of men" (κλέα ἀνδρῶν) to a
     lyre taken at the sack of Eëtion's Thebe — Andromache's city
     (6.414-428). The poem's only scene of epic performed shows its
     greatest hero as the singer, and his instrument as spoil of the
     very raid whose griefs thread the poem; while Patroclus waits,
     silent, for his turn in the song. What Achilles sings, he is
     choosing between at 410-416.

[17] (9.197-198, 202-220) "You who even in my anger are dearest to me"
     — the greeting concedes at once what the answers will struggle to
     unsay. The hosting is done by the heroes' own hands (Patroclus
     mixing "stronger" wine, Achilles carving) — the full feast
     type-scene performed personally: the quarrel is with one man, not
     with fellowship.

[18] — merged into [17].

[19] (9.252-259) Odysseus quotes Peleus's parting advice verbatim —
     "rein in the great-hearted spirit... break off strife" — the
     absent-father motif (cf. 7.124-128) turned into an instrument of
     persuasion. Whether Odysseus actually heard it (he was there,
     11.765-790 implies) or reconstructs what fathers say, it is the
     speech's shrewdest stroke — and its failure measures how far
     Achilles now stands from Phthia's ethics.

[20] (9.264-299) The relay: Odysseus repeats the catalogue word for
     word (the English repeats it too, person-shifted, per policy) —
     and stops at 299. The poison lines (158-161, "let him yield to
     me...") are silently omitted, and in their place he pivots to
     pity for the army and the bait of Hector's nearness (300-306).
     The poem's masterclass in diplomatic editing: fidelity as
     technique, suppression as tact. Achilles' "hateful as the gates
     of Hades" answer suggests he smells the redaction anyway.

[21] (9.308-314) "Hateful to me as the gates of Hades is the man who
     hides one thing in his mind and says another" (disputed_lines.md
     #6): the referent is contested — a warning aimed at Odysseus the
     professional speaker standing before him, or at the absent
     Agamemnon, the actual hider-and-sayer of the quarrel? The
     translation keeps the generalizing third person that lets both
     targets stand. Plato quotes the passage (Hippias Minor 365a) in
     just this connection — Achilles the plain speaker against
     Odysseus the polytropic.

[22] (9.318-321) "The portion is equal for the man who hangs back and
     the man who fights hard; in one honor stand the coward and the
     brave; death comes alike..." — the sentence that unmakes the
     poem's social contract. If timē no longer tracks merit, the whole
     exchange-economy of prizes, and the war built on it, loses its
     reason. This is the radical center of the book: not a bigger
     price demanded, but the currency refused.

[23] (9.323-327) Achilles as mother-bird, carrying every morsel to
     unfledged chicks "and it goes hard with herself" — the fighter's
     self-image as feeder and nurturer, gender-crossed; the tenderest
     simile any warrior applies to himself in the poem.

[24] (9.328-336) Twelve cities by sea, eleven by land: the sack-list
     that stocked the poem's backstory (Thebe, Lyrnessus, Lesbos,
     Scyros...) — all carried to Agamemnon, "who waited behind by the
     swift ships." The distribution-economy described from the point
     of view of its producer.

[25] (9.336-343) "He keeps my bed-dear wife (ἄλοχον θυμαρέα)... as I
     loved that one from my heart, spear-won though she was." Achilles
     calls Briseis by the word for wedded wife, and answers the whole
     war's premise with a question — "are the sons of Atreus alone
     among mortal men the ones who love their wives?" The captive
     "prize" of Book 1 is re-described as a loved woman; Briseis's own
     voice, confirming, comes at 19.287-300.

[26] (9.354-355) "only as far as the Scaean Gates and the oak" — the
     landmark of 7.22 and 6.237; Achilles' memory of a Hector who
     would not fight in the open is the exact inverse of the Hector
     of Book 8, and both are true in their seasons of divine favor.

[27] (9.378-387) "I count him at a hair's worth" (ἐν καρὸς αἴσῃ) — and
     the great refusal-crescendo: ten times, twenty times the gifts;
     the wealth of Orchomenus; "Egyptian Thebes, the city of a hundred
     gates" (the Iliad's only mention of Egypt); gifts like sand and
     dust. Not until "he has paid me back all the outrage" — a price
     no catalogue can denominate.

[28] (9.388-400) The marriage refused: "Peleus himself will find me a
     wife." Against Agamemnon's dynastic offer Achilles sets an
     ordinary happiness — a local bride, his father's goods — spoken
     by the one man in the poem who knows (410-416) he will never
     have it.

[29] (9.401-409) "Not worth my life (ψυχῆς ἀντάξιον)": cattle can be
     raided, tripods won, "but a man's life cannot be lifted back...
     once it has crossed the fence of his teeth." The formula for
     guarded speech (4.350) turned into the body's last gate — words
     return, breath does not. This is the poem's deepest sentence
     against its own heroic premise, and the Odyssey's Achilles in
     the underworld will say it again in other words.

[30] (9.410-416) The two fates: stay and win "imperishable fame"
     (κλέος ἄφθιτον — the oldest phrase of Indo-European praise-poetry,
     cognate formulas in the Vedas), or go home to long, fameless
     life. Only Achilles receives destiny as a menu; and the poem that
     preserves his fame is itself the content of the first option —
     the choice is between existing in epic and existing. Zenodotus
     omitted and Aristarchus athetized 416.

[31] (9.432-445) Phoenix: guardian of Achilles' boyhood, teacher of
     the heroic double curriculum — "a speaker of words, and a doer
     of deeds" (443) — the poem's one-line definition of the complete
     man, spoken by the man who taught it to its greatest exemplar.

[32] (9.447-461) Phoenix's own story: his father's concubine, his
     mother's plea, the old man's curse of childlessness sworn to the
     Erinyes and ratified by "Zeus of the world below" (Hades) "and
     dread Persephone." At this point the vulgate — and this project's
     Greek text — omits four lines (458-461), and the numbering jumps:
     preserved only by Plutarch (How to Study Poetry 26f), they read,
     in translation: "Then I planned in my heart to cut him down with
     the sharp bronze; but some immortal checked my anger, putting in
     my heart the people's talk and the many reproaches of men, that I
     might not be called a father-killer among the Achaeans."
     Aristarchus athetized them for their shocking content
     (disputed_lines.md #7), and the printed tradition dropped them so
     thoroughly that editors since Wolf (1785) have had to reinsert
     them from Plutarch (apparatus, Line 457). The translation
     reproduces the transmitted gap; the suppressed confession —
     Phoenix nearly a parricide, saved by fear of what men would say —
     is the darkest mirror the poem holds up to anger mastered.

[33] (9.485-495) Phoenix nursing the baby Achilles — cutting his meat,
     holding the wine, the tunic soaked "in your troublesome
     babyhood": the war's most terrifying fighter recalled as a
     spitting infant, by the childless man who made him his child.
     The plea's authority is this memory, and its failure is measured
     by it.

[34] (9.502-514) The Litai — the Prayers: daughters of Zeus, "lame,
     and wrinkled, and eyes turned aside," limping forever after
     swift, strong Blindness (Ἄτη) to heal what she wrecks; scorn
     them, and they ask Zeus to send Atē against the scorner. The
     poem's only sustained allegory, and its moral crux: within the
     fiction, Achilles' refusal of these suppliants files the request
     that his own ruin follow — as it does, through Patroclus. The
     ἄτη-thread (1.412, 2.111, 9.115, 19.91-136 where Agamemnon tells
     Atē's own myth) runs straight through this passage.

[35] (9.524-599) The Meleager paradigm — the poem in miniature, told
     to its own hero: Artemis slighted (the same forgotten-sacrifice
     motif, with the ἄτη-verb at 537), the boar, the wrath, the hero
     idle beside his wife while his city burns; the ladder of
     suppliants — priests and elders with a gift of land, father at
     the threshold, sisters, mother, dearest companions — all
     refused; then the wife, Cleopatra, with her catalogue of a
     sacked city's griefs; he yields, fights, saves them — "and they
     no longer made good the gifts." Every rung maps onto Achilles'
     own night (elders-with-gifts = Odysseus; father-figure =
     Phoenix; companions = Ajax), and the poem's audience can finish
     the map: the beloved whose plea will finally move Achilles is
     the one sitting silent in this very hut — and Meleager's wife
     bears the name Cleopatra, "famed of her fathers," Patroclus's
     name with its elements reversed. The onomastic mirror was
     noticed in antiquity and remains the most elegant argument in
     Homer. (The Marpessa-Idas digression, 557-564 — a mortal who
     drew his bow on Apollo for love, and a wife nicknamed Halcyon
     for her mother's mourning — folds yet another tale of defiance
     and grief inside the paradigm.) Phoenix's warning lands askew:
     Meleager's lesson as told is "yield before it is too late for
     gifts," but its buried lesson is "the wife's plea — the
     Cleopatra — comes last, and works." Book 16 is that plea.

[36] (9.606-619) To Phoenix, the first softening: "I am honored by
     the portion of Zeus" (the replacement for the army's failed
     timē); "be king equally with me; take half my honor"; and the
     schedule slips — from "tomorrow I sail" (357-363) to "at dawn we
     will decide whether to go or stay." ἄττα, "old father" — the
     nursery word — enters the register in the same breath as the
     threat to hate whomever Phoenix loves.

[37] (9.624-642) Ajax, to Odysseus, about Achilles, in front of him —
     the bluntest speech in the poem: a man takes blood-price
     (ποινή) even for a murdered brother or son, and the killer walks
     his own country; Achilles is implacable "over a girl — one
     girl! — while we offer seven." The argument from composition
     for homicide is the strongest yet — it names the going rate for
     the unpayable — and its miss is the most revealing: the wrong
     is no longer priced in girls at all. Then the master-stroke of
     the plain man: "respect your own roof: we are under it" — the
     xenia-claim, which is the one Achilles grants.

[38] (9.643-655) The third answer, to Ajax: "everything you have said
     is very nearly after my own heart" — and the line that started
     it all restated raw: he treated me "as if I were some vagrant
     without honor (ἀτίμητον μετανάστην)." Then the third position:
     not sailing, not deciding at dawn, but — I fight when Hector
     reaches the ships of the Myrmidons. Refusal has become a
     timetable; the plot of Books 11-16 is Achilles' own sentence
     coming true at his hut, where Hector is indeed stopped — by
     Patroclus's death.

[39] (9.658-668) The beds: Phoenix's made by the slave-women; Achilles
     with Diomede of Lesbos, Patroclus with Iphis, taken at Scyros —
     the sack-list of 328-329 embodied in the hut's sleeping
     arrangements; each concubine a city. The embassy departs one man
     lighter: Phoenix stays, the first crack in Achilles' isolation.

[40] (9.673-692) The report: to "Odysseus of the many tales"
     (πολύαινος — new epithet, register), Odysseus repeats Achilles'
     first answer verbatim (682-687 = 417-420, English likewise) —
     and reports nothing of the second or third: not "at dawn we
     decide," not "when Hector reaches my ships." The editor of the
     offer (note [20]) now edits the refusal, presenting the council
     with the starkest version — whether as rigor, or despair, or
     policy is the reader's to judge. Aristarchus athetized 688-692,
     Zenodotus 692.

[41] (9.693-709) Diomedes closes the book he opened: "would you had
     never begged him... he will fight again whenever the heart in
     his chest commands him, and the god rouses him" — the truest
     prophecy in the book, and the completion of the arc from the
     silent young man of 4.401 to the voice the kings applaud into
     the night. Eat, sleep, fight at dawn: the war goes on without
     its best man, which is exactly what Books 10-15 now show.
