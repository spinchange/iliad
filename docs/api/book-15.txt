BOOK 15

Zeus wakes: the plan of the whole poem is spoken aloud, Apollo kicks
down the wall like a child's sand, and Hector's hand closes on the
stern of Protesilaus's ship.

TRANSLATOR'S COMMENTARY

Book 15 is the great correction. Zeus wakes on Gargaron, sees the
rout and Hector vomiting blood, and in three moves restores the
poem's direction: Hera is terrorized with the memory of her
punishment — hung from heaven with anvils at her ankles (note [2]) —
and swears the greatest oath in the poem, by Styx, his sacred head,
and their bridal bed, a lawyer's truth that acquits her of Poseidon
while saying nothing of the seduction (note [3]); Iris carries an
ultimatum to Poseidon, who answers with the three-way division of
the cosmos — sea, murk, and sky by lot, "earth and Olympus common
to all" — and yields only to the argument of the Erinyes who attend
elder-born brothers (notes [8], [9]); and Apollo, given the aegis,
revives Hector and levels the ditch and wall "as when a boy by the
sea heaps sand in his childish play and then, still playing,
scatters it with his feet and hands" — the most devastating image
of divine ease in epic, delivered, uniquely, as an apostrophe to
the god himself (note [18]).

At the center, before any of this, Zeus speaks the rest of the poem
aloud (56-77, athetized in antiquity for that very completeness):
Patroclus sent out, Sarpedon killed, Patroclus killed by Hector,
Hector killed by Achilles, and after that the long Achaean tide
"until they take steep Ilion through Athena's counsels" — all of it
suspended from the promise to Thetis in Book 1 (note [4]). From
here forward the audience watches men choose what it has heard
decreed. The narrator twice does the same on his own account: Zeus
waits "to see with his eyes the flare of a burning ship," and
Hector rages superb in his last full day of glory — "for he was to
be short-lived: already Pallas Athena was speeding his death-day
under the strength of Peleus' son" (610-614, which Zenodotus cut;
note [30]).

The fighting that fills the second half is the war at its narrowest:
pikes on ship-decks, axes and swords at arm's length, Ajax striding
from deck to deck with a twenty-two-cubit boarding pike like a
trick-rider leaping between four horses (note [34]). The paired
exhortations are the book's moral architecture: Hector's "it is no
dishonor to die defending your fatherland — his wife is safe, his
children after him" (494-499, quoted three centuries later by
Lycurgus prosecuting an Athenian deserter, note [25]) against
Ajax's stripped-down answer to men who have no fatherland at their
backs: "our light of safety is in our hands, not in any gentleness
of war" (733-741, note [36]). Patroclus, mid-book, finally leaves
Eurypylus's hut quoting Nestor's own hope back to himself — the
fatal errand resumes (note [21]). The book ends with Hector's hand
on the stern-horn of the ship that carried the war's first casualty,
calling for fire, and Ajax on a seven-foot plank, counting his
twelve.

 103  did not warm; and she spoke among them all, vexed:[6]
 104  "Fools — who rage against Zeus with no more sense than children!

 106  by word or by force? He sits apart and takes no thought,
 107  no notice; for he says that among the immortal gods
 108  in power and might he is beyond dispute the first.
 109  So — swallow whatever evil he sends each one of you.
 110  Already now, I think, calamity is built for Ares:
 111  his son has perished in the fighting — the dearest of men to him —
 112  Ascalaphus, whom massive Ares calls his own."
 113  So she spoke; and Ares struck his sturdy thighs
 114  with his flat hands, and cried out in grief:
 115  "Do not blame me now, you who keep the halls of Olympus,[7]
 116  if I go down to the ships of the Achaeans to avenge
 117  the killing of my son — even if my fate is to be struck
 118  by Zeus's bolt, and lie with the corpses in the blood and the dust."
 119  So he spoke, and ordered Terror and Panic to yoke
 120  his horses; and he himself got into his blazing armor.
 121  And then a greater and more grievous anger and wrath
 122  would have been wrought by Zeus against the immortals,
 123  had not Athena, in terror for all the gods,
 124  sprung through the doorway, leaving the chair where she sat.
 125  She tore the helmet from his head, the shield from his shoulders,
 126  and took the bronze spear out of his heavy hand
 127  and set it up; and she lashed rushing Ares with words:
 128  "Madman! Wits gone — you are destroyed! So you do have ears
 129  for hearing — but your mind is perished, and your shame.
 130  Do you not hear what the goddess white-armed Hera says,
 131  come straight now from Olympian Zeus?
 132  Or do you want to fill your own cup of evils and come back
 133  to Olympus by compulsion, grieving as you go,
 134  and plant a great calamity for all the rest of us?
 135  For he will leave the high-hearted Trojans and the Achaeans
 136  on the instant, and come to Olympus to set us tumbling,
 137  and he will seize us each in turn, guilty or not.
 138  So drop your anger now for your son. Better men
 139  than he by strength of hands have been killed before this,
 140  or will be killed hereafter. It is a hard thing
 141  to rescue the line and offspring of every man."
 142  So speaking she seated rushing Ares on his chair.
 143  And Hera called Apollo outside the house,
 144  and Iris, who runs messages for the immortal gods,
 145  and speaking winged words she addressed them:
 146  "Zeus commands you both to come to Ida with all speed;
 147  and when you have come, and looked on the face of Zeus,
 148  do whatever he urges and commands."
 149  So speaking the lady Hera went back in
 150  and sat on her chair; and the two sprang up and flew.
 151  They came to Ida of the many springs, mother of wild things,
 152  and found the wide-seeing son of Cronos seated on Gargaron
 153  peak; and a fragrant cloud was wreathed about him.
 154  The two of them came before Zeus the cloud-gatherer
 155  and stood; and seeing them his heart was not angered,
 156  because they had obeyed his dear wife's words so quickly.
 157  To Iris first he spoke winged words:
 158  "Up, go, swift Iris; to lord Poseidon
 159  carry all this word, and be no false messenger.
 160  Command him to leave the battle and the war
 161  and go to the tribes of the gods, or into the bright sea.
 162  And if he will not obey my words, but makes light of them,
 163  let him consider then in his mind and heart
 164  whether, strong as he is, he can dare to stand my coming —
 165  since I say my strength is far greater than his,
 166  and my birth is elder; yet his heart does not shrink
 167  from calling himself my equal — whom the others dread."
 168  So he spoke, and wind-footed swift Iris did not disobey,
 169  and went down from the mountains of Ida to holy Ilion.
 170  As when out of the clouds the snow flies, or the hail,
 171  cold under the blast of the sky-born North Wind,
 172  so swiftly, straining, flew swift Iris,
 173  and standing close she spoke to the famed earth-shaker:
 174  "A message for you, holder of the earth, blue-maned one:
 175  I have come here carrying it from aegis-bearing Zeus.
 176  He commands you to leave the battle and the war
 177  and go to the tribes of the gods, or into the bright sea.
 178  And if you will not obey his words, but make light of them,
 179  he threatens to come here himself and fight you
 180  strength against strength; and he warns you to keep out
 181  from under his hands, since he says his strength is far greater
 182  than yours, and his birth is elder; yet your heart does not shrink
 183  from calling yourself his equal — whom the others dread."
 184  Then, deeply angered, the famed earth-shaker answered her:
 185  "Shame on him! Great as he is, this is arrogance —[8]
 186  to hold me back by force, against my will, who am his equal in honor.
 187  Three brothers are we, whom Rhea bore to Cronos:
 188  Zeus, and I, and Hades third, who is king of those below.
 189  All was divided three ways; each got his share of honor:
 190  I drew the grey sea to live in forever
 191  when the lots were shaken; and Hades drew the misty dark,
 192  and Zeus drew the wide heaven, in the clear air and the clouds;
 193  but the earth is common to all three still, and high Olympus.
 194  So I will not walk by the mind of Zeus. Let him stay
 195  quiet in his third portion, strong as he is;
 196  and let him not try to frighten me with his hands as though I were
 197  some coward. Better he keep his blustering words
 198  to scold the daughters and the sons he fathered himself,
 199  who will have to listen to whatever he commands."
 200  Then answered him wind-footed swift Iris:
 201  "Is this really the word, holder of the earth, blue-maned one,
 202  that I am to carry to Zeus — this hard, unbending word?
 203  Or will you turn a little? The hearts of the great can turn.[9]
 204  You know how the Erinyes always side with the elder-born."
 205  Then Poseidon the earth-shaker answered her:
 206  "Iris, goddess — that word of yours was rightly spoken:
 207  it is a fine thing when a messenger knows what is fitting.
 208  But this comes as bitter grief to my heart and spirit —
 209  when he chooses to rail with angry words at one
 210  allotted an equal share, doomed to the same portion.
 211  Still — for now I will yield, indignant though I am.
 212  But I will tell you this, and it is a threat from my heart:[10]
 213  if in spite of me, and Athena driver of the spoil,
 214  and Hera, and Hermes, and lord Hephaestus,
 215  he spares steep Ilion — if he refuses
 216  to sack it and to hand great power to the Argives —
 217  let him know this: between us two the anger will have no healing."

 219  and went into the sea and sank; and the Achaean heroes missed him.

 221  "Go now, dear Phoebus, to Hector of the bronze helmet.
 222  For by this time the holder of the earth, the earth-shaker,
 223  has gone into the bright sea, dodging our sheer anger —
 224  otherwise even the others would have heard of our fight,
 225  the gods who are down below, gathered around Cronos.

 230  and shake it hard over the Achaean heroes, to rout them.
 231  And for yourself, far-striker: let glorious Hector be your care.
 232  Wake the great fury in him, until the Achaeans
 233  come in their flight to the ships and the Hellespont.
 234  From there I myself will devise the word and the work
 235  by which the Achaeans may catch their breath again from toil."
 236  So he spoke, and Apollo did not disobey his father,
 237  but went down from the mountains of Ida like a hawk,
 238  the swift dove-killer, fastest of flying things.
 239  He found the son of war-minded Priam, brilliant Hector,
 240  sitting up — no longer lying down: he had just gathered his spirit,
 241  knowing the companions around him; and the gasping and the sweat
 242  had ceased, for the mind of aegis-bearing Zeus was waking him.
 243  And standing close, the worker-from-afar, Apollo, spoke to him:
 244  "Hector, son of Priam — why do you sit apart from the others,
 245  with so little strength in you? Has some trouble found you?"
 246  And with the little strength he had, Hector of the flashing helmet answered:[12]
 247  "Which of the gods are you, kindest one, who ask me face to face?
 248  Have you not heard? By the sterns of the Achaean ships,
 249  as I killed his companions, Ajax good at the war-cry
 250  struck me on the chest with a stone, and stopped my rushing courage.
 251  Indeed I thought that on this day I would look on the dead
 252  and the house of Hades: I was breathing out my own dear life."
 253  Then lord Apollo the worker-from-afar answered him:
 254  "Take heart: such a helper has the son of Cronos
 255  sent down from Ida to stand beside you and defend you —
 256  Phoebus Apollo of the golden sword: I who before now
 257  have guarded you, yourself and your steep citadel alike.
 258  Up now: command your many charioteers
 259  to drive their swift horses at the hollow ships;
 260  and I will go before them and smooth the whole road
 261  for the horses, and turn the Achaean heroes back."
 262  So speaking he breathed great fury into the shepherd of the people.
 263  As when a stalled horse, corn-fed at the manger,[13]
 264  breaks his tether and gallops drumming across the plain,
 265  used to bathing in the fair-flowing river,
 266  glorying: he holds his head high, and the mane
 267  streams along his shoulders; sure of his own splendor,
 268  his knees carry him lightly to the haunts and pastures of horses:
 269  so Hector plied his feet and knees, quick and light,
 270  urging on his horsemen, now he had heard the god's voice.
 271  And the others — as when dogs and countrymen
 272  chase an antlered stag or a wild goat,
 273  and a sheer rock-face or a shadowy wood
 274  saves it, since it was not their portion to catch it,
 275  and then, drawn by their clamor, a bearded lion
 276  shows himself in the way, and turns them all back for all their fury:
 277  so for a while the Danaans followed close, in a mass,
 278  stabbing with swords and double-curved spears;
 279  but when they saw Hector ranging down the ranks of men,
 280  they were afraid, and every man's heart dropped to his feet.
 281  Then Thoas spoke among them, Andraemon's son,[14]
 282  far the best of the Aetolians — skilled with the javelin,
 283  brave in the standing fight; and in assembly few Achaeans
 284  could beat him, when the young men contended in debate.
 285  With good will toward them he spoke and addressed them:
 286  "Shame! — a great marvel is this I see with my eyes:
 287  how he has risen again, escaped the death-spirits —
 288  Hector! The heart in each of us hoped hard enough
 289  that he had died under the hands of Ajax, Telamon's son.
 290  But some one of the gods has rescued and saved him again —
 291  Hector, who has unstrung the knees of many Danaans,
 292  as he will now too, I think: for not without loud-thundering Zeus
 293  does he stand there in the front rank, blazing like this.
 294  Come then — as I say it, let us all obey:
 295  Let us order the multitude back to the ships;
 296  and we ourselves, all who claim to be best in the army,
 297  let us stand, and see if we can blunt him first, meeting him
 298  with spears held high. For all his fury, I think
 299  his heart will fear to sink into the crowd of the Danaans."
 300  So he spoke; and they heard him well, and obeyed.
 301  Those around Ajax and lord Idomeneus,
 302  and Teucer, and Meriones, and Meges the equal of Ares,
 303  called up the best men and formed for battle,
 304  facing Hector and the Trojans; and behind them
 305  the multitude went back to the ships of the Achaeans.
 306  The Trojans struck first, massed; Hector led them
 307  with long strides; and in front of him went Phoebus Apollo,
 308  his shoulders wrapped in cloud, holding the rushing aegis,
 309  terrible, shag-fringed, dazzling — which the smith
 310  Hephaestus gave to Zeus, to carry for the routing of men;[15]
 311  holding this in his hands he led the army.
 312  And the Argives stood to meet them, close-massed, and the war-cry
 313  rose shrill from both sides; from the bowstrings the arrows
 314  leapt; and many spears from daring hands
 315  fixed themselves in the flesh of quick young fighters,
 316  and many midway, before they could reach the white flesh,
 317  stood planted in the earth, still hungry to glut themselves on it.
 318  So long as Phoebus Apollo held the aegis unmoving,
 319  so long the missiles of both sides struck home, and the people fell;
 320  but when he looked the swift-colted Danaans full in the face
 321  and shook it, and himself gave a great shout, then the heart
 322  in their chests he bewitched, and they forgot their rushing courage.
 323  And as two wild beasts stampede a herd of cattle
 324  or a great flock of sheep in the milking-dark of night,
 325  coming suddenly, when the herdsman is not by,
 326  so the Achaeans panicked, strengthless: for Apollo drove
 327  the panic into them, and handed glory to the Trojans and Hector.
 328  Then man killed man, as the battle scattered.
 329  Hector cut down Stichius and Arcesilaus,[16]
 330  the one a leader of the bronze-shirted Boeotians,
 331  the other the trusted companion of great-hearted Menestheus;
 332  and Aeneas stripped the life from Medon and Iasus.
 333  Now the one — Medon — was a bastard son of godlike Oïleus
 334  and brother of Ajax; but he lived in Phylace,
 335  far from his own fatherland, having killed a man,
 336  a kinsman of his stepmother Eriopis, wife of Oïleus;
 337  and Iasus was set a captain of the Athenians,
 338  and was called the son of Sphelus, Boucolus's son.
 339  And Polydamas killed Mecisteus, and Polites Echius,
 340  in the first onset, and brilliant Agenor killed Clonius;
 341  and Paris hit Deiochus at the base of the shoulder, from behind,
 342  as he fled through the front-fighters, and drove the bronze clean through.
 343  While they were stripping the armor from these, the Achaeans,
 344  tangled in the dug ditch and the stakes,
 345  were fleeing this way and that, and diving behind the wall by necessity.
 346  And Hector called to the Trojans with a great shout:
 347  "Straight for the ships — and leave the bloody spoils![17]
 348  Whomever I see hanging back, away from the ships,
 349  I will plan his death on the spot; and no brothers of his,
 350  no sisters, will give his dead body its portion of fire:
 351  the dogs will drag him before our city."
 352  So speaking, with a full swing of the whip he drove his horses on,
 353  calling to the Trojans down the ranks; and all of them with him,
 354  shouting as one, steered the chariot-pulling horses
 355  with a prodigious roar. And in front of them Phoebus Apollo,
 356  kicking down the banks of the deep ditch with his feet,
 357  easily, tumbled them into the middle, and bridged a causeway
 358  long and wide — about as wide as a spear-cast
 359  carries, when a man is trying his strength.
 360  Down this they poured, rank after rank, and before them Apollo
 361  held the aegis beyond all price; and he kicked down the wall
 362  of the Achaeans — easily — as when a boy by the sea[18]
 363  heaps sand into shapes in his childish play,
 364  and then, still playing, scatters them again with his feet and hands:
 365  so you, archer Phoebus, poured into ruin the long
 366  labor and misery of the Argives, and drove the panic through them.
 367  So they halted and held their ground beside the ships,
 368  calling to one another, and lifting their hands
 369  to all the gods, each man prayed aloud;
 370  and Nestor above all, the Gerenian, warden of the Achaeans,
 371  prayed, stretching his arms toward the starry heaven:
 372  "Father Zeus — if ever anyone in wheat-rich Argos,[19]
 373  burning to you the fat thighs of ox or sheep,
 374  prayed for his homecoming, and you promised it with a nod:
 375  remember it, Olympian; ward off the pitiless day,
 376  and do not let the Achaeans be broken so by the Trojans."
 377  So he spoke in prayer; and Zeus the counselor thundered loud,
 378  hearing the prayers of the old man, Neleus' son.
 379  But the Trojans, when they heard the thunder of aegis-bearing Zeus,
 380  leapt at the Argives all the harder, and remembered their war-craft.
 381  As a great wave of the wide-wayed sea[20]
 382  comes down over a ship's bulwarks, when the strength of the wind
 383  drives it high — for that above all makes the waves grow:
 384  so the Trojans with a great cry came down over the wall,
 385  and driving their horses in they fought at the sterns,
 386  hand to hand with double-curved spears — they from their chariots,
 387  and the Achaeans high up, from the decks of their black ships,
 388  with the long pikes that lay aboard for sea-fighting,
 389  jointed and socketed, their heads clothed in bronze.
 390  Now Patroclus, so long as the Achaeans and Trojans
 391  fought for the wall, outside the swift ships,
 392  sat in the hut of gracious Eurypylus
 393  and delighted him with talk, and on his grievous wound
 394  sprinkled the remedies against the black pains.
 395  But when he saw the Trojans storming over the wall,
 396  and the Danaans' shouting and panic broke out,
 397  he groaned aloud, and struck both his thighs
 398  with his flat hands, and cried out in grief:
 399  "Eurypylus — I can stay with you here no longer,[21]
 400  much as you need me: a great struggle has risen.
 401  Let your aide look after you now; I myself
 402  will hurry to Achilles, to rouse him to fight.
 403  Who knows whether, with a god's help, I might stir his heart
 404  by my persuading? The persuasion of a companion is a good thing."
 405  And even as he spoke his feet were carrying him. Meanwhile the Achaeans
 406  stood fast against the Trojan advance, yet could not
 407  push them from the ships, though they were fewer;
 408  nor could the Trojans ever break the Danaans' lines
 409  and force their way in among the huts and the ships.
 410  But as a chalk-line trues a ship-timber straight[22]
 411  in the hands of a skilled carpenter, who knows
 412  his whole craft well by the promptings of Athena,
 413  so their battle and their war were drawn even and taut.
 414  And others fought their fight at other ships,
 415  but Hector made straight for glorious Ajax.
 416  The two of them labored over a single ship, and neither
 417  could win: Hector could not drive Ajax off and fire the ship,
 418  nor Ajax push Hector back — since a god had brought him.
 419  Then shining Ajax hit Caletor, Clytius's son,[23]
 420  with the spear in the chest as he carried fire toward the ship:
 421  he fell with a thud, and the brand dropped from his hand.
 422  And Hector, when he saw with his own eyes his cousin
 423  fallen in the dust before the black ship,
 424  called to the Trojans and Lycians with a great shout:
 425  "Trojans and Lycians and Dardanians who fight at close quarters —
 426  do not give back from the battle in this narrow place:
 427  save the son of Clytius, or the Achaeans will strip
 428  his armor, fallen where the ships stand gathered."
 429  So speaking he cast at Ajax with his shining spear.
 430  Him he missed; but Lycophron, Mastor's son,
 431  Ajax's henchman from Cythera, who lived with him —
 432  for he had killed a man in most holy Cythera —
 433  him he struck on the head above the ear with the sharp bronze
 434  as he stood beside Ajax; and backward in the dust
 435  he dropped from the ship's stern to the ground, and his limbs were unstrung.
 436  And Ajax shuddered, and spoke to his brother:
 437  "Teucer, old friend — our trusted companion is killed,
 438  Mastor's son, the man from Cythera we honored
 439  in our halls like our own dear parents.
 440  Great-hearted Hector has killed him. Where now are your arrows
 441  of quick death, and the bow that Phoebus Apollo gave you?"
 442  So he spoke; and Teucer understood, and came running to stand
 443  beside him, his back-bent bow in his hand
 444  and the arrow-holding quiver; and fast he loosed his shafts at the Trojans.
 445  And he hit Cleitus, the shining son of Peisenor,
 446  companion of Polydamas, Panthous's illustrious son,
 447  with the reins in his hands, laboring with his horses;
 448  for he held them where the most battle-lines were driven
 449  in confusion, doing a favor to Hector and the Trojans. But swiftly
 450  the evil came on him that no one could hold off, wish it as they might:
 451  for the arrow of many groans dropped into the back of his neck,
 452  and he crashed from the chariot, and the horses shied back,
 453  rattling the empty car. And lord Polydamas saw it
 454  at once, and was first to come and stand at the horses' heads.
 455  He gave them to Astynous, Protiaon's son,
 456  with many orders to hold them close and keep them
 457  in his eye; and himself went back and mixed with the front-fighters.
 458  And Teucer was drawing another arrow, for Hector
 459  of the bronze helmet — and would have stopped the battle
 460  at the ships of the Achaeans, had he hit him mid-triumph
 461  and torn out his life. But he did not slip the close mind of Zeus,
 462  who was guarding Hector, and robbed Teucer, Telamon's son, of his triumph:[24]
 463  the well-twisted string on the blameless bow —
 464  Zeus broke it as he drew; and the bronze-weighted arrow
 465  swerved wide and away, and the bow dropped from his hand.
 466  And Teucer shuddered, and spoke to his brother:
 467  "Look — how utterly some power is shearing off the plans
 468  of our fighting! It has struck the bow from my hand
 469  and snapped the fresh-twisted string I bound on it
 470  this very morning, to bear the arrows springing thick and fast."
 471  Then great Telamonian Ajax answered him:
 472  "Old friend — then let the bow and the showering arrows
 473  lie, since a god has spited the Danaans and wrecked them.
 474  Take a long spear in your hands and a shield on your shoulder
 475  and fight the Trojans yourself, and drive on the rest of the army.
 476  If they take us, at least let it cost them labor —
 477  the well-benched ships. Let us remember our war-craft."
 478  So he spoke; and Teucer set the bow away in the huts,
 479  and about his shoulders he slung a shield of four hides,
 480  and on his powerful head he set a well-made helmet,
 481  horsehair-crested; the plume nodded terribly above it;
 482  and he took up a stout spear, edged with sharp bronze,
 483  and set off at a run, and quickly stood by Ajax.
 484  And Hector, when he saw Teucer's shots crippled,
 485  called to the Trojans and Lycians with a great shout:
 486  "Trojans and Lycians and Dardanians who fight at close quarters —
 487  be men, my friends; remember your rushing courage
 488  here along the hollow ships: for I have seen with my own eyes
 489  the shots of their best man crippled by Zeus.
 490  Easily known among men is the strength of Zeus —[25]
 491  both those to whom he hands the higher glory
 492  and those he wears down and will not defend:
 493  so now he wears down the Argives' fury, and helps us.
 494  Fight at the ships in one mass! And whichever of you,
 495  hit from afar or stabbed close, meets his death and his doom —
 496  let him die. It is no dishonor for him to die
 497  defending his fatherland: his wife is safe after him, and his children,
 498  his house and his portion of land unharmed, if the Achaeans
 499  go away in their ships to their dear fatherland."
 500  So speaking he stirred the fury and the heart of every man.
 501  And Ajax on the other side called to his companions:
 502  "Shame, Argives! Now it is certain: either we perish[26]
 503  or we are saved, and beat the ruin back from the ships.
 504  Do you hope, if Hector of the flashing helmet takes the ships,
 505  that each of you will walk home on foot to his fatherland?
 506  Do you not hear him driving his whole army —
 507  Hector, who is raging now to burn the ships?
 508  It is not to a dance he is inviting them: he invites them to fight.
 509  For us there is no plan, no better thought than this:
 510  to bring our hands and fury against them, body to body.
 511  Better once for all to seize our death or our life
 512  than be squeezed slowly dry in the dread combat,
 513  here by the ships, uselessly, under worse men than ourselves."
 514  So speaking he stirred the fury and the heart of every man.
 515  Then Hector cut down Schedius, Perimedes' son,
 516  a captain of the Phocians; and Ajax cut down Laodamas,
 517  a leader of the foot-soldiers, Antenor's shining son;
 518  and Polydamas stripped the life from Otus of Cyllene,
 519  companion of Phyleus' son, a captain of the great-hearted Epeians.
 520  Meges saw it and sprang at him; but Polydamas swerved
 521  from under, and Meges missed him — for Apollo
 522  would not let Panthous's son be broken among the front-fighters —
 523  but he stabbed Croesmus full in the chest with the spear:
 524  he fell with a thud, and Meges set to stripping the armor from his shoulders.
 525  But meanwhile Dolops sprang on him, a master of the spear,
 526  Lampus's son — the strongest son that Lampus fathered,
 527  Laomedon's own son, well skilled in rushing courage —
 528  who stabbed the middle of Phyleus' son's shield with his spear[27]
 529  at close range; but the thick corslet saved him,
 530  the corslet he wore, close-fitted with its plates: the one Phyleus
 531  brought home once out of Ephyra, from the river Selleïs.
 532  A guest-friend gave it to him — Euphetes, lord of men —
 533  to carry into war, a defense against enemy men;
 534  and now it kept destruction from the flesh of his son.
 535  Then Meges stabbed with his sharp-pointed spear at the topmost
 536  ridge of Dolops's bronze helmet, thick with horsehair,
 537  and sheared the horsehair plume clean off; and the whole of it
 538  fell in the dust, fresh-shining with its scarlet dye.
 539  While Meges held his ground and fought him, still hoping for victory,
 540  warlike Menelaus came to his rescue:
 541  he took his stand aside with his spear, unseen, and hit his shoulder
 542  from behind; and the point, raging, drove through his chest
 543  and out, straining onward; and he crumpled down on his face.
 544  The two of them moved in to strip the bronze armor
 545  from his shoulders; but Hector called on his kinsmen,
 546  all of them; and first he rebuked Hicetaon's son,
 547  strong Melanippus. He had herded his shambling cattle[28]
 548  in Percote a while, with the enemy far away;
 549  but when the double-ended ships of the Danaans came,
 550  he came back to Ilion, and stood out among the Trojans,
 551  and lived with Priam, who honored him like his own children.
 552  Him Hector rebuked, and spoke a word, and named him:
 553  "Is this how we slacken, Melanippus? Is your own heart
 554  not turned at all — for your cousin killed?
 555  Do you not see how they swarm over Dolops's armor?
 556  Follow me. No longer can we stand off and fight
 557  the Argives at a distance: now we kill them, or they pull down
 558  steep Ilion from its height and cut her citizens down."
 559  So speaking he led the way, and the other followed, a man like a god.
 560  And great Telamonian Ajax roused the Argives:
 561  "Friends — be men! Set shame in your hearts,
 562  and feel shame before each other in the strong encounters.
 563  Where men feel shame, more are saved than slain;[29]
 564  where they run, no fame rises, and no strength."
 565  So he spoke; and they themselves were furious to beat the enemy off,
 566  and took his word to heart, and fenced the ships
 567  in a hedge of bronze; and Zeus drove the Trojans on against them.
 568  And Menelaus, good at the war-cry, urged Antilochus:
 569  "Antilochus — no other Achaean is younger than you,
 570  none faster on his feet or braver in the fighting:
 571  might you not spring out and hit some man of the Trojans?"
 572  So speaking he hurried back; but he had stirred him,
 573  and out from the front-fighters he sprang, and cast his shining spear,
 574  glancing all round him; and the Trojans shrank away
 575  from the man as he threw. And his shot did not go in vain:
 576  Hicetaon's high-hearted son, Melanippus,
 577  coming into the battle, he hit in the chest by the nipple:
 578  he fell with a thud, and darkness covered his eyes.
 579  And Antilochus sprang on him as a hound springs on a fawn
 580  that is hit — the hunter's shot caught it leaping
 581  out of its bed, and unstrung its limbs beneath it:
 582  so on you, Melanippus, staunch Antilochus sprang,
 583  to strip your armor. But he was not unseen by brilliant Hector,
 584  who came running through the combat to face him;
 585  and Antilochus did not stand him, quick fighter though he was,
 586  but fled, like a wild beast that has done some mischief —[30]
 587  that has killed a dog, or a herdsman with his cattle,
 588  and runs before the crowd of men can gather:
 589  so Nestor's son ran; and after him the Trojans and Hector
 590  with a prodigious roar poured their missiles of groaning;
 591  and he turned and stood, when he reached the band of his companions.
 592  And now the Trojans, like flesh-eating lions,
 593  stormed at the ships, accomplishing the commands of Zeus,
 594  who woke the great fury in them always, and bewitched the heart
 595  of the Argives, and stole their triumph, and drove the others on.
 596  For his heart willed the glory for Hector,
 597  Priam's son, so he might hurl on the beaked ships
 598  the prodigious, unwearying fire, and bring to pass
 599  the whole extraordinary prayer of Thetis. For Zeus the counselor
 600  waited for one thing: to see with his own eyes
 601  the flare of a single burning ship. From that moment on
 602  he would make the Trojans' tide run back
 603  from the ships, and hand the glory to the Danaans.
 604  With this in mind he woke against the hollow ships
 605  Hector, Priam's son — already burning hot himself.
 606  He raged like Ares the spear-shaker, or as annihilating fire
 607  rages in the mountains, in the thickets of a deep wood;
 608  the foam stood at his mouth, and his two eyes
 609  blazed under his grim brows, and around his temples the helmet
 610  shook terribly as Hector fought. For out of the upper air
 611  Zeus himself was his defender — Zeus who honored
 612  and glorified him, one man among many; for he was to be[31]
 613  short-lived: already Pallas Athena was speeding
 614  his death-day on, under the strength of Peleus' son.
 615  And he strained to break the ranks of men, probing them
 616  wherever he saw the thickest crowd and the best armor;
 617  but he could not break them for all his blazing:
 618  they held, locked like a tower — like a rock-face,
 619  sheer and huge, hard by the grey salt water,
 620  that abides the quick paths of the shrill winds
 621  and the swollen waves belching against it:
 622  so the Danaans stood the Trojans, steady, and did not run.
 623  But he, glittering with fire on every side, leapt on the mass,
 624  and fell on them as a wave falls on a swift ship,
 625  wind-fed, ravening, out of the clouds: and the whole ship
 626  is buried in spray, and the terrible blast of the wind
 627  roars in the sail, and the sailors tremble at heart,
 628  terrified: for they are carried only just out from under death:
 629  so the heart was torn in the chest of every Achaean.
 630  And he came on like a murderous lion on cattle[32]
 631  grazing in their thousands in the water-meadow of a great marsh,
 632  and among them a herdsman not yet sure of his skill
 633  to fight a beast off from the carcass of a twist-horned cow:
 634  he keeps pace always with the first ranks or the last,
 635  but the lion leaps on the middle and devours a cow,
 636  and all the rest stampede: so then the Achaeans
 637  fled unearthly before Hector and father Zeus,
 638  all of them — though he killed one man only: Periphetes of Mycenae,
 639  the dear son of Copreus, who used to carry the messages
 640  of king Eurystheus to the might of Heracles.
 641  Of that far worse father was born a better son
 642  in every virtue — in speed of foot and in fighting,
 643  and in mind among the first of the Mycenaeans;
 644  and it was he, then, who handed Hector the higher glory.
 645  For turning back, he tripped against the rim of his shield —
 646  the shield he carried himself, reaching his feet, a tower against javelins;
 647  tangled in it he tumbled backward, and the helmet
 648  clanged terribly about his temples as he fell.
 649  And Hector saw it sharply, and ran to stand beside him,
 650  and fixed the spear in his chest, and killed him close by
 651  his own dear companions; and they could not help him, though grieving:
 652  they were themselves too much afraid of brilliant Hector.
 653  Now they had come inside the ships, and around them stood
 654  the ends of the ships drawn up in the first line; and the Trojans poured in.
 655  And the Argives gave back from the first line of ships
 656  by necessity, but held their ground right there among the huts,
 657  massed together, not scattering through the camp: shame held them,
 658  and fear; and they called to one another without ceasing.
 659  And Nestor above all, the Gerenian, warden of the Achaeans,
 660  begged each man by his parents, clasping their knees with his words:
 661  "Friends — be men! Set in your hearts the shame[33]
 662  of what other men will say; and remember, each of you,
 663  your children and your wives, your property and your parents —
 664  you whose parents live, and you whose parents are dead:
 665  for their sake, absent though they are, I clasp your knees here:
 666  stand strong — and do not turn yourselves to panic."
 667  So speaking he stirred the fury and the heart of every man.
 668  And from their eyes Athena thrust away the prodigious
 669  cloud of mist; and light came hard and clear on both sides,
 670  toward the ships and toward the leveling war alike.
 671  And they made out Hector, good at the war-cry, and his companions,
 672  both those who stood behind and were not fighting
 673  and those who fought the battle along the swift ships.
 674  Nor did it please the heart of great-hearted Ajax any longer
 675  to stand where the other sons of the Achaeans stood back:
 676  he went striding along the decks of the ships,
 677  wielding in his hands a great jointed pike for sea-fighting,
 678  twenty-two cubits long, socketed with rings.
 679  And as a man well skilled in trick-riding,[34]
 680  who has yoked together four horses picked from many
 681  and comes sweeping in from the plain toward the great city
 682  along the public road — and many marvel at him,
 683  men and women both — while he, steady, sure, and always
 684  leaping, changes from horse to horse as they fly along:
 685  so Ajax went striding deck to deck across the swift ships
 686  with long steps, and his voice went up to the very sky
 687  as with terrible shouts, always, he called on the Danaans
 688  to defend the ships and the huts. Nor did Hector
 689  stay back in the crowd of close-armored Trojans:
 690  but as a fiery eagle drops on a nation of winged birds
 691  feeding by a river's edge —
 692  geese, or cranes, or long-necked swans —
 693  so Hector went straight at a dark-prowed ship,
 694  charging it head-on; and from behind Zeus thrust him
 695  onward with his very great hand, and drove the army with him.
 696  And again the fighting sharpened by the ships.
 697  You would say they met each other in the war unworn
 698  and unbroken, so furiously they fought;
 699  and this was the thinking of the fighters: the Achaeans
 700  said they would not escape the evil, but must perish;
 701  and the heart in every Trojan's chest was hoping
 702  to fire the ships and cut down the Achaean heroes.
 703  With these thoughts they stood against each other;
 704  and Hector laid his hand on the stern of a seagoing ship —[35]
 705  a fair ship, quick on the salt water, that had carried Protesilaus
 706  to Troy, and never carried him home again to his fatherland.
 707  Around that ship Achaeans and Trojans
 708  cut each other down hand to hand; no longer at a distance
 709  did they stand the volleys of arrows and of javelins,
 710  but standing close, one heart in all of them,
 711  they fought with sharpened axes and with hatchets
 712  and with great swords and double-curved spears;
 713  and many fine blades, dark-bound at the hilt,
 714  fell to the ground, some from the hands, some from the shoulders
 715  of the fighting men; and the black earth ran blood.
 716  And Hector, once he had seized the stern-horn, would not let go:
 717  gripping it in his hands, he called to the Trojans:
 718  "Bring fire! And all together raise the war-cry!
 719  Now Zeus has given us a day worth all the rest:
 720  to take the ships — the ships that came here against the gods' will
 721  and made us so much misery — through the cowardice of the elders,
 722  who, when I wanted to fight at the ships' sterns,
 723  held me back myself, and kept the army in.
 724  But if wide-seeing Zeus was damaging our wits then,
 725  now he himself is driving and commanding us."
 726  So he spoke; and they leapt at the Argives all the harder,
 727  and Ajax could hold no longer: the missiles pressed him too hard.
 728  He gave back a little, thinking now to die,
 729  onto the seven-foot bridge-plank, and left the deck of the balanced ship.
 730  There he stood watchful; and always with his spear
 731  he beat the Trojans off the ships — whoever carried the unwearying fire —
 732  and always with terrible shouts he called on the Danaans:
 733  "Friends, Danaan heroes, squires of Ares —[36]
 734  be men, my friends; remember your rushing courage.
 735  Do we imagine there are allies at our backs?
 736  Some stronger wall, to ward the ruin off our men?
 737  There is no city near us fitted with towers
 738  where we might make defense, and a people to turn the scale.
 739  No: we sit in the plain of the close-armored Trojans,
 740  pinned against the sea, far from our fatherland.
 741  Our light of safety is in our hands — not in any gentleness of war."
 742  He spoke, and pressed on furious with his sharp-pointed spear.
 743  And whoever of the Trojans came driving at the hollow ships
 744  with blazing fire, to please Hector at his urging,
 745  Ajax stood waiting and stabbed him with the long spear:
 746  twelve men he wounded close-in before the ships.

NOTES

[1] (15.4-11) Zeus wakes to the whole tableau at once: the rout,
    Poseidon among the Argives, and Hector "laboring for breath,
    his wits astray, vomiting blood — for not the feeblest of the
    Achaeans had hit him." The litotes is the poem's dry salute to
    Ajax; the concussion is continuous from 14.433-439. What wakes
    is not just the god but the plot.

[2] (15.18-33) The punishment of Hera: hung from heaven in a golden
    unbreakable band, "and from your feet I slung two anvils" — the
    gods raging up and down Olympus, unable to free her, and any
    who tried "I seized and hurled from the threshold, till he
    struck the earth with little life in him." The cosmic-discipline
    memory (cf. Hephaestus's version, 1.590-594) is deployed as
    marital threat; and the occasion was the same Heracles-storm
    that Sleep remembered at 14.249-261 — the poem's back-story is
    consistent across books, and both tellings exist to make one
    point: this has happened before, and she did it anyway.

[3] (15.36-46) Hera's oath — the fullest in the poem: Earth, wide
    Heaven, the down-flowing water of Styx ("the greatest oath and
    the most terrible for the blessed gods"), "and your own sacred
    head, and the bed of the two of us, our bridal bed." And it is
    a lawyer's oath: every word true — Poseidon did act of his own
    heart (13.15-16 confirms it) — and the seduction, the girdle,
    and Sleep's errand to Poseidon (14.354-360) nowhere within its
    terms. She swears by the very bed she used. Zeus smiles (47):
    readers have argued ever since about how much of that smile is
    belief.

[4] (15.49-77) The plan of the poem, spoken by its administrator:
    Hector to drive the Achaeans on the ships of Achilles; "and he
    will send out his companion Patroclus; and glorious Hector will
    kill him with the spear... and among them my own son, brilliant
    Sarpedon. And in anger for him brilliant Achilles will kill
    Hector" — then the reversed tide "until the Achaeans take steep
    Ilion through the counsels of Athena." Sixty lines of Iliad and
    the fall of Troy, dictated in advance and pinned to the Book 1
    promise ("on the day the goddess Thetis clasped my knees").
    Aristophanes and Aristarchus athetized 56-77 and Zenodotus
    omitted 64-77 — prophecy so total seemed to them to deflate the
    tale. It does the opposite: from here on, suspense is replaced
    by witness. Zeus names his own son among the costs with zero
    comment; Book 16 will price that silence.

[5] (15.80-83) Hera travels at the speed of homesick thought: "as
    when the mind of a man darts, who has traveled over much
    country, and thinks in his quick wits 'would I were here, or
    there'" — the mind's instant travel as the fastest thing the
    poet knows (cf. Odyssey 7.36, ships "swift as wing or
    thought"). Cognition itself becomes the vehicle of a god.

[6] (15.101-103) Hera's laugh: "she laughed with her lips; but the
    forehead above her dark brows did not warm." The poem's
    greatest piece of face-writing — the smile as mask worn over
    rage, observed with a portraitist's exactness three thousand
    years before the psychology had a vocabulary.

[7] (15.110-142) The Ascalaphus fuse (13.518-525) detonates: Hera
    lights it deliberately, naming the death to wound Zeus through
    Ares. Ares' reply is the book's darkest heroism — "do not blame
    me... even if my fate is to be struck by Zeus's bolt, and lie
    with the corpses in the blood and the dust": the war-god
    volunteering for mortality's posture. Athena disarms him
    physically — helmet, shield, spear — and delivers the poem's
    mortality-policy in the flattest possible terms: "Better men
    than he... have been killed before this, or will be killed
    hereafter. It is a hard thing to rescue the line and offspring
    of every man." A god may not grieve past policy; men, the poem
    knows, do nothing else.

[8] (15.185-199) Poseidon's cosmology: three brothers, three lots —
    "I drew the grey sea to live in forever when the lots were
    shaken; and Hades drew the misty dark, and Zeus drew the wide
    heaven... but the earth is common to all three still, and high
    Olympus." Sovereignty by sortition, not primogeniture — the
    constitutional counter-claim to Zeus's "elder-born and
    stronger" (165-166). The tripartite division by lot has close
    Near Eastern kin (the Akkadian Atrahasis opens with Anu, Enlil,
    and Enki dividing sky, earth, and waters); here it surfaces
    once, in anger, and nowhere else in Homer. "Let him stay quiet
    in his third portion": for one speech, the Iliad's monarchy of
    heaven is a contested federation.

[9] (15.200-211) Iris earns her wage: "Is this really the word...
    Or will you turn a little? The hearts of the great can turn.
    You know how the Erinyes always side with the elder-born." The
    messenger exceeds her brief with a diplomat's question and a
    theological reminder — the Furies as enforcers of birth-order —
    and Poseidon's capitulation praises the craft itself: "it is a
    fine thing when a messenger knows what is fitting." The scene
    is the poem's tribute to its own couriers.

[10] (15.212-217) The yielding god's condition: if Zeus spares
    Ilion against the will of Poseidon, Athena, Hera, Hermes, and
    Hephaestus, "between us two the anger will have no healing."
    Athetized in antiquity, but load-bearing: the fall of Troy is
    established as an inter-divine settlement no single god — not
    even Zeus — can cheaply revoke (cf. 4.30-49, where Zeus traded
    away his own three cities).

[11] (15.229-230, 308-311) The aegis passes to Apollo: made by the
    smith Hephaestus for Zeus "to carry for the routing of men" —
    the one artifact in the poem whose function is pure panic.
    Apollo carries it unshaken at first (318-320: stalemate), then
    shakes it and shouts — and the balance-clause the poem used for
    whole mornings of battle ("the missiles of both sides struck
    home, and the people fell") snaps in a single line.

[12] (15.244-262) Hector's revival: he answers the unrecognized god
    with the accurate history of his own concussion, believing he
    was bound that day for "the dead and the house of Hades";
    Apollo answers with his full title — "Phoebus Apollo of the
    golden sword: I who before now have guarded you, yourself and
    your steep citadel alike" (the helmet of 11.353; the walls he
    built, 7.452-453; 21.446-447). The god who will one day stand
    off and let him die (22.213) introduces himself as his oldest
    bodyguard.

[13] (15.263-270) The stalled-horse simile, repeated verbatim from
    6.506-511 — our English kept, as the Greek is kept (ancient
    critics athetized the repeat here). In Book 6 it carried Paris,
    glittering and trivial, down from Pergamos; here it carries
    Hector back from the edge of death. Same gallop, same streaming
    mane, opposite gravities: the formula system's boldest double
    exposure, and the poem trusts its audience to hold both.

[14] (15.281-300) Thoas of Aetolia, given a rare résumé — best with
    the javelin, brave standing, "and in assembly few Achaeans
    could beat him, when the young men contended in debate": the
    full heroic curriculum (deeds and words, 9.443) in a supporting
    player. His plan — multitude back, champions stand — is the
    professional's answer to a revenant: you cannot outfight a
    portent, but you can blunt it.

[15] (15.318-327) The physics of the aegis: held still, nothing;
    shaken, with the god's shout laid over it, it "bewitched"
    (θέλγε) the heart — the enchantment-verb again (12.255, Circe's
    verb). Panic in Homer is not an emotion but a possession, and
    the stampede simile that follows (two beasts, the herdsman
    absent, "in the milking-dark of night") makes the Achaeans the
    cattle of a masterless field.

[16] (15.328-345) The counter-rout's ledger, paying debts across
    three books: Stichius (13.691) and Arcesilaus (2.495) fall to
    Hector; Aeneas kills Iasus and Medon — whose biography
    (334-337) repeats 13.694-697 verbatim, our English kept: the
    homicide-exile's story told once at his post and once at his
    death, as the register promised. Mecisteus, who carried the
    wounded off the field at 8.333 and 13.422, is carried off
    himself; Clonius (2.495) and Deiochus complete the roll. The
    poem audits its minor lives with the same care as its princes.

[17] (15.346-352) Hector's field-order crosses the poem's deepest
    line: the man who hangs back "I will plan his death on the
    spot; and no brothers of his, no sisters, will give his dead
    body its portion of fire: the dogs will drag him before our
    city." Denial of burial as military discipline, threatened by
    Hector against his own — the exact fate Achilles will attempt
    on Hector's corpse, and the theme of the poem's whole last
    movement. The words are spoken at his triumph's crest; the
    poem never forgets them.

[18] (15.355-366) Apollo bridges the ditch with a kick and levels
    the wall — the wall of Books 7-12, the wall that cost the dead
    their monument-fires — "as when a boy by the sea heaps sand
    into shapes in his childish play, and then, still playing,
    scatters them again with his feet and hands." And the narrator
    turns, for once, to face the god directly: "so you, archer
    Phoebus, poured into ruin the long labor and misery of the
    Argives." The apostrophe (elsewhere reserved for Menelaus and
    Patroclus, figures of pathos) here carries something close to
    reproach: the distance between divine play and human toil has
    never been measured more exactly, in either direction. What
    Books 12-13 fought for line by line, a god undoes as
    recreation.

[19] (15.372-380) Nestor's prayer — the old contract-form: "if
    ever anyone in wheat-rich Argos... prayed for his homecoming,
    and you promised it with a nod: remember it." Zeus thunders —
    hearing him, the poet says plainly. And the Trojans, hearing
    the same thunder, take it for their own sign and charge
    harder. One peal, two readings, both catastrophic in
    different keys: the poem's most economical lesson in omens.

[20] (15.381-390) The wave-over-the-bulwarks simile lands the
    Trojans inside the ships' line, and the fighting goes naval:
    they from chariots, the Achaeans from the decks "with the long
    pikes that lay aboard for sea-fighting, jointed and socketed,
    their heads clothed in bronze" — the ναύμαχα ξυστά, boarding
    weapons stowed since the landing ten years ago, at last taken
    down. The war has come full circle to the gear of its arrival.

[21] (15.390-405) The Patroclus thread, resumed from 11.848: he is
    still in Eurypylus's hut, "delighting him with talk" and
    dressing the wound — the poem's gentlest man at his
    gentlest — when the wall's fall ends the interlude. His exit
    couplet repeats Nestor's own persuasion-lines (11.792-793) in
    the first person, our English kept: "Who knows whether, with a
    god's help, I might stir his heart by my persuading?" He
    leaves to fetch Achilles and will return only inside the plan
    of Zeus (note [4]). "Even as he spoke his feet were carrying
    him": the poem's quietest sentence of doom.

[22] (15.410-414) The carpenter's chalk-line: battle-equilibrium as
    a timber trued straight "in the hands of a skilled carpenter,
    who knows his whole craft well by the promptings of Athena."
    The deadlock similes have run from farmers' scales (12.433) to
    boundary stones (12.421) to this: war measured, again, by the
    honest work it interrupts.

[23] (15.419-441) Caletor, Hector's cousin, falls with the fire-
    brand still in his hand; and Hector's answering cast kills
    Lycophron of Cythera — Ajax's henchman, a homicide-exile "we
    honored in our halls like our own dear parents." The third
    such exile in three books (Medon, note [16]; Patroclus himself,
    23.85-88): the heroic household absorbs killers as kin, and
    they die defending the men who took them in.

[24] (15.458-478) Teucer's bowstring, "fresh-twisted... bound on
    this very morning," snaps in the draw — Zeus, guarding Hector,
    disarms the one man whose shot could end the day. Teucer reads
    it exactly ("some power is shearing off the plans of our
    fighting"); Ajax's answer is the poem's practical theology:
    "let the bow and the showering arrows lie, since a god has
    spited the Danaans... Take a long spear." No lament, no
    inquiry: identify the interference, change weapons, keep
    fighting. Between them the brothers state the whole range of
    sane response to an intervening god.

[25] (15.486-499) Hector's great exhortation. First the sign read
    right for once ("I have seen with my own eyes the shots of
    their best man crippled by Zeus — easily known among men is
    the strength of Zeus"); then the lines Lycurgus read out to an
    Athenian jury in 330 BC (Against Leocrates 103), prosecuting a
    man who fled the city after Chaeronea: "whichever of you...
    meets his death and his doom — let him die. It is no dishonor
    for him to die defending his fatherland: his wife is safe
    after him, and his children, his house and his portion of land
    unharmed." The patriot-death ethic of 12.243 completed with
    its economic clause — what the dying man's death purchases,
    itemized. That the poem gives this creed to Hector, whose
    wife will not be safe, whose child will not live, whose city
    burns in every listener's knowledge, is the deepest irony the
    Iliad ever builds into a speech; and it was still doing civic
    work in a law-court three centuries later.

[26] (15.502-514) Ajax's counter-speech strips the same choice of
    all its consolations: "either we perish or we are saved"; the
    savage joke ("It is not to a dance he is inviting them"); and
    the preference — "Better once for all to seize our death or
    our life than be squeezed slowly dry... under worse men than
    ourselves." Where Hector offers death a meaning, Ajax offers
    only the arithmetic of now or slowly: the defender's creed,
    with no city behind it (completed at 733-742, note [36]).

[27] (15.525-539) Dolops, son of Lampus — grandson of Laomedon:
    even the Trojan royal cousins now feed the ships' battle. His
    death turns on heirloom armor: the corslet Phyleus brought
    out of Ephyra, guest-gift of Euphetes, "and now it kept
    destruction from the flesh of his son" — a cuirass with a
    passport, protecting the second generation (cf. the boar's-
    tusk helmet, 10.261-271). The plume Meges shears falls "fresh-
    shining with its scarlet dye" — the ornament dies first; then
    Menelaus, "unseen," finishes the man from behind.

[28] (15.547-559) Melanippus of Percote: the cattle-herding cousin
    who came back when the ships came — lines 550-551 repeat the
    Imbrius formula (13.175-176) verbatim, our English kept: the
    poem has a fixed biography for Troy's returned provincials,
    honored "like his own children" by Priam and spent like coin
    by Hector, whose rebuke ends on the naked alternative: "now we
    kill them, or they pull down steep Ilion from its height and
    cut her citizens down."

[29] (15.561-565) Ajax's shame-couplet repeats 5.531-532 (our
    English kept): "Where men feel shame, more are saved than
    slain; where they run, no fame rises, and no strength." Once
    Agamemnon's marshaling wisdom, now the last doctrine of a
    shrinking perimeter — αἰδώς as the army's remaining wall.

[30] (15.579-591) Antilochus springs like a hound on a shot fawn,
    then flees Hector "like a wild beast that has done some
    mischief — that has killed a dog, or a herdsman with his
    cattle, and runs before the crowd of men can gather." The
    guilty-animal simile is unique — predation with a conscience
    attached — and it fits the poem's tenderest young fighter:
    Antilochus kills, knows the price of the field instantly, and
    is standing again the moment he reaches his friends.

[31] (15.592-615) The narrator's double disclosure. Zeus drives
    everything toward one image: "to see with his own eyes the
    flare of a single burning ship" — Thetis's "extraordinary
    prayer" made a visual trigger; from that flare the tide
    reverses. Then over Hector — foam at his mouth, eyes blazing
    under grim brows — the lines Zenodotus cut and Aristarchus
    athetized: Zeus honored him "for he was to be short-lived:
    already Pallas Athena was speeding his death-day on, under the
    strength of Peleus' son." Glory as hospice-gift: every act of
    Hector's greatest hour is performed inside a sentence that has
    already ended him. The ancients found the disclosure
    intolerable; it is the poem's whole method — nothing is
    suspenseful, everything is witnessed.

[32] (15.630-652) The lion takes one cow from the middle of
    thousands: Periphetes of Mycenae — son of Copreus ("Dung-man"),
    Eurystheus's herald to Heracles, "of that far worse father was
    born a better son in every virtue." The Heracles-cycle
    surfaces as family shame redeemed one generation on — and then
    the poem's most pitilessly ordinary death: he trips backward
    over his own shield-rim, "the shield he carried himself,
    reaching his feet, a tower against javelins," and Hector spears
    him where he lies tangled in his own protection. The best
    equipment in the panic is the thing that kills him; his
    friends watch, too afraid to help.

[33] (15.653-667) The last line before the huts. Nestor "begged
    each man by his parents, clasping their knees with his words"
    — supplication conducted by proxy, at range: "remember, each
    of you, your children and your wives, your property and your
    parents — you whose parents live, and you whose parents are
    dead: for their sake, absent though they are, I clasp your
    knees here." The absent invoked as the army's true country
    (Ajax will say the country itself is absent, note [36]); and
    Athena lifts the mist so that they can at least see the enemy
    that is upon them — the poem's recurring mercy of light
    (17.646-647).

[34] (15.674-688) Ajax's pike-walk: deck to deck with the twenty-
    two-cubit boarding pike, "as a man well skilled in trick-
    riding... yoked together four horses... steady, sure, and
    always leaping, changes from horse to horse as they fly
    along." The one extended image of equestrian spectacle in the
    poem — public road, admiring crowd, the city ahead — laid
    over a man hopping burning-threatened hulls with a siege-pole:
    peacetime's showman as war's last acrobat.

[35] (15.704-726) Hector's hand closes on the stern of the ship
    "that had carried Protesilaus to Troy, and never carried him
    home again": the war's first casualty (2.698-702) supplying
    the altar for its crisis — the fleet's mortality concentrated
    in one hull. Hector's speech from the stern-horn rewrites his
    own history — the elders' "cowardice" held him back (it was
    Polydamas's prudence, and his own obedience) — and then
    produces the poem's most convenient theology: "if wide-seeing
    Zeus was damaging our wits then, now he himself is driving
    and commanding us." Both clauses are true; neither means what
    he thinks. The day Zeus gave him is real, and expires at
    sundown (11.192-194); "bring fire!" is the exact fulfillment,
    and the exact limit, of his warrant.

[36] (15.727-746) The last stand, measured in carpentry: Ajax
    gives back from the deck to "the seven-foot bridge-plank" —
    θρῆνυς ἑπταπόδης, the cross-bench of a beached hull as the
    war's entire remaining ground. His speech is the reply to
    Hector's fatherland-creed (note [25]) from men who have none
    in reach: "Do we imagine there are allies at our backs? Some
    stronger wall?... There is no city near us fitted with towers
    ... We sit in the plain of the close-armored Trojans, pinned
    against the sea, far from our fatherland. Our light of safety
    is in our hands — not in any gentleness of war." τὼ ἐν χερσὶ
    φόως: salvation as a thing held, like a spear. Then the book's
    last sentence does the accounting its hero would approve:
    "twelve men he wounded close-in before the ships." No simile,
    no god, no glory-word — a count. The fire is one torch away,
    and Book 16 opens with Patroclus weeping in front of Achilles.
