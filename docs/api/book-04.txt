BOOK 4

The truce broken: Pandarus's arrow, the review of the troops, and the
first battle of the poem.

TRANSLATOR'S COMMENTARY

Book 4 contains the poem's moral pivot — the gods deliberately engineer the
breaking of a sworn truce — and its first battle. The divine council that
opens the book is the darkest divine politics in the Iliad: Zeus proposes
peace (perhaps in earnest, perhaps as bait), Hera offers him her own three
dearest cities, Argos, Sparta, and Mycenae, to destroy at will in exchange
for Troy's ruin, and Zeus concedes with the unforgettable "willingly — with
unwilling heart." Aristarchus athetized the sharpest couplet of Hera's
speech (55-56; note [3]), but the transaction stands in the text: the war
resumes because two gods trade cities like counters. I have translated the
scene coldly, without softening the horse-trading tone, because the
Pandarus scene that follows — Athena talking a fool into perjury his whole
people will pay for — is its direct execution.

This is also the book where the poem's combat idiom begins, and the
conventions set here bind the remaining twenty books (all logged in
translation/CONVENTIONS.md): death formulas rendered fixed and unvarying
("darkness covered his eyes"; "he fell with a thud, and his armor clattered
upon him"; "unstrung his limbs"), wound anatomy kept clinically precise as
the Greek keeps it, and the armor vocabulary settled (ζωστήρ "war-belt,"
ζῶμα "loin-guard," μίτρη "waist-guard"). The first man to die in the Iliad
is Echepolus at 457 (note [22]); the first extended pathos-vignette is
Simoeisios at 473-489 (note [23]), the young man felled like a poplar cut
for a chariot-wheel, who "never paid back the cost of his rearing" — the
template for a hundred deaths to come.

Agamemnon's inspection tour (the epipōlēsis, 223-421) is the book's long
character study: honeyed words for Idomeneus and the Ajaxes, wistful
respect for Nestor, and gratuitous insults for Odysseus and Diomedes —
both unearned, both answered in ways that define each man (Odysseus with
hot dignity, Diomedes with a silence that will pay out in Book 9). The
rebuke of Diomedes drags in the Theban saga (notes [18], [19]) — the
Iliad's fullest glance at the war of the previous generation, and
Sthenelus's retort ("we took seven-gated Thebes; our fathers died of
their own recklessness") was athetized by Aristarchus, perhaps for its
flat contradiction of Agamemnon's point.

   1  Now the gods, seated beside Zeus, were holding assembly
   2  on the golden floor, and among them the lady Hebe
   3  poured nectar; and they with golden cups
   4  pledged one another, looking out at the city of the Trojans.
   5  And at once the son of Cronos set about provoking Hera
   6  with needling words, speaking sidelong:
   7  "Two of the goddesses are helpers of Menelaus —
   8  Hera of Argos and Athena of Alalcomenae —[1]
   9  and yet the two of them sit apart, looking on,
  10  taking their pleasure; while laughter-loving Aphrodite
  11  is forever at that other's side, and keeps the fates of death from him:
  12  just now she saved him when he thought to die.
  13  Still — victory belongs to Menelaus dear to Ares.
  14  So let us consider how these things shall be:
  15  whether we shall stir up evil war again and the dread din
  16  of battle, or cast friendship between the two sides.
  17  If somehow this should be welcome and sweet to all,
  18  then let the city of lord Priam stay a place men live in,
  19  and let Menelaus take Argive Helen home again."
  20  So he spoke, and they murmured at it, Athena and Hera,
  21  who sat side by side plotting evils for the Trojans.
  22  Athena, to be sure, stayed silent and said nothing,
  23  sulking at father Zeus, wild anger gripping her;
  24  but Hera's breast could not contain her anger, and she spoke:
  25  "Most dread son of Cronos, what a word you have spoken!
  26  How can you wish to make my labor vain and unaccomplished —
  27  the sweat I sweated in my toil, my horses worn out
  28  gathering the host, to bring evils on Priam and his sons?
  29  Do it. But we other gods do not all approve it."
  30  Greatly troubled, Zeus the cloud-gatherer answered her:
  31  "Possessed one — what great evils do Priam and Priam's sons
  32  do you, that you rage without respite
  33  to sack the well-built citadel of Ilion?
  34  If you could go in through the gates and the long walls
  35  and eat Priam raw, and Priam's sons,[2]
  36  and the other Trojans too — then you might cure your anger.
  37  Do as you please. Only let not this quarrel
  38  become in time a great strife between you and me.
  39  And I will tell you another thing — put it away in your heart:
  40  whenever I in my turn am bent on sacking a city,
  41  and choose one where men dear to you were born,
  42  do not try to delay my anger, but let me be:
  43  for I too have given you this willingly — with unwilling heart.
  44  Of all the cities under the sun and the starry sky
  45  that men who dwell on the earth inhabit,
  46  holy Ilion was most honored in my heart,
  47  and Priam, and the people of Priam of the good ash spear.
  48  Never did my altar lack its fair share of the feast,
  49  the libation and the savor: for that is the honor-portion we gods have."
  50  Then the ox-eyed lady Hera answered him:
  51  "Three cities there are far dearest of all to me:
  52  Argos, and Sparta, and wide-wayed Mycenae.
  53  Sack these, whenever they grow hateful to your heart;
  54  I do not stand in front of them; I do not grudge them to you.
  55  For even if I did begrudge them, and refused you their sacking,
  56  my grudging would accomplish nothing: you are far the stronger.[3]
  57  But my labor too must not be made unaccomplished.
  58  I also am a god; my birth is from the same stock as yours;
  59  crooked-counseled Cronos begot me, eldest of his daughters —
  60  eldest doubly: by birth, and because I am called
  61  your wife, and you are king among all the immortals.
  62  Come — in this let us yield to one another,
  63  I to you and you to me, and the other immortal gods
  64  will follow. Quickly now: order Athena
  65  to go down into the dread din of Trojans and Achaeans
  66  and contrive that the Trojans be the first to do harm,
  67  against the oaths, to the Achaeans now in their glory."
  68  So she spoke, and the father of men and gods did not disobey:
  69  at once he addressed Athena with winged words:
  70  "Go with all speed to the armies, to the Trojans and Achaeans,
  71  and contrive that the Trojans be the first to do harm,
  72  against the oaths, to the Achaeans now in their glory."
  73  So speaking, he stirred Athena, already eager;
  74  and down from the peaks of Olympus she went darting.
  75  As the son of crooked-counseled Cronos sends a star,
  76  a portent to sailors or to the wide host of an army,
  77  blazing, and sparks stream from it in multitudes —
  78  in such a likeness Pallas Athena darted to earth
  79  and leapt down into the middle; and amazement held the watchers,
  80  the horse-taming Trojans and the strong-greaved Achaeans;
  81  and thus one would say, with a glance at his neighbor:
  82  "Either evil war and the dread din of battle
  83  are coming again — or Zeus is setting friendship
  84  between the two sides, Zeus, the dispenser of war to men."
  85  So one of the Achaeans and Trojans would say.
  86  And she, in the likeness of a man, went down into the Trojan throng —
  87  of Laodocus, Antenor's son, a strong spearman —
  88  searching for godlike Pandarus, if she might find him.
  89  She found Lycaon's son, blameless and strong,
  90  standing; and around him the strong ranks of shield-bearing
  91  men who had followed him from the streams of Aesepus.
  92  Standing close, she spoke winged words to him:
  93  "Would you let me persuade you, war-minded son of Lycaon?
  94  Would you dare send a swift arrow at Menelaus,
  95  and win favor and glory in the eyes of all the Trojans —
  96  of prince Alexandros most of all?
  97  From him first of anyone you would carry off shining gifts,
  98  if he should see Menelaus, Atreus' warlike son,
  99  brought down by your shaft and set on the grievous pyre.
 100  Come then — shoot an arrow at glorious Menelaus,
 101  and vow to Apollo the Lycian-born, famed for the bow,[4]
 102  to offer a splendid hecatomb of firstborn lambs
 103  when you come home to the town of holy Zeleia."
 104  So spoke Athena, and persuaded the fool's heart.
 105  At once he uncased his polished bow, made from the horn[5]
 106  of a leaping wild ibex which he himself had once shot
 107  under the chest as it stepped out from a rock — lying
 108  in wait in a blind, he struck it in the chest,
 109  and it fell back flat on the rock. From its head the horns
 110  had grown sixteen palms; and the craftsman, the horn-polisher,
 111  fitted them together, smoothed all smooth, and set on a tip of gold.
 112  This bow he strung, bending it well, and laid it down
 113  propped against the ground; and his good companions held their shields before him,
 114  so the warlike sons of the Achaeans should not spring up
 115  before Menelaus, Atreus' warlike son, was hit.
 116  Then he stripped the lid from his quiver and took out an arrow,
 117  never yet shot, winged, a base of black pains;[6]
 118  and quickly he set the bitter shaft in order on the string,
 119  and vowed to Apollo the Lycian-born, famed for the bow,
 120  to offer a splendid hecatomb of firstborn lambs
 121  when he came home to the town of holy Zeleia.
 122  Gripping together the notched butt and the string of ox-sinew, he drew:
 123  the string he brought to his breast, and to the bow the iron head.[7]
 124  And when he had strained the great bow into a circle,
 125  the horn sang, the string cried aloud, and the arrow leapt,
 126  sharp-pointed, raging to fly on into the crowd.
 127  But the blessed immortal gods did not forget you,
 128  Menelaus — first among them Zeus's daughter, the driver of the spoil,
 129  who stood before you and warded off the piercing shaft.
 130  She brushed it back from the skin just so far as a mother
 131  brushes a fly from her child when he lies in sweet sleep,
 132  and steered it herself to where the golden buckles[8]
 133  of the war-belt met and the corslet lay doubled.
 134  Into the fastened war-belt fell the bitter arrow:
 135  through the wrought belt it was driven,
 136  through the richly worked corslet it pressed on,
 137  and through the waist-guard he wore to shield his flesh, a fence against javelins —
 138  that guarded him best, yet it passed through even this;
 139  and the arrow grazed the outermost skin of the man,
 140  and at once dark-clouded blood ran down from the wound.
 141  As when some woman of Maeonia or Caria stains[9]
 142  ivory with crimson, to be a cheek-piece for horses;
 143  it lies in a treasure-room, and many horsemen
 144  have prayed to carry it; but it lies there, a king's treasure,
 145  both ornament for the horse and glory for the driver:
 146  so, Menelaus, your shapely thighs were stained
 147  with blood, your shins, and your fine ankles beneath.
 148  Then the lord of men Agamemnon shuddered
 149  as he saw the black blood running down from the wound,
 150  and Menelaus dear to Ares shuddered himself;
 151  but when he saw the binding-cord and the barbs still outside the flesh,
 152  the spirit was gathered back into his breast.
 153  But groaning heavily, the lord Agamemnon spoke among them,
 154  holding Menelaus by the hand, and the companions groaned with him:
 155  "Dear brother, it was your death I was cutting in those oaths,[10]
 156  setting you out alone before the Achaeans to fight the Trojans —
 157  now the Trojans have shot you down and trampled the sworn oaths.
 158  Yet not in vain are the oath, the blood of lambs,
 159  the unmixed libations, the right hands we trusted.
 160  For if the Olympian has not fulfilled it at once,
 161  he fulfills it late — and men pay greatly for it,
 162  with their own heads, and their wives, and their children.
 163  For this I know well in my mind and heart:
 164  the day will come when holy Ilion shall perish,
 165  and Priam, and the people of Priam of the good ash spear,
 166  and Zeus the son of Cronos, high-throned, dwelling in the sky,
 167  will himself shake his dark aegis over them all
 168  in anger at this deceit. These things will not go unaccomplished.
 169  But my grief for you will be terrible, O Menelaus,
 170  if you die and fill up your portion of life.
 171  I would go back to thirsty Argos in utter disgrace,
 172  for the Achaeans would think at once of their fatherland,
 173  and we would leave to Priam and the Trojans their boast —
 174  Argive Helen; and the field would rot your bones
 175  as you lay in Troy, the work unaccomplished.
 176  And thus some overweening Trojan will say,
 177  leaping on the tomb of glorious Menelaus:
 178  'May Agamemnon bring his anger to this end in every case —
 179  as now he led an Achaean army here for nothing,
 180  and has gone off home to his dear fatherland
 181  with empty ships, and left good Menelaus behind.'
 182  So some day one of them will say. Then let the wide earth gape for me."
 183  But tawny-haired Menelaus spoke to reassure him:
 184  "Take heart; do not frighten the Achaean host at all.
 185  The sharp shaft is not fixed in a mortal place: before that
 186  my glittering war-belt saved me, and beneath it
 187  the loin-guard, and the waist-guard bronze-smiths labored on."
 188  Answering him spoke the lord Agamemnon:
 189  "May it be so indeed, dear Menelaus.
 190  But a healer will probe the wound and lay on it
 191  drugs to stop the black pains."
 192  He spoke, and addressed Talthybius the godlike herald:
 193  "Talthybius — as quick as you can, call Machaon here,
 194  the man who is son of Asclepius the blameless healer,[11]
 195  to see Menelaus, Atreus' warlike son,[12]
 196  whom someone skilled in the bow has shot with an arrow,
 197  some Trojan or Lycian: glory for him, grief for us."
 198  So he spoke, and the herald heard and did not disobey.
 199  He went through the host of the bronze-shirted Achaeans
 200  peering for the hero Machaon; and he saw him
 201  standing, with the strong ranks of shield-bearing men
 202  around him, who had followed him from horse-pasturing Tricca.
 203  Standing close, he spoke winged words:
 204  "Rise, son of Asclepius. The lord Agamemnon calls,
 205  to see Menelaus, the warlike captain of the Achaeans,
 206  whom someone skilled in the bow has shot with an arrow,
 207  some Trojan or Lycian: glory for him, grief for us."
 208  So he spoke, and stirred the heart in his breast,
 209  and they went through the crowd, through the wide Achaean camp.
 210  And when they came where tawny-haired Menelaus
 211  was wounded — and around him all the best men were gathered
 212  in a circle, and he, a man like a god, stood among them —
 213  at once Machaon drew the arrow from the fastened belt;
 214  and as it was drawn out, the sharp barbs broke backward.
 215  Then he loosed the glittering war-belt, and beneath it
 216  the loin-guard, and the waist-guard bronze-smiths labored on.
 217  And when he saw the wound where the bitter arrow had struck,
 218  he sucked out the blood, and skillfully sprinkled on it
 219  soothing drugs, which Chiron once gave his father out of friendship.
 220  While they were busy about Menelaus, good at the war-cry,
 221  the ranks of the shield-bearing Trojans came on;
 222  and the Achaeans put on their armor again, and remembered their fighting.
 223  Then you would not have seen brilliant Agamemnon drowsing,[13]
 224  or cowering, or unwilling to fight,
 225  but hurrying hard toward the battle where men win glory.
 226  He left his horses and his chariot patterned with bronze —
 227  his aide kept them snorting off to the side,
 228  Eurymedon, son of Ptolemaeus, Peiraeus' son,
 229  with strict orders to keep them close at hand for the moment
 230  weariness should take his limbs, commanding through so many —
 231  and on foot he ranged the ranks of men.
 232  Whomever of the Danaans of swift colts he saw pressing on,
 233  he would stand beside them and hearten them with his words:
 234  "Argives, do not let go your rushing courage now!
 235  Father Zeus will be no helper to liars:
 236  those who were first to do harm against the oaths —
 237  vultures will eat their tender flesh,
 238  and we will carry off their dear wives and little children
 239  in our ships, when we take their citadel."
 240  But whomever he saw hanging back from hateful war,
 241  those he would rake with angry words:
 242  "Argives — loud boasters — disgraces! Have you no shame?[14]
 243  Why do you stand stunned like this, like fawns
 244  that have run themselves tired over a wide plain
 245  and stand still, with no courage left in their hearts —
 246  so you stand stunned, and do not fight.
 247  Are you waiting for the Trojans to come right up to where the ships
 248  with their fine sterns are drawn up on the shore of the grey sea,
 249  to find out whether the son of Cronos will hold his hand over you?"
 250  So, commanding, he ranged through the ranks of men,
 251  and came to the Cretans, moving through the crowd of fighters.
 252  They were arming around war-minded Idomeneus:
 253  Idomeneus among the front-fighters, like a wild boar in courage,
 254  while Meriones hurried on the rearmost companies.
 255  Seeing them the lord of men Agamemnon rejoiced,
 256  and at once he spoke to Idomeneus with honeyed words:
 257  "Idomeneus, I honor you beyond all the Danaans of swift colts,
 258  in war and in every other work,
 259  and at the feast, when the chief men of the Argives
 260  mix the elders' gleaming wine in the bowl.
 261  For even if the other long-haired Achaeans
 262  drink their allotted share, your cup stands always
 263  full, as mine does, to drink whenever the heart bids.
 264  Rouse yourself for battle, and be the man you claim of old to be."
 265  And Idomeneus, captain of the Cretans, answered him:
 266  "Son of Atreus, I will be a trusty companion to you
 267  indeed, as I first promised and bowed my head;
 268  go and rouse the other long-haired Achaeans,
 269  so we may fight at once — since the Trojans have poured away
 270  the oaths. Death and sorrows hereafter will be theirs,
 271  since they were first to do harm against the oaths."
 272  So he spoke, and Atreus' son passed on, glad at heart,
 273  and came to the two Ajaxes, moving through the crowd of fighters;
 274  they were arming for war, and a cloud of foot-soldiers followed them.
 275  As when from a lookout a goatherd sees a cloud
 276  coming over the deep before the drive of the West Wind:
 277  from far off, blacker than pitch it shows,
 278  moving over the deep, and it brings a great storm with it;
 279  he shivers at the sight, and drives his flocks into a cave:
 280  such, beside the two Ajaxes, moved the close-packed companies
 281  of Zeus-nurtured fighting men, dark masses,
 282  into destroying war, bristling with shields and spears.
 283  Seeing them too the lord Agamemnon rejoiced,
 284  and speaking winged words he addressed them:
 285  "Ajaxes, leaders of the bronze-shirted Argives —
 286  to you two I give no orders: it would not be right to push you;
 287  you yourselves drive your people hard to fight in strength.
 288  O father Zeus, Athena, Apollo —
 289  if such a spirit were in every breast,
 290  then the city of lord Priam would soon bow its head,
 291  taken and sacked beneath our hands."
 292  So speaking he left them there and went to the others.
 293  There he came on Nestor, the clear-voiced speaker of Pylos,
 294  arraying his companions and driving them to fight,
 295  around tall Pelagon, and Alastor, and Chromius,
 296  and the lord Haemon, and Bias, shepherd of the people.
 297  The charioteers he arrayed in front with horses and cars,[15]
 298  the foot-soldiers behind, many and brave,
 299  to be a wall of war; the shirkers he drove into the middle,
 300  so that even the unwilling man would have to fight.
 301  To the charioteers he gave his orders first: he bade them
 302  hold their horses in hand and not tangle in the crowd:
 303  "Let no man, trusting his horsemanship and his manhood,
 304  burn to fight the Trojans alone out in front of the rest,
 305  and let no man fall back — you will be the weaker for it.
 306  But the man who from his own car can reach an enemy's,
 307  let him thrust with the spear: it is far better so.
 308  This was the mind the men before us had when they sacked
 309  cities and walls, keeping this thought and spirit in their breasts."
 310  So the old man drove them on, wise in war long since.
 311  Seeing him too the lord Agamemnon rejoiced,
 312  and speaking winged words he addressed him:
 313  "Old man — would that, as the spirit is in your own breast,
 314  so your knees kept pace, and your strength stood firm.
 315  But age wears you, age that comes to all. If only
 316  some other man had your years, and you were among the young."
 317  Then answered him the Gerenian horseman Nestor:
 318  "Son of Atreus, I too could dearly wish to be
 319  the man I was when I cut down brilliant Ereuthalion.
 320  But the gods never give men everything at once:[16]
 321  if I was young then, now old age comes with me.
 322  Even so I will be among the charioteers, and command them
 323  with counsel and with words: that is the honor-portion of the old.
 324  The spears the younger men will wield, who were born
 325  after me and trust in their strength."
 326  So he spoke, and Atreus' son passed on, glad at heart.
 327  He found Menestheus, driver of horses, son of Peteos,
 328  standing; and around him the Athenians, masters of the war-cry;
 329  and close by stood resourceful Odysseus,
 330  and beside him the ranks of Cephallenians, no weaklings,
 331  stood; for their people had not yet heard the war-cry,
 332  since the companies of horse-taming Trojans and Achaeans
 333  had only just begun to move; and they stood waiting
 334  for another Achaean column to come on,
 335  charge the Trojans, and begin the war.
 336  Seeing them, the lord of men Agamemnon raked them,
 337  and speaking winged words he addressed them:
 338  "You — son of Peteos, that Zeus-nurtured king;
 339  and you, master of evil tricks, greedy-hearted:
 340  why do you stand apart cowering, and wait for others?
 341  It is for you two to stand among the first
 342  and take your share of scorching battle face on;
 343  for you are first to hear of my feast
 344  whenever we Achaeans set out a feast for the elders.
 345  There it pleases you to eat the roasted meat and drink
 346  the cups of honey-sweet wine as long as you wish;
 347  but now you would gladly watch even if ten columns of Achaeans
 348  fought with the pitiless bronze out in front of you."
 349  Glaring at him darkly, resourceful Odysseus answered:
 350  "Son of Atreus — what a word has escaped the fence of your teeth![17]
 351  How can you say I hang back from fighting, whenever we Achaeans
 352  wake sharp war on the horse-taming Trojans?
 353  You will see — if you wish to, and if it matters to you —
 354  the dear father of Telemachus mixing with the front-fighters
 355  of the horse-taming Trojans. What you say is empty wind."
 356  And the lord Agamemnon answered him, smiling,
 357  when he saw his anger, and took back his word:
 358  "Zeus-sprung son of Laertes, Odysseus of many devices,
 359  I neither scold you beyond measure nor give you orders.
 360  I know the heart in your breast
 361  knows gentle counsels: you think what I think.
 362  Come — we will make this good later, if any harsh thing
 363  has been said now; may the gods make it all come to nothing."
 364  So speaking he left them there and went to the others.
 365  He found Tydeus' son, high-hearted Diomedes,
 366  standing among the horses and the bolted chariots,
 367  and beside him stood Sthenelus, Capaneus' son.
 368  Seeing him too the lord Agamemnon raked him,
 369  and speaking winged words he addressed him:
 370  "Ah me — son of war-minded Tydeus, breaker of horses:[18]
 371  why do you cower? Why do you eye the causeways of battle?
 372  It was not Tydeus' way to cower like this,
 373  but to fight the enemy far ahead of his own companions —
 374  so said those who watched him at his work; I myself
 375  never met him, never saw him; but they say he was beyond all others.
 376  Once, indeed, he came to Mycenae — no war in it —
 377  a guest, with godlike Polyneices, raising troops:
 378  they were then marching on the holy walls of Thebes,
 379  and they begged us hard to give them famed allies;
 380  and our people were willing, and approved what they asked,
 381  but Zeus turned them back with unfavorable signs.
 382  So they went on their way, and when they were well along the road
 383  and came to the Asopus, deep in rushes, bedded in grass,
 384  the Achaeans sent Tydeus forward with a message.
 385  He went, and found the Cadmeans in their numbers
 386  feasting through the house of the might of Eteocles.
 387  There, stranger though he was, the horseman Tydeus
 388  was not afraid, alone among so many Cadmeans:
 389  he challenged them to contests, and won every one
 390  with ease — such a helper to him was Athena.
 391  But the Cadmeans, goaders of horses, were angered,
 392  and as he rode back they set a close-packed ambush,
 393  fifty young men, with two leaders:
 394  Maeon, Haemon's son, a match for the immortals,
 395  and Autophonus' son, Polyphontes steadfast in battle.
 396  On these too Tydeus let loose an unseemly fate:
 397  he killed them all, and sent one man alone home —
 398  Maeon he sent off, obeying the gods' portents.
 399  Such a man was Tydeus the Aetolian. But the son
 400  he fathered is worse than he in battle — better in the assembly."
 401  So he spoke, and strong Diomedes said nothing at all,
 402  humbled by the reverend king's rebuke.
 403  But the son of glorious Capaneus answered him:
 404  "Son of Atreus, do not lie when you know how to speak the truth.[19]
 405  We claim to be far better men than our fathers:
 406  we took the seat of seven-gated Thebes,
 407  though we led a smaller host beneath a stronger wall,
 408  because we trusted the gods' portents and the help of Zeus;
 409  they perished by their own recklessness.
 410  So never set our fathers in honor equal to ours."
 411  Glaring at him darkly, strong Diomedes answered:
 412  "Old friend — sit quiet, and listen to my word.[20]
 413  I hold nothing against Agamemnon, shepherd of the people,
 414  for driving the strong-greaved Achaeans to fight:
 415  the glory will follow him if the Achaeans
 416  cut down the Trojans and take holy Ilion —
 417  and his the great grief if the Achaeans are cut down.
 418  Come — let the two of us also remember our rushing courage."
 419  He spoke, and leapt from the chariot to the ground in his armor,
 420  and the bronze rang terribly on the chest of the lord
 421  as he sprang: fear would have seized even a stone-hearted man.
 422  As when on a booming beach the swell of the sea
 423  rises, wave on wave, under the West Wind's driving:
 424  out on the deep it first rears its crest, and then
 425  breaking on the land it thunders aloud, and around the headlands
 426  it arches and comes to a peak and spits out the salt foam:
 427  so wave on wave the companies of the Danaans moved
 428  without pause into war; and each captain called commands
 429  to his own; and the rest went silently — you would not think
 430  so great a host was following with voices in their chests —
 431  in silence, fearing their commanders; and on all of them
 432  the patterned armor shone as they marched in order.
 433  But the Trojans — as ewes past counting stand in the fold
 434  of a man of much substance while their white milk is drawn,
 435  bleating without cease because they hear the voices of their lambs:
 436  so the clamor of the Trojans rose through the wide army;
 437  for their speech was not one, their cry not a single tongue,
 438  but language mixed with language, men called from many lands.
 439  These Ares drove on, and those grey-eyed Athena,
 440  and Terror, and Panic, and Strife insatiably raging,[21]
 441  sister and companion of man-slaughtering Ares —
 442  she who rears her crest small at first, but soon
 443  props her head against heaven while she walks the earth.
 444  She then cast common hatred into their midst,
 445  moving through the crowd, swelling the groaning of men.
 446  And when they had come together into one place,
 447  they dashed together shields, and spears, and the fury of men
 448  armored in bronze; the bossed shields
 449  pressed hard on one another, and a great din arose.
 450  There rose together the groaning and the boasting of men
 451  killing and killed, and the earth ran with blood.
 452  As when winter torrents pouring down the mountains
 453  hurl together their weight of water at a meeting of glens
 454  out of great springs, deep in a hollow ravine,
 455  and far off in the mountains a shepherd hears the thunder of it:
 456  such was the roar and the toil of them as they came together.
 457  First Antilochus took a Trojan armored fighter,[22]
 458  a brave man among the front-fighters, Echepolus, Thalysias' son:
 459  he struck him first on the ridge of his horsehair helmet,
 460  and the bronze point fixed in his forehead and drove
 461  inside the bone; and darkness covered his eyes,
 462  and he crashed like a tower in the strong encounter.
 463  As he fell, the lord Elephenor took him by the feet —
 464  Chalcodon's son, chief of the great-hearted Abantes —
 465  and dragged him out from under the missiles, straining to strip
 466  his armor with all speed. But his effort was short-lived:
 467  great-hearted Agenor saw him dragging the corpse,
 468  and where his side showed from under the shield as he bent,
 469  he stabbed him with the bronze-pointed spear, and unstrung his limbs.
 470  So the spirit left him; and over his body the work
 471  of Trojans and Achaeans grew grim: like wolves
 472  they leapt at one another, and man tore at man.
 473  Then Telamonian Ajax struck Anthemion's son,[23]
 474  the young man Simoeisios in his bloom, whom once his mother
 475  bore beside the banks of Simois, coming down
 476  from Ida, where she had followed her parents to see the flocks:
 477  for that they called him Simoeisios. But to his dear parents
 478  he never paid back the cost of his rearing; his life was short,
 479  broken under the spear of great-hearted Ajax.
 480  For as he came on in the front, Ajax struck him in the chest
 481  by the right nipple, and straight through his shoulder
 482  the bronze spear came; and he fell to the ground in the dust
 483  like a poplar that has grown up in the wet meadow
 484  of a great marsh — smooth, with the branches growing
 485  at its very top — and a chariot-maker with the bright iron
 486  fells it, to bend a wheel-rim for a beautiful car,
 487  and it lies there seasoning along the river bank:
 488  such was Anthemion's son Simoeisios, whom Ajax
 489  the Zeus-sprung killed. And at him Antiphos of the glancing corslet,
 490  Priam's son, cast his sharp spear through the crowd:
 491  he missed him, but struck Leucus, Odysseus' brave companion,
 492  in the groin as he dragged the corpse to the other side,
 493  and he collapsed over it, and the body fell from his hand.
 494  For his killing Odysseus was enraged to the heart,
 495  and he strode through the front-fighters helmed in flashing bronze,
 496  and came up very close and cast his shining spear,
 497  glancing all around him; and the Trojans shrank back
 498  from the man as he cast. He did not send the shaft in vain,
 499  but struck Democoon, Priam's bastard son,
 500  who had come from Abydos, from the fast mares:
 501  him Odysseus, enraged for his companion, struck with the spear
 502  in the temple, and out through the other temple passed
 503  the bronze point; and darkness covered his eyes,
 504  and he fell with a thud, and his armor clattered upon him.
 505  Then the front-fighters gave ground, and glorious Hector too;
 506  and the Argives shouted aloud and dragged away the corpses
 507  and pressed far forward. But Apollo took offense,
 508  looking down from Pergamos, and called aloud to the Trojans:
 509  "Rouse yourselves, horse-taming Trojans! Do not yield the fight
 510  to the Argives: their flesh is not stone, not iron,
 511  to hold off the flesh-cutting bronze when they are hit.
 512  And Achilles, son of lovely-haired Thetis, is not
 513  fighting: by the ships he digests his heart-paining anger."
 514  So spoke the dread god from the city. And the Achaeans
 515  were urged on by Zeus's daughter, most glorious Tritogeneia,[24]
 516  moving through the crowd wherever she saw them slacken.
 517  Then fate shackled Diores, Amarynceus' son:
 518  he was struck by a jagged stone beside the ankle,
 519  in his right shin, and the thrower was the captain of the Thracians,
 520  Peiroos, Imbrasus' son, who had come from Aenus.
 521  Both tendons and the bones the shameless stone
 522  crushed utterly; and he fell back in the dust,
 523  stretching out both hands to his dear companions,
 524  gasping out his life; and the man who hit him ran up,
 525  Peiroos, and stabbed him with the spear beside the navel; and all
 526  his bowels poured out on the ground, and darkness covered his eyes.
 527  But as Peiroos sprang back, Thoas the Aetolian struck him
 528  with a spear in the chest above the nipple, and the bronze fixed in his lung;
 529  and Thoas came up close, wrenched the heavy spear
 530  out of his chest, and drew his sharp sword,
 531  and struck him full in the belly, and took his life.
 532  But he did not strip his armor: his companions stood round him,
 533  the Thracians with their hair in topknots, long spears in their hands,
 534  who, big and strong and proud as he was,
 535  thrust him back from them; and he gave ground, staggering.
 536  So the two lay stretched in the dust beside each other,
 537  captains, one of the Thracians, one of the bronze-shirted
 538  Epeians; and around them many others were killed.
 539  Then no man could have come and made light of the work —[25]
 540  no man still unhit, unstabbed by the sharp bronze,
 541  who whirled through the middle of it, with Pallas Athena leading him
 542  by the hand and turning aside the rush of missiles:
 543  for many Trojans and Achaeans on that day
 544  were stretched face-down in the dust beside one another.

Notes

[1] (4.8) "Hera of Argos and Athena of Alalcomenae" — cult titles tying
    each goddess to a major sanctuary (the Argive Heraion; Alalcomenae
    in Boeotia). Zeus needles them by their official addresses, like
    reading their titles off a temple inscription.

[2] (4.34-36) "eat Priam raw" — the extremity of hatred in the poem's
    idiom; the image recurs at its darkest moments (Achilles to Hector,
    22.346-347; Hecuba on Achilles, 24.212). Here it is Zeus's sardonic
    measure of Hera's appetite.

[3] (4.51-56) Hera offers Zeus her own three dearest cities — Argos,
    Sparta, Mycenae, the great centers of her cult — to destroy at his
    pleasure, as the price of Troy. Aristarchus athetized 55-56
    (apparatus, Line 55-56: ath. Ar), where she concedes that resistance
    would be futile anyway. The bargain frames the war's resumption as a
    cold divine transaction; note Zeus's matching concession at 43,
    "willingly — with unwilling heart" (ἑκὼν ἀέκοντί γε θυμῷ), the
    poem's most compressed oxymoron.

[4] (4.101) "Apollo the Lycian-born" (Λυκηγενής) — disputed already in
    antiquity: "born in Lycia" (Pandarus's men are Lycians of the Troad)
    or "wolf-born" (λύκος). The traditional rendering is kept. This is
    the same Pandarus whose bow "Apollo himself gave" (2.827) — the god
    of archery presides, with grim irony, over the poem's most disastrous
    bowshot.

[5] (4.105-111) The bow: horns of a wild ibex Pandarus shot himself,
    sixteen palms (about four feet) of horn, joined and gold-tipped by a
    craftsman. The loving technical excursus at the moment of treachery
    is a signature Homeric effect — the made thing described while its
    use damns its user.

[6] (4.117) Aristarchus athetized this line ("never yet shot, winged, a
    base of black pains"), as he did 140 and 149 in the same scene
    (apparatus) — ornamental lines the tradition found dispensable.
    All are kept as printed.

[7] (4.123) "to the bow the iron head" — one of the poem's rare iron
    weapons: arms in the Iliad are bronze, and iron appears mainly as
    raw wealth or for tools (the chariot-maker's axe at 485). The
    arrowhead here and the mace of Areithous (7.141) are the notable
    exceptions — often taken as glimpses of the poet's own later age.

[8] (4.132-138) The arrow passes through three layers: the ζωστήρ
    ("war-belt," the outer clasped belt), the doubled corslet, and the
    μίτρη ("waist-guard," a bronze-faced apron beneath). These renderings
    are fixed for the rest of the translation; the ζῶμα ("loin-guard")
    appears at 187.

[9] (4.141-147) The ivory simile: a Maeonian or Carian craftswoman
    staining ivory crimson for a horse's cheek-piece — a treasure "many
    horsemen prayed to carry." Blood on Menelaus's thighs becomes
    luxury inlay: the poem's first great aestheticizing of a wound, and
    a deliberate shock after the fly-brushing tenderness of 130-131.

[10] (4.155) "it was your death I was cutting in those oaths" — the
     oath-cutting idiom (see Book 3 commentary) turned bitter:
     Agamemnon hears the ritual he performed as a throat-cutting of
     his own brother.

[11] (4.194, 219) Asclepius is in Homer a mortal hero-physician, taught
     by Chiron the centaur (the wise counter-image of the beast-men of
     1.268) — not yet the god of healing of later cult. His sons Machaon
     and Podaleirius lead a contingent (2.731-732); Machaon's treatment
     — extraction, sucking the wound, herbal dressings — is the poem's
     standard battlefield medicine.

[12] (4.195-197) These lines recur verbatim at 205-207 when Talthybius
     delivers the message. Aristarchus athetized the first occurrence
     (apparatus, Line 195-197: ath. hic Ar. (= 205-207)) — a doctrinaire
     application of his dislike of repetition; the messenger-repetition
     is normal epic technique (cf. Book 2, note [4]). Both passages are
     translated, identically.

[13] (4.223-421) The epipōlēsis ("review"): Agamemnon on foot down the
     line, praising Idomeneus, the Ajaxes, and Nestor, insulting
     Menestheus, Odysseus, and Diomedes. The scene is a portrait
     gallery — and a study of Agamemnon's leadership, whose goads are
     as ill-judged as his flatteries are smooth.

[14] (4.242) "loud boasters" (ἰόμωροι) — obscure insult, already opaque
     in antiquity: perhaps "braggarts with arrows" (fighting from range,
     not in the line), perhaps just "loudmouths." The translation keeps
     the taunt and this note keeps the doubt.

[15] (4.297-309) Nestor's tactics — chariots massed in front, infantry
     behind, shirkers penned in the middle, with a doctrine of chariot-
     line fighting "the men before us" used — describe a kind of massed
     chariot warfare that the poem's actual battles never show (Homeric
     chariots are battle-taxis). Either a memory of older warfare or
     the old man's idealization of it; the poem lets Nestor be the
     keeper of both.

[16] (4.320) "the gods never give men everything at once" — athetized
     by Aristarchus (apparatus; cf. the similar 13.729). Ereuthalion
     (319) is the club-wielding champion Nestor killed in his youth —
     the full story is saved for 7.136-156.

[17] (4.350) "what a word has escaped the fence of your teeth" (ἕρκος
     ὀδόντων) — the fixed formula for speech that should have been kept
     in; always so rendered.

[18] (4.370-400) Agamemnon's Tydeus-tale reaches back to the Theban
     war: Tydeus and Polyneices recruiting at Mycenae, the embassy to
     Thebes, the athletic challenges, the fifty-man ambush he wiped out.
     This is the Iliad's fullest window on the Theban saga — the great
     rival epic cycle — deployed, characteristically, as a father held
     up against a son. "The might of Eteocles" (386) is the same
     periphrasis pattern as "the might of Heracles" (2.658).

[19] (4.404-410) Sthenelus's retort: the Epigoni — the sons of the
     Seven — actually took Thebes, which their fathers failed to do and
     died trying, "by their own recklessness" (ἀτασθαλίῃσιν, the
     Odyssey's opening moral keyword). Aristarchus athetized 407-409
     (apparatus, Line 407-409: ath. Ar). Kept: the exchange sets up
     Diomedes' pointed contrast.

[20] (4.412) "Old friend" (τέττα) — an affectionate, untranslatable
     address found only here. Diomedes silences Sthenelus and absorbs
     the insult out of respect for the king's office — restraint he
     will pointedly recall when he answers Agamemnon back at 9.32-49.
     The moment defines him: youngest of the great captains and the
     most self-governed.

[21] (4.440-443) Terror (Δεῖμος), Panic (Φόβος), and Strife (Ἔρις) —
     war's demons personified; Strife "small at first" but growing to
     prop her head on heaven is the ancestor of Virgil's Fama. Her
     epithet "insatiable" earns itself over the next twenty books.

[22] (4.457-462) The first death of the Iliad: Antilochus (Nestor's
     son) kills Echepolus. The kill establishes the death-formulas this
     translation keeps fixed from here on: "darkness covered his eyes,"
     "he fell with a thud, and his armor clattered upon him," "unstrung
     his limbs," "his spirit left him." Their unvarying recurrence is
     the poem's own drumbeat and is preserved, never elegantly varied.

[23] (4.473-489) Simoeisios: named for the river Simois where his
     mother bore him, killed in his first bloom, who "never paid back
     the cost of his rearing" (θρέπτρα — the son's debt of care to his
     parents), falling like a poplar cut down to be a chariot's
     wheel-rim. The first full pathos-vignette of the battle books:
     an entire peacetime life conjured at the instant of its ending,
     the template for the poem's way of killing men.

[24] (4.515) "Tritogeneia" — ancient title of Athena, of unknown
     meaning even to the ancients (connected variously with lake
     Tritonis, the river Triton, or "third-born"). Left as a name,
     like Atrytone (2.157).

[25] (4.539-544) The book closes with an imagined observer led through
     the carnage by Athena, untouched and turning missiles aside —
     only under those impossible conditions could a man "make light of
     the work." The narrator's device measures the day: many Trojans
     and Achaeans "stretched face-down in the dust beside one another."
