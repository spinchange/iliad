BOOK 12

The battle at the wall: the Trojans storm it on foot, Sarpedon states
the heroic contract, Hector overrules the omen, and the gate goes
down.

TRANSLATOR'S COMMENTARY

The shortest book of the Iliad so far translated is also its most
tightly built: a single military action — the storming of the Achaean
wall — organized as a sequence of thresholds: ditch, gates,
breastwork, bar; and at each threshold, a debate. Polydamas counsels
leaving the chariots, and Hector accepts; Asius refuses, and the
narrator files his death-notice in advance (note [7]); Polydamas
reads the eagle-and-snake omen and counsels retreat, and Hector
answers with the most quoted line of the poem — "One bird-sign is
best: to fight for your fatherland" (243) — an answer that is
magnificent, politically immortal, and wrong (note [12]). The book's
crown is Sarpedon's speech to Glaucus (310-328), the classic
statement of why kings fight — honor received obliges honor paid,
and since death comes anyway, the only choice left is the manner —
the exact contract whose repudiation by Achilles in Book 9 the poem
has already staged (note [19]).

The opening is unlike anything else in Homer: the narrator steps out
of the story's time to tell how, after the war, Poseidon and Apollo
turned eight rivers against the wall and erased it from the beach
(3-35) — epic covering its own tracks, since no trace of the wall
survived to be seen; the passage contains the poem's only "race of
half-god men," the heroes seen for once from the far side of the
gulf that separates them from the audience (note [2]). Equally
unusual is the narrator's cry of overload at 176 — "hard for me to
tell it all through, as though I were a god" — which ancient critics
athetized, perhaps for its very humanity (note [10]).

Throughout, the poet measures the widest war by the smallest peace:
stones fall like snow muffling the works of men (note [16]); the
deadlock is two farmers with measuring-rods disputing a boundary
(note [27]); the balance of battle is an honest wool-worker's scale,
held level "to win a pitiful wage for her children" (note [28]).
Then Hector lifts the stone two men of today could not shift, breaks
the double gate, and leaps through "in his face the very likeness of
swift night" — and the book ends inside the breach, the Danaans in
rout among their ships, nothing resolved except the door.

 414  pressed on harder around their counselor-king.

 416  inside the wall; and the work before them showed very great:
 417  for neither could the powerful Lycians break the wall
 418  of the Danaans and make a path to the ships,
 419  nor could the Danaan spearmen ever push back
 420  the Lycians from the wall, once they had come close against it.
 421  But as two men quarrel over boundary-stones,
 422  measuring-rods in hand, in a common field,
 423  who in a little strip of ground contend over the fair share:[27]
 424  so the breastworks held them apart; and over them
 425  they hacked at one another, at the chests, at the oxhide
 426  shields, round and well-made, and the fluttering bucklers.
 427  Many were wounded in the flesh by the pitiless bronze,
 428  whenever a man turned and his back was left bare
 429  in the fighting, and many straight through the shield itself.
 430  Everywhere the towers and breastworks were spattered
 431  with the blood of men, from both sides, Trojan and Achaean.
 432  But even so they could not turn the Achaeans to flight:
 433  the lines held — as an honest working woman holds the scales,
 434  who with the weight in one pan and the wool in the other
 435  draws them level, to win a pitiful wage for her children:[28]
 436  so their battle and their war were drawn level and taut,
 437  until the moment Zeus handed the higher glory to Hector,
 438  Priam's son, who was first to leap inside the wall of the Achaeans.
 439  He cried a piercing cry, to carry to the Trojans:
 440  "Rise up, horse-taming Trojans! Break the wall
 441  of the Argives, and hurl prodigious fire among the ships!"
 442  So he spoke, spurring them on; and every ear heard him,
 443  and they charged the wall in one mass; and then they
 444  began mounting the galleries, sharpened spears in hand,
 445  while Hector snatched up a stone and carried it — it stood
 446  before the gates, thick at the base and sharp
 447  on top; the two best men of a district could not easily
 448  lever it off the ground and onto a wagon,
 449  such as mortals are now; but he swung it easily, even alone,[29]
 450  for the son of crooked-counseled Cronos made it light for him.
 451  As when a shepherd carries the fleece of a ram
 452  easily in one hand, and its weight is little burden,
 453  so Hector lifted the stone and carried it straight
 454  at the doors that guarded the close and strong-fitted gates,
 455  double doors, high, with two crossed bars
 456  inside to hold them, fastened with a single bolt.
 457  He came and stood very close, and planted himself, and struck them
 458  full in the middle, legs braced wide, to give the blow its force,
 459  and broke both hinges away; and the stone fell inside
 460  by its own weight; and the gates groaned loud on either side; the bars
 461  did not hold, and the doors were burst apart this way and that
 462  under the rush of the stone. And glorious Hector leapt in,
 463  in his face the very likeness of swift night; and he blazed in the bronze
 464  that terribly clothed his body, and in his hands he held
 465  two spears. No one who met him could have held him back —
 466  none but the gods — when he leapt the gates; and his eyes flared with fire.
 467  Wheeling in the crowd, he called out to the Trojans
 468  to climb over the wall; and they obeyed his urging:
 469  at once some were climbing over the wall, while others poured
 470  through the built gates themselves; and the Danaans fled
 471  among the hollow ships; and the din rose unabating.[30]

NOTES

[1] (12.1-2) The book opens by holding the camera on Patroclus,
    still in the hut healing Eurypylus — where 11.848 left him, and
    where he stays until 15.390-404. The whole battle at the wall is
    thus bracketed inside Patroclus's delayed errand: the poem keeps
    its catastrophe waiting in a doorway.

[2] (12.3-35) Unique in Homer: the narrator leaves the story's time
    entirely to relate the wall's future annihilation — after the
    sack, Poseidon and Apollo turn the eight rivers of Ida against
    it for nine days, Zeus rains, the trident-bearer levers out the
    foundations and re-beds the beach in sand. The passage explains
    to the poet's own audience why no trace of so great a work
    survived to be visited: epic erasing its own footprint (already
    at 7.443-464 Poseidon complained the wall would out-fame the one
    he built for Laomedon; Zeus there licensed this very deletion).
    Line 23 contains the poem's only "race of half-god men"
    (ἡμιθέων γένος ἀνδρῶν): elsewhere the heroes are simply men;
    only here, looking back from the audience's diminished present,
    does the narrator name the gulf. Aristotle admired the maneuver
    (Strabo 13.1.36 preserves the remark): "the poet who made the
    wall also made it disappear."

[3] (12.41-48) The boar-or-lion at bay, wheeling on the closing
    ring: "his glorious heart knows no fear and no flight — and his
    courage kills him" (ἀγηνορίη δέ μιν ἔκτα, 46). The aside is
    proverb-grade and is the book's thesis about Hector — spoken
    inside a simile about him, six books before 22.99-130 proves it.

[4] (12.60-80) Polydamas's first counsel, accepted. He appears here
    for the first time speaking (named 11.57): born the same night
    as Hector (18.251), the poem's designated counter-voice — his
    four speeches (here; 211; 13.726; 18.254) run accepted,
    rejected, accepted, rejected, and the last rejection kills
    Hector's army. The pattern begins with success: chariots left
    at the ditch, the wall attacked on foot.

[5] (12.96-97) Asius's entry repeats his Catalogue lines (2.838-839)
    verbatim — our wording kept: the muster-roll recalled at the
    moment the muster begins to die (cf. the Merops sons, 11.329).

[6] (12.87-104) The five battalions — the Trojan order of battle,
    with two debuts that matter: Alcathous (dead at 13.427-444) and
    Asteropaeus the Paeonian, "warlike," chosen by Sarpedon as third
    — the man whose duel with Achilles in the river (21.139-204)
    will be the last full combat before Hector's. Sarpedon "stood
    out even among all": the poem promotes him steadily toward
    Book 16.

[7] (12.108-117) Asius refuses the plan — "the fool" (νήπιος), and
    the narrator files the death-notice in advance, naming the
    killer: Idomeneus, at 13.384-393. The prolepsis-with-obituary is
    the poem's standard seal on disobedience (2.38, 16.46, 20.466);
    "fate of the hateful name" (μοῖρα δυσώνυμος) belongs to the
    small class of fate-phrases where the word itself winces.

[8] (12.127-153) The Lapith aristeia: Polypoetes and Leonteus keep
    their Catalogue credentials (2.740-746). The similes stack —
    oaks with roots running deep (131-134), then wild boars
    shattering the brush (146-150). The apparatus preserves
    Hephaestion's observation that 131-140 and 141-153 read like
    alternative versions of the same scene, only one of which was
    meant to stand — a rare window onto the poem's own workshop;
    the printed text, and this translation, keep both.

[9] (12.162-172) Asius's outrage: "Father Zeus, so even you, it
    turns out, are an utter lover of lies!" (φιλοψευδής — nowhere
    else does a mortal call Zeus a liar to his face). The wasps-
    and-bees simile he builds — nest defenders who "will not
    abandon their hollow house... for their children's sake" — is
    finer than he knows: he has conceded that the two Lapiths are
    the ones fighting for something. The narrator's dry audit
    follows: Zeus was not persuaded; the glory was Hector's.

[10] (12.175-181) "hard for me to tell it all through, as though I
    were a god" — the narrator's only confession of overload in the
    Iliad, athetized by Aristophanes and Aristarchus and omitted by
    Zenodotus, perhaps precisely because it lets the human singer
    show through the Muse's voice. The "prodigious fire... of
    stone" (θεσπιδαὲς πῦρ λάϊνον) is a famous compression: the
    stone barrage burns like fire along the whole wall — the enjambed
    adjective detonates the metaphor.

[11] (12.200-209) The omen: an eagle on the left with a great snake
    in its talons; the snake, "not forgetting its war-craft,"
    strikes the eagle's chest, is dropped in the crowd, and the
    eagle screams away empty. Polydamas will read it exactly
    (218-227): you may take the prey, but you will not carry it
    home. The scene is the poem's fullest bird-sign, and the
    interpretation is never refuted — only overruled.

[12] (12.230-250) Hector's answer, culminating in the most quoted
    hexameter of the Iliad: "One bird-sign is best: to fight for
    your fatherland" (243). Cited from classical Athens onward as
    the motto of patriotic defense — and in context it is a king
    dismissing accurate intelligence on the strength of a private
    promise from Zeus (236) that expires at sundown (11.192-194,
    which Hector was not told). The rhetoric is immortal; the
    generalship is the disaster Polydamas predicted, and both men
    will say so later — Polydamas at 18.254-283, Hector to himself
    at 22.99-107. The closing threat (248-250) to kill any Trojan
    who hangs back completes the inversion: the omen-scene ends
    with the patriot promising to spear his own men.

[13] (12.251-255) Zeus's dust-squall off Ida "bewitched the mind of
    the Achaeans": the day's tilt made weather. θέλγε — the verb of
    enchantment (Circe's verb in the Odyssey) — for what a dust
    storm does to a defending army's judgment.

[14] (12.258-264) Siege engineering, precisely itemized: the
    galleries (κρόσσαι), the breastworks, the jutting buttress-
    piles set as footings for the towers — pulled, pried, and
    levered by hand. The Iliad's only sustained description of
    fortification assault, and the vocabulary is a hapax-cluster to
    match the singular event.

[15] (12.265-276) The Ajaxes' exhortation sorts the army into
    "excellent... middling... lesser — since men are in no way all
    equal in war — now there is work for every man": the clearest
    statement in Homer of the mass army's ethic, where even the
    worse man's labor counts — a note closer to the hoplite line
    than to the duel of champions, and fittingly spoken by the
    defensive hero par excellence.

[16] (12.278-289) The great snow simile: Zeus "shows them what his
    missiles are," lulls the winds, and covers peaks, headlands,
    clover meadows, "men's rich worked fields," harbors and shores
    — only the breaking wave keeps it off. The barrage of stones is
    rendered as the silencing of the whole worked world under
    steady snow: din translated into hush, war into weather, and
    for four lines the poem simply looks at peace. Among the most
    admired similes in ancient and modern criticism alike.

[17] (12.290-293) "Yet not even then would the Trojans... have
    broken the gates, had not Zeus roused his own son Sarpedon":
    the breach requires a demigod, and the poet says so in the
    counterfactual frame he reserves for real turning-points. The
    lion simile that follows (299-306) is the poem's fullest: the
    hungry lion who will either seize his prey or "himself be hit
    among the foremost" — the exact two outcomes Sarpedon offers
    Glaucus at 328.

[18] (12.294-297) Sarpedon's shield, described from the smith's
    bench: hammered bronze face, oxhides stitched inside with
    golden rods running full circle. The poem's craftsmanship-
    passages dignify what is about to be risked; compare the cup of
    Nestor (11.632-637) and Hector's stone below.

[19] (12.310-328) The Sarpedon speech — the classic statement of
    the heroic contract, in two movements. First, honor as
    obligation: the seat, the cuts, the full cups, the riverside
    estate — all are wages, and the Lycian in the ranks must be
    able to say his kings earn them ("not inglorious... their
    strength too is noble"). Second, the deeper clause (322-328):
    if escaping this battle meant being "ageless and immortal
    forever," fighting would be senseless — I would neither fight
    nor send you; it is because death comes anyway, in ten thousand
    forms no mortal dodges, that courage is rational: "let us go:
    we will give the triumph to some man, or he to us." Mortality
    is not the objection to heroism but its premise. This is the
    contract Achilles has just repudiated in Book 9 (life outweighs
    all Troy's wealth, 9.401-409), and the poem has placed its
    finest defense in the mouth of the man Zeus's own son — whom
    Zeus will watch die in Book 16, weeping blood-rain. Quoted and
    imitated from antiquity forward; the whole aristocratic ethics
    of the West descends through these nineteen lines.

[20] (12.331-363) The Menestheus episode: the Athenian captain —
    praised in the Catalogue as a marshal, silent ever since —
    peers for help and sends Thootes. The herald delivers the
    message nearly word for word (354-363 = 343-350 with only the
    deixis shifted: "here" to "there"), our wording kept: messenger-
    fidelity as verbal discipline. Ancient critics athetized the
    Teucer line in both copies (350 = 363) as needless; it is the
    request's whole point — the bowman is wanted against Sarpedon.

[21] (12.365) "Oïleus' son" — the lesser Ajax. Zenodotus read a
    different form of the patronymic here (Ἰλιάδην), and — a
    marvel of textual history — his reading appears on a vase of
    the sixth century BC (published J.H.S. 1898): a pre-
    Alexandrian variant caught in paint before any surviving
    manuscript. The printed text follows Aristarchus.

[22] (12.378-386) Epicles goes down under the jagged marble block —
    "not easily could a man hold it in both hands... such as
    mortals are now" (the fixed diminished-present formula, 5.304)
    — and falls "like a diver... from the high tower." The diver-
    image for a death-plunge recurs at 16.742 (Cebriones, with
    Patroclus's terrible joke about oysters): grace at the instant
    of obliteration.

[23] (12.387-391) Teucer's arrow finds Glaucus's bared arm, and
    Glaucus drops back off the wall hiding the wound, "so that no
    Achaean might see that he was hit and glory over him." The
    concealment is heroic bookkeeping — deny the enemy the boast —
    and the wound is a plot-load: at 16.508-531 it is this arm
    Glaucus begs Apollo to heal so he can defend Sarpedon's corpse.

[24] (12.397-399) Sarpedon pulls down the breastwork with his bare
    hands: "the wall was laid naked; and he had made a road for
    many." He does not enter — that honor is reserved (note [30]);
    he opens. Made a road for many: the plain half-line has the
    whole tragedy of Book 16 folded in it — the road is for Hector,
    and for Patroclus coming the other way.

[25] (12.400-403) Teucer's arrow finds only the shield-strap:
    "Zeus beat the death-spirits away from his son — he was not to
    be broken by the ships' sterns." Not saved: rescheduled. The
    clause quietly fixes the terms of 16.433-461, where Zeus will
    weigh saving him and be talked out of it by Hera. Every rescue
    in this poem is a date moved.

[26] (12.409-412) After the grandest speech in the book, Sarpedon's
    working voice: I cannot break through alone — "Follow me in!
    More hands make better work." The proverb (πλεόνων δέ τι ἔργον
    ἄμεινον) is the plain-prose twin of Diomedes' "when two go
    together" (10.224-226): heroism, at the wall as in the night,
    is cooperative labor.

[27] (12.421-426) The deadlock as a boundary dispute: two men with
    measuring-rods in a common field, quarreling "in a little strip
    of ground over the fair share." The war for Troy reduced to
    village litigation over inches — and with the surveyor's
    precision comes the bitter joke: this, too, is a war about
    property. The breastwork is the boundary-stone; the poem's
    great space annihilates to a farmer's furrow.

[28] (12.432-436) The second scales of the poem: not Zeus's golden
    balance (8.69) but a wage-worker's — "an honest working woman"
    (γυνὴ χερνῆτις ἀληθής) holding weight against wool, drawing
    them level "to win a pitiful wage for her children." The
    adjective ἀληθής — "true, honest" — is given to the poorest
    person in the Iliad, and the whole stalemated battle hangs from
    her steady hand. The pathos is in the economics: she balances
    so carefully because the wage is pitiful and the children are
    hers. No simile in the poem reaches further down the social
    order, or carries more weight per line.

[29] (12.445-450) Hector's stone: thick-butted, sharp-topped — a
    natural battering ram — "the two best men of a district could
    not easily lever it off the ground and onto a wagon, such as
    mortals are now; but he swung it easily even alone." The
    diminished-present formula again (its second use in the book,
    the fifth in the poem), and then the theological asterisk:
    "the son of crooked-counseled Cronos made it light for him."
    Line 450 was omitted by Zenodotus and athetized by Aristophanes
    and Aristarchus — deleting the asterisk credits Hector; keeping
    it credits Zeus; the printed text keeps it, and the shepherd-
    and-fleece simile (451-452) plays the weight both ways.

[30] (12.457-471) The breach, in carpenter's detail: double doors,
    two crossed bars, one bolt; the stance ("legs braced wide, to
    give the blow its force"), both hinges sheared, the stone
    falling inside "by its own weight," the gates bellowing. Then
    the poem's great darkness-image: Hector leaps in "in his face
    the very likeness of swift night" — νυκτὶ θοῇ ἀτάλαντος — man
    as nightfall, the bronze blazing about the dark (Zenodotus read
    "wrapped in night," and the vulgate's phrase has passed into
    proverb as "sudden night"). "No one who met him could have held
    him back — none but the gods... and his eyes flared with fire."
    The book ends at the exact instant of penetration — Trojans
    over the wall and through the gates, Danaans in rout among the
    ships — the only book of the poem that closes inside an
    unresolved catastrophe: the door broken, and everything still
    to lose.
