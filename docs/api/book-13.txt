BOOK 13

Poseidon's defiance: Zeus looks away, the sea-god slips into the
battle in disguise, and the line holds — shield on shield, man on
man.

TRANSLATOR'S COMMENTARY

Book 13 begins with a look and ends with a noise. Zeus, satisfied,
turns his shining eyes away from Troy toward the far pastoral north —
the mare-milkers and the Abioi, "most righteous of men" (note [1]) —
certain no god will dare interfere; Poseidon, watching from the peak
of Samothrace, comes down in four strides, and the whole book runs on
his stolen presence: a god working the Achaean line in borrowed
shapes and voices, "in secret, always, up and down the army" —
because Zeus is elder-born and knows more (note [15]). The last line
closes the frame: the clamor of both armies "reached the ether and
the radiance of Zeus" — the battle he refused to watch arrives as
sound (note [37]).

Its centerpiece is the poem's one aristeia of a middle-aged man:
Idomeneus of Crete, "half-grey," met coming back from a comrade's
sickbed, talked into glory by a god, and given the book's most
famous set-piece before he throws a spear — the anatomy of courage
and cowardice in ambush, where the coward's color runs and his teeth
chatter and the brave man's color never changes (note [12]). His
kills are the book's plot: Othryoneus, who bid for Cassandra with a
promise instead of gifts (note [16]); Asius, paying the death-notice
filed in Book 12; and Alcathous, whom Poseidon freezes where he
stands — the god holding the victim for the blow, the most terrible
divine intervention in the poem (note [19]).

Around the aristeia the book builds the day's resistance out of
lists and low things: the locked phalanx where "shield pressed on
shield, helmet on helmet, man on man" (note [6]); the defense roster
with its Ionians (nowhere else in Homer) and its exiled homicide
Medon; the Locrians who fight shoeless of honor, with bows and
wool slings, unseen from behind (note [31]); beans jumping from a
winnowing-shovel; oxen sweating at one plow; a roped bull; a worm.
Where Book 12 measured war by peace, Book 13 arms peace and sends
it back in. Two wounds are planted for later books: Ascalaphus lies
dead and his father Ares, penned on Olympus by Zeus's command, does
not know it (note [23]); and Harpalion's father walks weeping behind
the bier — Pylaemenes, whom the poem killed in Book 5: the most
famous inconsistency in Homer, which ancient critics solved with an
athetesis and this translation keeps in the open (note [27]).

   1  Now Zeus, when he had brought the Trojans and Hector to the ships,
   2  left them beside them, to bear the toil and the misery
   3  without respite; and he himself turned his shining eyes
   4  away, gazing far off at the land of the horse-herding Thracians,
   5  and the close-fighting Mysians, and the proud Mare-milkers
   6  who drink the milk, and the Abioi, most righteous of men.[1]
   7  To Troy he no longer turned his shining eyes at all,
   8  for he did not expect in his heart that any immortal
   9  would come to stand by either Trojans or Danaans.
  10  But the lord earth-shaker kept no blind man's watch:
  11  he sat marveling at the war and the fighting,
  12  high on the topmost peak of wooded Samos,
  13  Thracian Samos; for from there all Ida showed clear,
  14  and Priam's city showed clear, and the ships of the Achaeans.
  15  There he sat, come up out of the sea, and he pitied the Achaeans
  16  broken under the Trojans; and against Zeus his anger was strong.
  17  At once he came down from the rugged mountain,
  18  striding fast on his feet; and the tall hills trembled, and the timber,
  19  under the immortal feet of Poseidon as he went.[2]
  20  Three strides he made, and with the fourth he reached his goal,
  21  Aegae, where his famous house in the depths of the water,
  22  golden, glittering, is built for him, imperishable forever.
  23  There he came, and yoked beneath his chariot the bronze-hoofed horses,
  24  swift-flying, with manes streaming gold,
  25  and clothed himself in gold, and took the whip,
  26  gold, well-wrought, and mounted the chariot,
  27  and drove out over the waves. And the sea-beasts gamboled beneath him,
  28  coming from every hollow, and did not fail to know their lord;
  29  and the sea in its joy stood apart; and the horses flew on
  30  lightly, and the bronze axle beneath was not even wetted;[3]
  31  and the bounding horses carried him toward the ships of the Achaeans.
  32  There is a broad cave in the depths of the deep water,
  33  midway between Tenedos and rugged Imbros:
  34  there Poseidon the earth-shaker stayed his horses,
  35  loosing them from the chariot, and threw down ambrosial fodder
  36  for them to eat; and around their feet he cast golden hobbles,
  37  unbreakable, not to be undone, so they would stay there unmoved
  38  for their lord's return; and he went to the army of the Achaeans.
  39  And the Trojans, massed like flame or like a stormwind,
  40  followed Hector, Priam's son, in insatiable fury,
  41  roaring, crying as one; and they hoped to take the ships
  42  of the Achaeans, and kill all their best men beside them.
  43  But Poseidon, holder of the earth, the earth-shaker,
  44  come up out of the deep water, was rousing the Argives,
  45  likened to Calchas in body and tireless voice.
  46  First he spoke to the two Ajaxes, eager as they already were:
  47  "You two Ajaxes — you will save the army of the Achaeans
  48  if you remember your courage, and no shiver of panic.
  49  Elsewhere I do not fear the invincible hands
  50  of the Trojans who have climbed our great wall in a mass:
  51  the strong-greaved Achaeans will hold all of them.
  52  But here I fear most terribly that we suffer something —
  53  here where that rabid one leads them, like a flame,
  54  Hector, who claims to be a son of mighty Zeus.
  55  May some god put it into the hearts of you two
  56  to stand hard yourselves, and to command the others:
  57  then you might beat him back from the swift-faring ships
  58  for all his fury, even if the Olympian himself is driving him."
  59  He spoke, and the holder of the earth, the earth-shaker,
  60  striking both of them with his staff, filled them with strong fury,
  61  and made their limbs light, their feet and their hands above.
  62  And he himself rose to fly like a swift-winged hawk
  63  that lifts from a sheer towering rock-face
  64  and swoops to chase some other bird across the plain:
  65  so Poseidon the earth-shaker darted away from them.
  66  Of the two, swift Ajax, Oïleus' son, knew him first,
  67  and at once he spoke to Ajax, Telamon's son:
  68  "Ajax — since some one of the gods who hold Olympus,
  69  likened to the seer, tells us to fight beside the ships —
  70  and that is not Calchas, the reader of gods' bird-signs:
  71  the trace of his feet and legs from behind, as he went off,
  72  I knew it easily — the gods are easy to know —[4]
  73  now the heart in my own chest, for its part,
  74  is set far more on warring and fighting,
  75  and my feet beneath are hungry for it, and my hands above."
  76  Answering him spoke Telamonian Ajax:
  77  "So with me: my invincible hands about the spear
  78  are hungry, and my fury has risen, and beneath me both
  79  my feet are driving; and I long — even alone —
  80  to fight Hector, Priam's son, in his insatiable fury."
  81  So the two of them spoke to one another,
  82  in the joy of battle-craving the god had cast into their hearts;
  83  and meanwhile the holder of the earth roused the Achaeans behind them,
  84  who were cooling their hearts back by the swift ships.
  85  Their limbs were unstrung with grievous weariness,
  86  and grief was rising in their hearts as they watched
  87  the Trojans, who had climbed the great wall in a mass:
  88  looking at them, they let the tears spill under their brows,
  89  for they did not think they would escape the evil. But the earth-shaker,
  90  passing easily among them, stirred the strong battle-lines.
  91  To Teucer first he came, and Leïtus, commanding,
  92  and the hero Peneleos, and Thoas, and Deïpyrus,
  93  and Meriones and Antilochus, masters of the war-shout;
  94  urging these on, he spoke winged words:
  95  "Shame, Argives — young men! It was you I trusted:
  96  fight, and you will save our ships.
  97  But if you slacken from the misery of this war,
  98  now dawns the day we are broken under the Trojans.
  99  Shame! — a great marvel is this I see with my eyes,
 100  a terrible thing I never once said would come to pass:
 101  the Trojans advancing on our ships — the Trojans, who before
 102  were like skittering deer, which through the timber,
 103  feed for jackals and leopards and wolves,
 104  wander witless and weaponless, with no fight in them:
 105  so before now the Trojans would not stand against
 106  the fury and the hands of the Achaeans — not for a moment.
 107  Now, far from their city, they are fighting at the hollow ships,
 108  through a leader's failing and the slackness of the army,
 109  who quarrel with him, and will not defend
 110  the swift-faring ships, but are killed among them.
 111  Yet even if the truth is that the man wholly to blame
 112  is the hero, Atreus' son, wide-ruling Agamemnon,
 113  because he dishonored the swift-footed son of Peleus,
 114  we, for our part, have no leave to slacken from the war.
 115  No: let us heal it, quickly. The minds of good men can be healed.[5]
 116  It is no longer honorable, this slackening of your rushing courage,
 117  you who are all of you the best men in the army. I would not
 118  quarrel with any man who hung back from the fighting
 119  because he was worthless; but with you my heart is angry to the core.
 120  Soft men! Soon you will make some greater evil yet
 121  by this hanging back. Let each of you put in his heart
 122  shame, and the fear of blame: a great struggle has risen.
 123  Hector is fighting at the ships now, good at the war-cry,
 124  in his power, and he has broken the gates and the long bar."
 125  So the holder of the earth, commanding, roused the Achaeans.
 126  And around the two Ajaxes the battle-lines took their stand,
 127  strong lines: Ares himself could not have come and faulted them,
 128  nor Athena, driver of armies. For the chosen best
 129  stood and awaited the Trojans and brilliant Hector,
 130  locking spear on spear, shield on layered shield:
 131  buckler pressed on buckler, helmet on helmet, man on man,
 132  and the horsehair crests on the bright ridges touched
 133  as they nodded, so close they stood to one another;[6]
 134  and the spears, shaken in daring hands,
 135  crossed over one another; and their minds were set straight ahead, hot to fight.
 136  The Trojans struck first, massed; Hector led them,
 137  straining straight on, like a boulder off a cliff-face
 138  that a river swollen with winter shoves down the ledge,
 139  breaking with prodigious rain the shameless rock's moorings;
 140  bounding high it flies, and the timber crashes
 141  under it; and it runs on unswerving, steady, until it reaches
 142  the level plain — and there it rolls no more, for all its rush:[7]
 143  so Hector for a while threatened to sweep clean to the sea,
 144  clean through the huts and ships of the Achaeans,
 145  killing; but when he ran against those close-set battle-lines,
 146  he stopped, jammed hard against them. And the sons of the Achaeans facing him,
 147  stabbing with swords and double-curved spears,
 148  thrust him away from them; and he shuddered and gave ground.
 149  He cried a piercing cry, to carry to the Trojans:
 150  "Trojans and Lycians and Dardanians who fight at close quarters —
 151  stand by me! Not for long will the Achaeans hold me,
 152  not though they have built themselves into a tower,
 153  no — I think they will give way under my spear, if truly
 154  the best of gods drove me on, the loud-thunderer, Hera's lord."
 155  So speaking he stirred the fury and the heart of every man.
 156  And Deiphobus strode among them thinking great things,
 157  Priam's son, holding before him his shield, balanced every way,
 158  stepping light on his feet, advancing under the shield's cover.
 159  And Meriones aimed at him with his shining spear
 160  and threw, and did not miss, striking the shield, balanced every way,
 161  the bull's-hide shield — but drove nowhere through it: well before that
 162  the long spear snapped at the socket. And Deiphobus
 163  held the bull's-hide shield away from him, and his heart feared
 164  the spear of war-minded Meriones; but that hero
 165  drew back into the band of his companions, angry to the bone
 166  both ways — for the lost victory, and the spear he had shattered —
 167  and set off along the huts and ships of the Achaeans
 168  to fetch a long spear that was left him in his hut.
 169  The others fought on, and the quenchless roar rose.
 170  Teucer, Telamon's son, was first to kill his man,
 171  the spearman Imbrius, son of Mentor rich in horses.
 172  He lived in Pedaeum, before the sons of the Achaeans came,
 173  and had for wife a bastard daughter of Priam, Medesicaste;
 174  but when the double-ended ships of the Danaans came,
 175  he came back to Ilion, and stood out among the Trojans,
 176  and lived with Priam, who honored him like his own children.
 177  Him the son of Telamon stabbed under the ear with the long spear,
 178  and wrenched the spear out; and he fell like an ash tree
 179  that on the crest of a mountain seen clear from every side
 180  is cut down by the bronze, and brings its soft leaves to the ground:
 181  so he fell, and his armor, intricate with bronze, clashed about him.
 182  And Teucer rushed in, hot to strip the armor off him;
 183  but as he rushed, Hector cast at him with his shining spear.
 184  He saw it coming and dodged the bronze spear —
 185  barely; and Hector struck Amphimachus, son of Cteatus
 186  of Actor's line, with the spear in the chest as he came into the battle:
 187  he fell with a thud, and his armor clattered upon him.
 188  And Hector rushed in to tear the helmet, close-fitted
 189  at the temples, from the head of great-hearted Amphimachus;
 190  but as he rushed, Ajax lunged at him with the shining spear —
 191  yet nowhere reached the flesh, for all of him was sheathed
 192  in terrible bronze. He stabbed the boss of the shield instead
 193  and shoved him back with great strength; and Hector gave ground
 194  behind the two corpses, and the Achaeans dragged them clear.
 195  Amphimachus, then, Stichius and brilliant Menestheus,
 196  captains of the Athenians, carried back among the Achaean host;
 197  and Imbrius the two Ajaxes, hot with rushing courage,
 198  as two lions snatch a goat from under the guard
 199  of sharp-toothed dogs and carry it through the thick brush,
 200  holding it high above the ground in their jaws:
 201  so the two Ajaxes, helmed for war, holding him high,
 202  stripped his armor; and the head from the soft neck
 203  Oïleus' son cut off, in wrath for Amphimachus,
 204  and swung and sent it rolling like a ball through the crowd;
 205  and it fell in the dust in front of Hector's feet.[8]
 206  And then it was that Poseidon was angered to the heart,
 207  his grandson fallen in the dread combat,[9]
 208  and he set off along the huts and ships of the Achaeans
 209  to rouse the Danaans; and he was building sorrows for the Trojans.
 210  And Idomeneus, famed with the spear, met him
 211  coming from a companion who had just come out of the fighting,
 212  cut behind the knee by the sharp bronze.
 213  His comrades carried him in; and Idomeneus, giving the healers
 214  their orders, was going to his hut, for he still longed
 215  to face the war. And the lord earth-shaker spoke to him,
 216  likening his voice to Thoas, Andraemon's son,
 217  who in all Pleuron and steep Calydon
 218  ruled the Aetolians, honored like a god by the people:
 219  "Idomeneus, counselor of the Cretans, where have they gone,
 220  the threats the sons of the Achaeans threatened against the Trojans?"
 221  And Idomeneus, leader of the Cretans, answered him:
 222  "Thoas, no man is at fault now — not so far
 223  as I can tell; for all of us know how to fight.
 224  No one is gripped by bloodless fear, no one yields
 225  to shirking and ducks out of this evil war. No — this, it seems,
 226  must be the pleasure of the overmastering son of Cronos:
 227  that the Achaeans perish here, nameless, far from Argos.
 228  But Thoas — you were always staunch before,
 229  and you rouse any other man you see slackening:
 230  so do not leave off now. Call to every man."
 231  Then answered him Poseidon the earth-shaker:
 232  "Idomeneus, may the man never come home
 233  from Troy — may he be sport for dogs on this ground —
 234  who on this day chooses to slacken from the fighting.
 235  Come then, take up your weapons and come out with me. We must
 236  press this together, and see if the two of us can be of use.
 237  Even sorry men, combined, come to some courage —[10]
 238  and the two of us would know how to fight even the best."
 239  So speaking, the god went back into the toil of men;
 240  and Idomeneus, when he reached his well-built hut,
 241  put his fine armor about his body, caught up two spears,
 242  and went his way like the lightning-bolt the son of Cronos
 243  takes in his hand and brandishes from glittering Olympus,
 244  showing a sign to mortals, and its flashes blaze far:
 245  so the bronze blazed on his chest as he ran.
 246  And Meriones, his brave aide, met him
 247  while he was still near the hut — he was going to fetch
 248  a bronze spear — and the strength of Idomeneus spoke to him:
 249  "Meriones, Molus's son, swift-footed, dearest of my companions,[11]
 250  why have you come, leaving the war and the combat?
 251  Are you hit somewhere — is an arrowhead wearing you down?
 252  Or do you come after me with some message? I myself
 253  have no wish to sit in the huts, but to fight."
 254  And level-headed Meriones answered him:
 255  "Idomeneus, counselor of the bronze-shirted Cretans,
 256  I come to fetch a spear, if one is left
 257  in your huts; the one I carried before, we shattered it —
 258  I struck the shield of Deiphobus in his arrogance."
 259  And Idomeneus, leader of the Cretans, answered him:
 260  "Spears — if you want them you will find one, and twenty,
 261  standing in my hut against the bright entrance-wall:
 262  Trojan spears, which I strip from the men I kill. I hold[12]
 263  it is not my way to fight standing far from enemy men;
 264  and so I have spears, and bossed shields,
 265  and helmets, and corslets shining bright."
 266  And level-headed Meriones answered him:
 267  "I too, by my hut and my black ship, have plenty
 268  of Trojan spoils — but they are not close by for the taking.
 269  For I say I am no more forgetful of courage than you:
 270  I take my stand among the first in the battle where men win glory
 271  whenever the strife of war has risen.
 272  Some other of the bronze-shirted Achaeans might miss
 273  my fighting; but you — you are the one who knows it, I think."
 274  And Idomeneus, leader of the Cretans, answered him:
 275  "I know your worth, and what kind of man you are. Why recite it?
 276  If now beside the ships all our best were being counted
 277  for an ambush — where the worth of men shows clearest,
 278  where the coward and the brave man stand revealed:
 279  the coward's color changes to one thing and another,
 280  and the spirit in his chest cannot hold him steady in his seat,
 281  but he shifts his crouch, sits back from foot to foot,
 282  and the heart in his ribs hammers hugely
 283  as he imagines the death-spirits, and his teeth chatter aloud;
 284  but the brave man's color never changes, and no fear in him
 285  is too great, once he has settled into the ambush of men,
 286  and he prays to be mixed at once in the misery of battle —
 287  there, no man could fault your fury or your hands.
 288  For even if you were hit in the work, or struck close in,
 289  the missile would not fall on the nape of your neck, or your back:
 290  it would meet your chest or your belly
 291  as you pressed forward into the front-fighters' trysting-place.[13]
 292  But come, no more standing here talking of this
 293  like children — someone might scoff beyond all measure.
 294  Go to the hut, and choose yourself a massive spear."
 295  So he spoke; and Meriones, the equal of swift Ares,
 296  quickly took up a bronze spear from the hut
 297  and went after Idomeneus, his whole mind on war.
 298  As Ares, bane of mortals, goes out to war,
 299  and with him follows Panic, his dear son, strong and fearless,
 300  who sends even the patient-hearted warrior running;
 301  the two of them arm out of Thrace against the Ephyri
 302  or the great-hearted Phlegyes — and they do not hear[14]
 303  both sides' prayers, but hand the glory to one:
 304  such were Meriones and Idomeneus, leaders of men,
 305  as they went into the war, helmed in blazing bronze.
 306  And Meriones spoke to him first with his word:
 307  "Son of Deucalion, where are you eager to enter the crowd?
 308  On the right of the whole army, or through the middle,
 309  or on the left? For nowhere else, I think, do the long-haired
 310  Achaeans so fail of hands in the fighting."
 311  And Idomeneus, leader of the Cretans, answered him:
 312  "In the center ships there are others to hold the defense,
 313  the two Ajaxes, and Teucer, who is best of the Achaeans
 314  in bowmanship, and good too in the standing fight:
 315  they will drive him to sickness of it, however hot for battle —
 316  Hector, Priam's son — however strong he is.
 317  It will be steep going for him, blaze as he may to fight:
 318  against their conquering fury and invincible hands
 319  he will not fire the ships — unless the son of Cronos in person
 320  hurls a burning brand onto the swift ships.
 321  To no man would he yield, great Telamonian Ajax —
 322  to no man who is mortal and eats the grain of Demeter,
 323  who can be broken by bronze or by great hurled stones.
 324  Not even to Achilles, breaker of men, would he give way —
 325  in the standing fight; in speed of foot there is no contesting him.
 326  So take us leftward of the army, and quickly: we will see
 327  whether we give the triumph to some man, or he to us."
 328  So he spoke; and Meriones, the equal of swift Ares,
 329  led on, until they came to the army where he had bidden.
 330  And when they saw Idomeneus, strong as a flame,
 331  him and his aide, in their intricate war-gear,
 332  they called to one another through the crowd and made for him,
 333  and their struggle joined, one mass, by the sterns of the ships.
 334  As when squalls come racing under shrill winds
 335  on a day when the dust lies thickest on the roads,
 336  and all together they raise up a great cloud of dust:
 337  so their battle came together in one mass, and they were hot
 338  in their hearts to slaughter each other in the crowd with the sharp bronze.
 339  And the man-destroying battle bristled with the long
 340  flesh-cutting spears they gripped; and eyes were blinded
 341  by the flare of bronze off glittering helmets
 342  and fresh-burnished corslets and shining shields
 343  as they came on in one mass. It would be a hard-hearted man
 344  who could rejoice at the sight of that toil, and not be grieved.[15]
 345  And the two strong sons of Cronos, divided in purpose,
 346  were building grim sorrows for the fighting men.
 347  Zeus willed the victory for the Trojans and Hector,
 348  glorifying swift-footed Achilles; yet he had no wish
 349  for the Achaean army to perish wholly before Ilion —
 350  he was glorifying Thetis, and her strong-hearted son.
 351  But Poseidon had come among the Argives and was stirring them,
 352  slipped up in secret from the grey salt water; for it galled him
 353  to see them broken by Trojans, and against Zeus his anger was strong.
 354  Both, to be sure, were of one stock and one parentage,
 355  but Zeus was elder-born, and he knew more.
 356  For that reason Poseidon shunned defending them openly,
 357  and in secret, always, roused them down the army, likened to a man.
 358  So the two of them knotted the cord of strong strife
 359  and leveling war, and drew it taut over both sides —
 360  a cord unbreakable, not to be untied; and it unstrung the knees of many.[16]
 361  Then — though his hair was half-grey — Idomeneus,
 362  calling on the Danaans, leapt on the Trojans and drove panic among them.
 363  For he killed Othryoneus, in the town from Cabesus —
 364  lately come, chasing the report of the war,
 365  and he had asked for the loveliest of Priam's daughters,
 366  Cassandra, without bride-gifts — promising a great work instead:
 367  to drive the sons of the Achaeans out of Troy against their will.
 368  And old Priam had promised her, had bowed his head:
 369  give her he would; and Othryoneus fought trusting the promises.
 370  Idomeneus aimed at him with his shining spear
 371  and threw, and hit him in mid-stride; the corslet of bronze
 372  he wore did not hold: the spear fixed in his belly's middle.
 373  He fell with a thud; and Idomeneus exulted, calling:
 374  "Othryoneus — I congratulate you beyond all living men
 375  if you are really going to accomplish all you promised
 376  Priam, Dardanus' heir! He promised you his daughter.
 377  Look — we too could promise you as much, and keep it:
 378  we would give you the loveliest of the daughters of Atreus' son,
 379  bringing her out of Argos for you to marry, if you will join us
 380  and sack the well-peopled city of Ilion.
 381  Come along: let us settle the marriage at the seafaring ships —
 382  you will find we are not hard men over bride-terms."[17]
 383  So speaking the hero Idomeneus dragged him by the foot
 384  through the strong encounter. But Asius came to avenge him,
 385  on foot before his horses; the aide who drove them
 386  kept them breathing at his shoulders always. And his heart was hot
 387  to hit Idomeneus; but the other was quicker, and struck him with the spear
 388  in the throat under the chin, and drove the bronze clean through.
 389  He fell as an oak falls, or a white poplar,
 390  or a towering pine, that carpenters up in the mountains
 391  cut down with fresh-whetted axes to be a ship's timber:[18]
 392  so he lay stretched out before his horses and chariot,
 393  bellowing, clawing at the blood-soaked dust.
 394  And the charioteer was struck out of the wits he had:
 395  he did not dare wheel the horses round and slip
 396  out of the enemy's hands; and Antilochus, staunch in the fight,
 397  caught him with the spear mid-body; the corslet of bronze
 398  he wore did not hold: the spear fixed in his belly's middle,
 399  and he fell gasping out of the well-built chariot.
 400  And the horses Antilochus, great-hearted Nestor's son,
 401  drove off from the Trojans to the strong-greaved Achaeans.
 402  Then Deiphobus came up very close to Idomeneus,
 403  grieving for Asius, and cast with his shining spear.
 404  But Idomeneus saw it coming and dodged the bronze spear:
 405  he hid himself behind his shield, balanced every way,
 406  the shield he carried, built of oxhides and gleaming bronze
 407  rounded on the wheel, fitted with two cross-rods:
 408  under it he gathered himself whole, and the bronze spear flew over
 409  and the shield rang dry and harsh as the spear grazed
 410  across it. Yet Deiphobus's heavy hand had not thrown in vain:
 411  he hit Hypsenor, Hippasus's son, shepherd of the people,
 412  in the liver below the midriff, and unstrung his knees on the instant.
 413  And Deiphobus vaunted terribly over him, crying far:
 414  "Not unavenged, after all, lies Asius! No — I say
 415  that even going down to Hades, the strong closer of the gate,
 416  he will be glad at heart: I have given him an escort for the road."[19]
 417  So he spoke, and grief came over the Argives at his vaunting,
 418  and most of all he stirred the heart of war-minded Antilochus;
 419  yet grieved as he was, he did not forget his companion:
 420  running, he stood over him and covered him with the shield,
 421  and two loyal companions stooped beneath him,
 422  Mecisteus, Echius' son, and brilliant Alastor,
 423  and carried him, groaning heavily, to the hollow ships.
 424  And Idomeneus did not slacken his great fury: he strained
 425  always either to wrap some Trojan in blinding night
 426  or to fall himself with a crash, warding ruin from the Achaeans.
 427  Then the dear son of Zeus-nurtured Aesyetes,
 428  the hero Alcathous — he was Anchises' son-in-law,
 429  married to the eldest of his daughters, Hippodameia,
 430  whom her father and her lady mother loved with all their hearts
 431  at home; for she outshone all the girls of her age
 432  in beauty and in handiwork and in wit, and for that
 433  the best man in the breadth of Troy had married her —
 434  him, then, Poseidon broke under Idomeneus's hand:
 435  he charmed his shining eyes and shackled his glorious limbs,[20]
 436  so that he could neither run back nor swerve aside,
 437  but stood unmoving, like a grave-pillar or a tree
 438  in high leaf, while the hero Idomeneus stabbed him with the spear
 439  mid-chest, and burst the bronze tunic around it,
 440  the tunic that till then had kept ruin from his flesh:
 441  it screamed dry and harsh as it tore around the spear.
 442  He fell with a thud, the spear stuck fast in his heart,
 443  and the heart, still beating, shook even the butt-end
 444  of the spear, quivering — until at last massive Ares stilled its fury.
 445  And Idomeneus vaunted terribly over him, crying far:
 446  "Deiphobus! Shall we call it a fair reckoning now —
 447  three men killed for one? — since it was you who boasted.
 448  Strange man: come stand against me yourself, face to face,
 449  and learn what son of Zeus's line has come here:
 450  Zeus first begot Minos, warden of Crete;
 451  and Minos got a son, blameless Deucalion;
 452  and Deucalion fathered me, lord over many men
 453  in wide Crete; and now the ships have carried me here,
 454  an evil to you, and your father, and the rest of the Trojans."[21]
 455  So he spoke; and Deiphobus was torn two ways —
 456  whether to fall back and take as companion some one
 457  of the great-hearted Trojans, or to try it alone.
 458  And as he weighed it, this seemed to him the better course:
 459  to go for Aeneas. He found him standing rearmost
 460  of the crowd; for he was forever furious at brilliant Priam,
 461  because, brave among men as he was, Priam gave him no honor.[22]
 462  Standing close, he spoke winged words to him:
 463  "Aeneas, counselor of the Trojans, now the great need is on you
 464  to stand for your sister's husband — if kinship's grief can reach you.
 465  Come, let us fight for Alcathous, who long ago,
 466  as your sister's husband, reared you in his house when you were little;
 467  and Idomeneus, famed with the spear, has stripped him."
 468  So he spoke, and stirred the heart in Aeneas's chest,
 469  and he went after Idomeneus, his whole mind on war.
 470  But no panic took hold of Idomeneus, as though he were some pampered boy:
 471  he stood his ground, like a boar in the mountains sure of his strength,
 472  who abides the great oncoming rabble of men
 473  in a lonely place, and the ridge of his back bristles up,
 474  and his eyes shine with fire, and he whets his tusks,
 475  hot to beat off the dogs and the men:
 476  so Idomeneus, famed with the spear, stood fast and did not yield
 477  to Aeneas coming swift to the rescue. But he called his companions,
 478  looking to Ascalaphus and Aphareus and Deïpyrus
 479  and Meriones and Antilochus, masters of the war-shout;
 480  urging these on, he spoke winged words:
 481  "Here, friends — I am alone: defend me! I dread him terribly,
 482  swift-footed Aeneas, who is coming at me —
 483  who is very strong in battle at killing men,
 484  and has the flower of youth besides, which is the greatest power.
 485  If we were of one age, with the heart I carry now,
 486  quickly would he carry off great power — or I would."
 487  So he spoke; and all of them, one spirit in their chests,
 488  took their stand close by him, shields leaned against their shoulders.
 489  And Aeneas on the other side called to his companions,
 490  looking to Deiphobus and Paris and brilliant Agenor,
 491  fellow-leaders of the Trojans with him; and after them
 492  the troops followed, as flocks follow the bell-ram
 493  down from the pasture to drink — and the shepherd's heart is glad:[23]
 494  so the heart in Aeneas's chest rejoiced
 495  when he saw the mass of troops following his own lead.
 496  And around Alcathous they went at each other, close in,
 497  with their long pikes; and the bronze about their chests
 498  rang terribly as they aimed at one another
 499  through the crowd. And two men of war beyond the others,
 500  Aeneas and Idomeneus, the equals of Ares,
 501  strained to hew each other's flesh with the pitiless bronze.
 502  Aeneas first cast at Idomeneus;
 503  but he saw it coming and dodged the bronze spear,
 504  and the point of Aeneas's spear went quivering
 505  into the earth, hurled in vain from his massive hand.
 506  But Idomeneus struck Oenomaus mid-belly,
 507  and burst the plate of his corslet, and the bronze
 508  spilled his bowels through; and falling in the dust he clutched the earth in his palm.
 509  And Idomeneus wrenched the long-shadowed spear out
 510  of the corpse, but could not strip the rest of the fine armor
 511  from his shoulders: the missiles pressed him too hard.
 512  For the joints of his feet no longer held firm in the charge —
 513  no spring left, either to follow his own cast or to dodge another's.
 514  So in the standing fight he still fended off the pitiless day,
 515  but in retreat his feet could no longer carry him quick out of the battle.
 516  And as he was stepping slowly back, Deiphobus cast at him
 517  with his shining spear — for the grudge he held him was relentless, always.
 518  But this time too he missed; and the spear hit Ascalaphus,
 519  the son of Enyalius; through the shoulder the massive spear
 520  drove; and falling in the dust he clutched the earth in his palm.
 521  But loud-roaring massive Ares knew nothing of it yet —
 522  that his son had fallen in the strong encounter —
 523  for he sat on the peak of Olympus, under the golden clouds,
 524  penned there by the designs of Zeus, where the other
 525  immortal gods sat too, barred from the war.[24]
 526  And around Ascalaphus they went at each other close in:
 527  Deiphobus tore the shining helmet from Ascalaphus,
 528  but Meriones, the equal of swift Ares, leaping in,
 529  stabbed his upper arm with the spear, and out of his hand
 530  the socketed helmet fell ringing to the ground.
 531  And Meriones, springing in again like a vulture,
 532  wrenched the massive spear out of his upper arm
 533  and drew back into the band of his companions. And Polites,
 534  Deiphobus's own brother, throwing his arms around his waist,
 535  led him out of the harsh-sounding war, until they reached
 536  his swift horses, which stood behind the battle and the fighting
 537  with their charioteer and the intricate chariot;
 538  these carried him toward the city, groaning heavily,
 539  worn; and the blood ran down from the fresh wound on his arm.
 540  The others fought on, and the quenchless roar rose.
 541  There Aeneas sprang on Aphareus, Caletor's son,
 542  and struck his throat — it was turned toward him — with the sharp spear;
 543  his head dropped to one side, and shield and helmet
 544  crumpled down on him, and death that breaks the spirit poured round him.
 545  And Antilochus, watching Thoon as he turned around,
 546  leapt in and stabbed him, and severed the whole vein
 547  that runs up the length of the back to reach the neck:
 548  the whole of it he severed, and the man dropped on his back in the dust,
 549  stretching both hands out toward his dear companions.
 550  And Antilochus sprang on him and set to stripping the armor
 551  from his shoulders, watching all round; for the Trojans, ringing him,
 552  stabbed at his broad glittering shield from this side and that, yet could not
 553  get through to graze the soft flesh behind it with the pitiless bronze —
 554  the flesh of Antilochus; for Poseidon the earth-shaker
 555  guarded Nestor's son on every side, even amid the thick missiles.
 556  For never was he out of reach of enemies; he wheeled
 557  among them; and his spear was never held still, but always
 558  shaken, quivering; and his aim was set in his mind
 559  either to cast at someone, or to charge in close.
 560  But as he aimed through the crowd he did not go unseen
 561  by Adamas, Asius's son, who leapt in close and stabbed
 562  the middle of his shield with the sharp bronze; but the blue-maned
 563  Poseidon made the point strengthless, grudging him that life.[25]
 564  And half of it stuck there, like a fire-hardened stake,
 565  in the shield of Antilochus, and half lay on the ground;
 566  and Adamas drew back into the band of his companions, dodging doom.
 567  But Meriones, following as he went, threw the spear
 568  and hit him between the genitals and the navel — there
 569  where the war-god comes most grievous to wretched mortals.
 570  There the spear stuck fast; and doubled over the shaft
 571  he writhed, as a bull writhes that herdsmen in the mountains
 572  have bound with willow-ropes and drag along unwilling:
 573  so, stabbed, he writhed a little while — not long —
 574  until the hero Meriones came close and wrenched
 575  the spear out of his flesh; and darkness covered his eyes.
 576  And Helenus struck Deïpyrus close in, on the temple,
 577  with a great Thracian sword, and sheared the helmet away;
 578  it was knocked spinning to the ground, and some Achaean
 579  picked it up as it rolled between the fighters' feet;
 580  and gloomy night came down and covered his eyes.
 581  Then grief seized Atreus' son, Menelaus good at the war-cry,
 582  and he strode out threatening against the hero, lord Helenus,
 583  shaking his sharp spear; and Helenus drew back the grip of his bow.
 584  So the two came on together, the one hot to cast
 585  the sharp-pointed spear, the other the arrow off the string.
 586  Priam's son struck him first, with the arrow, on the chest,
 587  on the corslet's plate — and the bitter arrow sprang off.
 588  As off a broad winnowing-shovel, on a great threshing-floor,
 589  the dark-skinned beans or the chickpeas jump
 590  under the shrill wind and the winnower's swing,
 591  so from the corslet of glorious Menelaus
 592  the bitter arrow glanced far off, turned hard away.[26]
 593  But Atreus' son, Menelaus good at the war-cry, struck
 594  the hand in which he held the polished bow: and into the bow
 595  the bronze spear drove, clean through the hand.
 596  He drew back into the band of his companions, dodging doom,
 597  his hand hanging slack at his side, dragging the ash spear.
 598  Great-hearted Agenor drew it out of the hand,
 599  and bound the hand itself in a sling of twisted
 600  sheep's wool, which his aide carried for the shepherd of the people.[27]
 601  Then Peisander came straight at glorious Menelaus —
 602  but an evil fate was leading him toward death's end:
 603  toward you, Menelaus, to be broken in the dread combat.
 604  And when they were close, advancing on one another,
 605  Atreus' son missed his cast; the spear was turned aside;
 606  and Peisander stabbed at the shield of glorious Menelaus
 607  but could not drive the bronze on through:
 608  the broad shield held it, and the spear snapped
 609  at the socket. Yet he rejoiced in his heart, and hoped for victory.
 610  But Atreus' son, drawing his silver-studded sword,
 611  sprang at Peisander; and he, from under his shield, pulled out
 612  a fine axe of good bronze on a haft of olive-wood,
 613  long and well-polished; and in the same instant they reached each other.
 614  Peisander chopped at the ridge of the horsehair-crested helmet
 615  at its very peak, under the plume; Menelaus caught him coming on
 616  at the forehead, above the base of the nose: the bones cracked loud,
 617  and his eyes, all blood, dropped in the dust by his feet,
 618  and he doubled over and fell. And setting a heel on his chest
 619  Menelaus stripped his armor, and spoke in triumph over him:
 620  "This is how you will leave the ships of the swift-colted Danaans —
 621  you Trojans past all pride, never sated with the terrible war-shout —
 622  nor short of any other outrage and disgrace,
 623  like the outrage you did to me, you foul dogs, with never a fear
 624  in your hearts for the harsh wrath of loud-thundering Zeus
 625  of Guest-right, who one day will destroy your steep city.
 626  You who went off — wantonly — carrying away my wedded wife
 627  and much of my wealth, when it was she who had made you welcome;
 628  and now you rage to hurl annihilating fire
 629  on the seafaring ships, and to kill the Achaean heroes.
 630  But you will be stopped, somewhere, war-hungry as you are.
 631  O father Zeus — they say you stand above all others
 632  in wisdom, men and gods alike; yet all this comes from you:
 633  what favor you are showing to men of violence —
 634  these Trojans, whose fury is forever reckless, who cannot
 635  get their fill of the din of leveling war!
 636  Of all things there is satiety — of sleep, of love,
 637  of sweet song and of blameless dancing;
 638  a man would sooner pray to take his fill of these
 639  than of war. But the Trojans are never sated with battle."[28]
 640  So speaking, blameless Menelaus stripped the bloody armor
 641  from the body and gave it to his companions,
 642  and went back himself again and mixed with the front-fighters.
 643  There the son of king Pylaemenes leapt out at him,
 644  Harpalion, who had followed his dear father to fight
 645  at Troy, and never came home again to his fatherland.
 646  He stabbed the middle of Atreus' son's shield with his spear,
 647  close in, but could not drive the bronze on through,
 648  and drew back into the band of his companions, dodging doom,
 649  glancing on every side, lest flesh of his meet bronze.
 650  But Meriones, as he retreated, shot a bronze-tipped arrow
 651  and hit him in the right buttock; and the arrow
 652  passed under the bone, straight on, into the bladder.
 653  Sinking down on the spot, in the arms of his dear companions,
 654  breathing out his life, he lay stretched on the ground
 655  like a worm; and the dark blood ran out and soaked the earth.[29]
 656  Around him the great-hearted Paphlagonians busied themselves:
 657  they lifted him into a chariot and drove him toward holy Ilion,
 658  grieving; and among them went his father, shedding tears;
 659  and there was no blood-price for his son who was dead.
 660  Now at his killing the heart of Paris was angered exceedingly,
 661  for he had been his guest-friend among the many Paphlagonians;
 662  and angered for him, he let fly a bronze-tipped arrow.
 663  There was one Euchenor, the son of the seer Polyidus,
 664  a rich man and a brave one, whose house was in Corinth,
 665  who had boarded his ship knowing well his deadly fate:
 666  for the good old man Polyidus had told him, often,
 667  that he would waste to death of a grievous sickness at home,
 668  or go down under Trojan hands among the ships of the Achaeans;
 669  and so he shunned two things at once: the heavy fine
 670  of the Achaeans, and the loathsome sickness, that his heart might feel no pain.[30]
 671  Him Paris struck below the jaw, under the ear; and quickly the spirit
 672  went out of his limbs, and the hateful darkness took him.
 673  So they fought on, like blazing fire;
 674  and Hector, dear to Zeus, had not heard, and did not know at all
 675  that on the left of the ships his people were being cut down
 676  by the Argives — and soon the triumph would have belonged
 677  to the Achaeans: such was the holder of the earth, the earth-shaker,
 678  driving the Argives on, and defending them besides with his strength.
 679  But Hector held where first he had leapt the gates and the wall,
 680  breaking the close-set battle-lines of Danaan shield-men,
 681  there where the ships of Ajax and Protesilaus
 682  were drawn up on the beach of the grey sea, and above them
 683  the wall was built at its lowest — there where beyond all places
 684  the men themselves and their horses were fiercest in the fight.
 685  There the Boeotians, and the Ionians with their trailing tunics,[31]
 686  the Locrians, and the Phthians, and the shining Epeians
 687  held him off the ships as he charged hard, but had not strength
 688  to thrust him back away from them — brilliant Hector like a flame —
 689  the picked men of the Athenians among them; and their leader
 690  was Menestheus, Peteos' son, and with him followed
 691  Pheidas and Stichius and brave Bias; and the Epeians' leaders
 692  were Meges, Phyleus' son, and Amphion and Dracius,
 693  and before the Phthians, Medon, and Podarces steadfast in battle.
 694  Now the one — Medon — was a bastard son of godlike Oïleus
 695  and brother of Ajax; but he lived in Phylace,
 696  far from his own fatherland, having killed a man,
 697  a kinsman of his stepmother Eriopis, wife of Oïleus;[32]
 698  and the other was a son of Iphiclus, Phylacus's line.
 699  These, armed in the front of the great-hearted Phthians,
 700  fought in defense of the ships beside the Boeotians;
 701  and Ajax, the swift son of Oïleus, would no longer
 702  stand apart from Ajax, Telamon's son — not by a step:
 703  but as in fallow land two wine-dark oxen strain
 704  at the jointed plow with one and the same heart, and around
 705  the roots of their horns the sweat wells up in streams,
 706  and only the polished yoke holds the two apart
 707  as they drive down the furrow, and the plow cuts to the field's edge:
 708  so the two of them took their stand, shoulder to shoulder.[33]
 709  But the son of Telamon had troops with him, many and brave,
 710  companions who took his shield from him
 711  whenever weariness and sweat came over his knees;
 712  while no Locrians followed the great-hearted son of Oïleus:
 713  their hearts had no stomach for the standing fight,
 714  for they had no bronze helmets, thick with horsehair,
 715  no round shields and no ash spears:
 716  it was trusting in their bows, and in the twisted wool
 717  of sheep, that they had followed him to Ilion; and with these,
 718  shooting thick and fast, they broke the Trojan battle-lines.
 719  So then: the ones in front, in their intricate war-gear,
 720  fought the Trojans and Hector of the bronze helmet,
 721  while the others shot from behind, unseen; and the Trojans
 722  lost all memory of the joy of battle: the arrows drove them into rout.[34]
 723  And then, in misery, back from the ships and the huts
 724  the Trojans would have retreated to windy Ilion,
 725  had not Polydamas come and stood by daring Hector:
 726  "Hector, there is no dealing with you: no man's persuasion moves you.
 727  Because a god gave you the works of war beyond others,
 728  you insist on knowing counsel, too, better than others;
 729  but you cannot possibly take all things to yourself at once.
 730  To one man the god has given the works of war,
 731  to another the dance, to another the lyre and song,
 732  and in another's chest wide-seeing Zeus sets a mind
 733  of worth, and many men get profit of it,
 734  and it saves many, and he who has it knows it best of all.[35]
 735  But I will say it as it seems to me to be best.
 736  Everywhere around you the garland of war is blazing;
 737  and the great-hearted Trojans, now they have crossed the wall,
 738  are some of them standing back with their arms, and some fighting,
 739  fewer against more, scattered along the ships.
 740  Fall back, then, and summon all our best men here;
 741  and then we could weigh the whole counsel together —
 742  whether to fall on the many-benched ships, if the god
 743  is willing to hand us the power, or whether after all
 744  to come away from the ships unhurt. For I, for one, fear
 745  the Achaeans may pay us back yesterday's debt in full:
 746  a man waits by the ships insatiate of war,
 747  and I think he will hold back from the fighting no longer."
 748  So spoke Polydamas, and the prudent word pleased Hector,
 749  and at once he leapt with his weapons from the chariot to the ground,
 750  and speaking winged words he addressed him:
 751  "Polydamas — you hold all our best men here,
 752  I will go over there and take my share of the fighting,
 753  and come back quickly, when I have given them their orders."
 754  He spoke, and set off — like a snow-shining mountain —[36]
 755  screaming, and flew through the Trojans and the allies.
 756  And they all surged toward Panthous's son, gracious Polydamas,
 757  when they heard the voice of Hector.
 758  But he went ranging through the front-fighters, searching
 759  for Deiphobus, and the strength of lord Helenus,
 760  and Adamas, Asius's son, and Asius, Hyrtacus's son —
 761  if he might find them anywhere. He found them
 762  not unhurt, not unruined: some by the sterns
 763  of the Achaean ships lay dead at Argive hands,
 764  and some were inside the wall with wounds from far or near.
 765  But one he found quickly, on the left of the tearful battle:
 766  brilliant Alexandros, the husband of lovely-haired Helen,
 767  heartening his companions and urging them to fight.
 768  Standing close, he raked him with shaming words:
 769  "Paris — evil Paris — beauty and nothing more, woman-mad, seducer:[37]
 770  where is Deiphobus, and the strength of lord Helenus,
 771  and Adamas, Asius's son, and Asius, Hyrtacus's son?
 772  And where is Othryoneus? Now all of steep Ilion
 773  is ruined, top to bottom. Now your own sheer destruction is sure."
 774  Then godlike Alexandros answered him:
 775  "Hector — since your heart is set on blaming the blameless,
 776  some other day, perhaps, I may hang back from the fighting;
 777  but not now: my mother did not bear me wholly a coward.
 778  From the moment you woke the battle of our companions at the ships,
 779  from then we have stood our ground here, engaging the Danaans
 780  without respite. The companions you ask after are dead —
 781  except Deiphobus and the strength of lord Helenus:
 782  those two have gone off, each wounded in the hand
 783  by long spears; the son of Cronos kept death from them.
 784  Now lead on, wherever your heart and spirit command,
 785  and we will follow you, and gladly; nor do I think
 786  you will find us short of courage, so far as the strength is in us.
 787  Beyond his strength no man can fight, however hot he burns."
 788  So speaking the hero turned his brother's mind.
 789  They set off for where the battle and struggle burned thickest,
 790  around Cebriones, and blameless Polydamas,
 791  and Phalces, and Orthaeus, and godlike Polyphetes,
 792  and Palmys, and Ascanius and Morys, sons of Hippotion,
 793  who had come in relief out of deep-soiled Ascania
 794  the dawn before; and now Zeus drove them to fight.
 795  And they came on like a squall of punishing winds
 796  that under the thunder of father Zeus drives at the plain
 797  and with a prodigious roar meets the sea, and the countless
 798  seething waves of the loud-roaring water
 799  arch up, white-crested, some in front, and others behind them:
 800  so the Trojans, closed up, some in front, and others behind them,
 801  glittering in bronze, came on behind their leaders.
 802  And Hector led them, the equal of Ares bane of mortals,
 803  Priam's son; before him he held his shield, balanced every way,
 804  thick with hides, with heavy bronze hammered over it,
 805  and about his temples the shining helmet shook.
 806  Everywhere he probed the battle-lines, advancing,
 807  trying whether they might give before him as he came under shield-cover;
 808  but he could not confound the heart in the Achaeans' chests.
 809  And Ajax, striding long, was first to challenge him:
 810  "Possessed one — come closer! Why try to frighten Argives
 811  with mere noise? We are not unschooled in battle;
 812  it is the evil whip of Zeus that has broken us Achaeans.
 813  I suppose your heart hopes to strip the ships clean —
 814  well, we too have hands, quick hands, to hold them.
 815  Long before that, mark me, your own well-peopled city
 816  will be taken under our hands, and sacked.
 817  And for you yourself, I say the hour is near when, running,
 818  you will pray to father Zeus and the other immortals
 819  to make your fair-maned horses swifter than falcons
 820  as they carry you to the city, raising the dust of the plain."
 821  And as he spoke, a bird flew by on the right hand,
 822  an eagle, flying high; and the Achaean army shouted at it,
 823  heartened by the sign. But glorious Hector answered:
 824  "Ajax, you blundering ox of a braggart — what a thing to say!
 825  I wish I were as surely, all my days, the son
 826  of aegis-bearing Zeus, and the lady Hera had borne me,
 827  and I were honored as Athena and Apollo are honored,
 828  as surely as this day now brings evil to the Argives —
 829  all of them; and you will be killed among them, if you dare
 830  stand against my long spear, which will tear open
 831  your lily-soft skin; and you will glut the dogs and birds of Troy[38]
 832  with your fat and your flesh, fallen beside the ships of the Achaeans."
 833  So speaking he led the way, and they followed him
 834  with a prodigious roar, and the army screamed behind him.
 835  And the Argives on the other side raised their cry, and did not forget
 836  their courage, but stood fast against the best of the Trojans coming on;
 837  and the clamor of both reached the ether and the radiance of Zeus.[39]

NOTES

[1] (13.1-9) Zeus, sure of his arrangements, turns his shining eyes
    away from the war — north, to the horse-herding Thracians, the
    close-fighting Mysians, and then clean off the map of the war:
    the Hippemolgoi, "Mare-milkers who drink the milk," and the
    Abioi, "most righteous of men." The lines are the most famous
    ethnographic glance in Homer — steppe pastoralists (later
    identified with Scythians; Ammianus Marcellinus was still
    quoting the verse in the fourth century AD) whose remoteness
    from the war is precisely their righteousness. Zeus rests his
    eyes on the people who own least and quarrel least — and his
    inattention is the hinge of the plot: no god, he thinks, will
    dare (7-9). One will.

[2] (13.17-22) Poseidon's descent: "the tall hills trembled, and
    the timber, under the immortal feet of Poseidon as he went.
    Three strides he made, and with the fourth he reached his goal."
    Longinus quotes the passage in On the Sublime (9.8) as an
    instance of the divine measured by cosmic stride. Aegae, his
    golden house under the water, was paired with Helice at 8.203;
    Samothrace ("Thracian Samos"), the north-Aegean peak, really
    does command a sight-line over Imbros to the Troad — the poet's
    geography of watching is checkable, and correct.

[3] (13.23-31) The ride: gold horses, gold whip, gold armor — and
    then the great verse of the animal world's homage: "the
    sea-beasts gamboled beneath him, coming from every hollow, and
    did not fail to know their lord; and the sea in its joy stood
    apart." The wave makes way; the axle stays dry. After eleven
    books of the sea as grey backdrop for grief, one god's commute
    turns it joyful — the Iliad's single moment of happy nature.

[4] (13.66-75) The lesser Ajax detects the god from the way he
    moves: "the trace of his feet and legs from behind, as he went
    off, I knew it easily — the gods are easy to know" (ἀρίγνωτοι
    δὲ θεοί περ). Divine disguise deceives the face-to-face view
    only; gait betrays godhead. The other symptom is inward: both
    Ajaxes' hands and feet go "hungry" for battle — possession
    described from inside the possessed.

[5] (13.95-124) Poseidon's rebuke, pivoting on the poem's political
    nerve: even if Agamemnon is "wholly to blame... because he
    dishonored the swift-footed son of Peleus, we, for our part,
    have no leave to slacken" — and then the great clause: "let us
    heal it, quickly. The minds of good men can be healed" (ἀκεσταί
    τοι φρένες ἐσθλῶν, 115). The god concedes the grievance and
    dissolves its authority in nine words — the whole ethics of the
    quarrel-plot, and the sentence Achilles will not hear until
    Book 19.

[6] (13.126-135) The locked phalanx: "buckler pressed on buckler,
    helmet on helmet, man on man, and the horsehair crests on the
    bright ridges touched as they nodded." The lines (131-133)
    recur at 16.215-217 when the Myrmidons form up — reuse this
    English there. Whatever the date of hoplite tactics, this is
    the sensation of close order: equipment touching equipment
    before any enemy is met.

[7] (13.136-146) Hector as the boulder: torn loose by a river in
    flood, bounding, the woods crashing beneath it — "until it
    reaches the level plain, and there it rolls no more, for all
    its rush." The simile carries its own prophecy: the stone's
    momentum is the cliff's gift, not the stone's, and the plain is
    waiting. Hector jams against the close-set lines within five
    lines of it.

[8] (13.170-205) Imbrius, the son-in-law from Pedaeum who came back
    to Troy when the war began and was honored by Priam "like his
    own children" — another of the poem's in-law obituaries (cf.
    Iphidamas, Othryoneus, Alcathous: Troy's defense is a web of
    marriages). He falls like an ash on a mountain crest cut down
    by bronze. Then the book's most brutal image: the lesser Ajax
    cuts the head from the corpse "in wrath for Amphimachus" and
    bowls it through the crowd "like a ball," to stop in the dust
    at Hector's feet. Wrath answered with sport: the small Ajax's
    savagery is a fixed trait (cf. 23.473-498, and his end in the
    Odyssey), and the head at Hector's feet is a delivery with an
    address on it.

[9] (13.206-209) Amphimachus was Cteatus's son — a grandson of the
    Moliones pair whom Poseidon fathered and once rescued from
    Nestor (11.750-752). So the god's rage here is family grief:
    the Iliad's gods enter its casualty-lists two generations
    removed, and Poseidon's next intervention (note [25]) is
    likewise a grandfather's grudge.

[10] (13.232-238) Poseidon-as-Thoas: on the day's deserter, "may he
    be sport for dogs"; then the recruiting proverb — "Even sorry
    men, combined, come to some courage — and the two of us would
    know how to fight even the best." The arithmetic of the wall
    (12.412) again: courage as a sum, spoken by a god enlisting one
    tired middle-aged king.

[11] (13.249) "Meriones, Molus's son": Molus received the boar's-
    tusk helmet in its pedigree (10.269-270) — the same household's
    war-gear economy continues here, an entire scene about where
    spears come from. Idomeneus and Meriones bicker with perfect
    courtesy — the older man teasing, the younger standing on his
    record — before the god-stirred battle lets either spend it.

[12] (13.260-294) The armory speech: twenty Trojan spears against
    the bright wall of the hut, "which I strip from the men I
    kill," because Idomeneus does "not fight standing far from
    enemy men." Then the great set-piece (275-286): the ambush as
    the assay of worth — the coward's color running, his crouch
    shifting from foot to foot, the heart hammering on the ribs,
    the teeth chattering aloud; the brave man's color "never
    changes," and his one prayer is to be mixed in the fighting at
    once. It is the poem's fullest psychology of fear, set not in
    the charge but in the waiting — and given to the oldest
    front-fighter in the army.

[13] (13.291) "the front-fighters' trysting-place" — ὀαριστύς, the
    word for lovers' intimate talk (cf. 22.127-128, where Hector
    uses it of a boy and girl whispering), applied to the range
    where chests meet spears. The battlefield as terrible intimacy:
    one of the boldest single words in the Iliad.

[14] (13.298-305) Ares and Panic (Phobos, his son, the capitalized
    personification of 4.440) arming out of Thrace against the
    Ephyri or Phlegyes — gods who "do not hear both sides' prayers,
    but hand the glory to one." War-god theology in miniature; and
    an uneasy simile to hang on Idomeneus and Meriones, since Ares
    at this hour is penned on Olympus, deaf to everything — not
    even hearing of his own son's death (note [24]).

[15] (13.339-344) The battle "bristled" (ἔφριξεν) with flesh-
    cutting spears — the field itself gets gooseflesh; eyes are
    blinded by bronze glare. Then the narrator's aside: "It would
    be a hard-hearted man who could rejoice at the sight of that
    toil, and not be grieved." As at 12.176 and 4.539-542, the poem
    turns to check the audience's pleasure — the singer measuring
    the distance between glory sung and toil seen.

[16] (13.345-360) The theological knot of the middle Iliad: Zeus
    wills Trojan victory (to glorify Thetis and her son), yet not
    Achaean annihilation; Poseidon works the other side "in
    secret... likened to a man" — because "both were of one stock
    and one parentage, but Zeus was elder-born, and he knew more."
    Primogeniture as cosmology (restated by force in 15.165-167,
    and contested at 15.185-199). And so the two sons of Cronos
    "knotted the cord of strong strife and leveling war, and drew
    it taut over both sides — a cord unbreakable, not to be untied;
    and it unstrung the knees of many": the tug-of-war image of
    battle (cf. 7.102, 11.336) tied to its grimmest clause — the
    knot the gods cannot break is loosed instead in men's knees.
    Aristarchus athetized 350 (the Thetis line) as needless; the
    printed text keeps it.

[17] (13.363-382) Othryoneus, war-bridegroom: he bid for Cassandra
    — her first appearance in the poem, "loveliest of Priam's
    daughters," with no word of prophecy, which is post-Homeric —
    "without bride-gifts," offering instead the war itself as
    bride-price. Idomeneus kills him mid-stride and conducts the
    poem's cruelest wedding-negotiation over the body: we'll give
    you Agamemnon's loveliest daughter "if you will join us and
    sack the well-peopled city of Ilion. Come along: let us settle
    the marriage at the seafaring ships — you will find we are not
    hard men over bride-terms." The marriage-economics of death
    (Iphidamas, 11.241-245) turned entirely to mockery: the dead
    man dragged by the foot toward his "wedding."

[18] (13.389-393) Asius falls "as an oak falls, or a white poplar,
    or a towering pine, that carpenters up in the mountains cut
    down with fresh-whetted axes to be a ship's timber" — paying
    the death-notice filed at 12.116-117, killed by the very man it
    named. The simile recurs verbatim at 16.482-484 for Sarpedon —
    reuse this English there. Man as felled timber "to be a ship's
    timber": the war's dead as material for the very machines that
    brought the war.

[19] (13.402-416) Deiphobus's vaunt for Hypsenor: Asius shall not
    lie unavenged — "even going down to Hades, the strong closer of
    the gate, he will be glad at heart: I have given him an escort
    for the road." The dead man as travel-arrangement; the poem's
    bookkeeping of vengeance (ποινή) spoken as courtesy. (In fact
    Hypsenor's death-wound is described and mourned — but the poet
    never says he died; ancient critics worried the point.)

[20] (13.424-444) Alcathous: the poem's most terrible intervention.
    Poseidon "charmed his shining eyes and shackled his glorious
    limbs" — the god holds the man still, "like a grave-pillar or a
    tree in high leaf," while Idomeneus spears him through the
    chest. The bridegroom-obituary again (Hippodameia, who outshone
    her age-mates "in beauty and in handiwork and in wit"); the
    grave-stele simile stands the man's own monument up in advance;
    and the heart, still beating, shakes the butt-end of the spear
    — the most clinically horrifying detail in the poem. A god
    fighting for the Achaeans does this: the poem does not flinch,
    and does not comment.

[21] (13.446-454) Idomeneus's ledger ("three for one") and pedigree:
    Zeus - Minos "warden of Crete" - Deucalion - Idomeneus. The
    Cretan king's genealogy is the shortest distance in the poem
    between Zeus and a living fighter — three generations — and he
    ends it flatly: the ships have carried me here, "an evil to
    you, and your father, and the rest of the Trojans."

[22] (13.459-461) Aeneas found sulking, "rearmost of the crowd, for
    he was forever furious at brilliant Priam, because, brave among
    men as he was, Priam gave him no honor." The Achilles-pattern
    in Trojan miniature: worth unhonored by the king, service
    withheld. The grievance (never elsewhere explained) belongs to
    the junior branch of the royal house — cf. 20.178-183, where
    Achilles taunts him with hoping to inherit Priam's rank, and
    20.302-308, where Poseidon prophesies that his line, not
    Priam's, will rule the Trojans hereafter.

[23] (13.489-495) Aeneas's men follow "as flocks follow the
    bell-ram down from the pasture to drink — and the shepherd's
    heart is glad": war-following rendered as the tamest scene the
    poem knows, with the commander's joy set inside the shepherd's.
    The gentlest simile in a savage book.

[24] (13.518-525) Deiphobus's cast kills Ascalaphus, "son of
    Enyalius" (Ares' son by the Catalogue, 2.512) — "but
    loud-roaring massive Ares knew nothing of it yet... he sat on
    the peak of Olympus, under the golden clouds, penned there by
    the designs of Zeus." A father's ignorance held in suspension
    across two books: at 15.110-142 Ares learns, arms for revenge
    against Zeus's edict, and must be forced down by Athena. The
    quiet lines here are the fuse.

[25] (13.554-575) Poseidon shields Antilochus (Nestor's son — the
    god's affection for the house of Neleus again) and makes
    Adamas's spear-point "strengthless... grudging him that life."
    Adamas dies under Meriones' throw "between the genitals and
    the navel — there where the war-god comes most grievous to
    wretched mortals" — the poem's plainest statement about wounds
    and pain — writhing "as a bull writhes that herdsmen in the
    mountains have bound with willow-ropes and drag along
    unwilling." The pastoral world keeps arriving as the measure
    of agony.

[26] (13.586-592) Helenus's arrow springs off Menelaus's corslet
    "as off a broad winnowing-shovel, on a great threshing-floor,
    the dark-skinned beans or the chickpeas jump under the shrill
    wind and the winnower's swing." Harvest again — the missile as
    chaff-work; the deadly and the domestic in one bounce. The
    duel itself is asymmetric: spear against bow, and the spear
    pins the bow-hand to the bow.

[27] (13.598-600) The wounded hand is bound "in a sling of twisted
    sheep's wool" — the σφενδόνη, the Iliad's only named sling,
    here as bandage; at 716 the same twisted wool arms the Locrians
    as a weapon. One humble implement, both uses, thirty lines
    apart: the poem's quartermaster memory at work.

[28] (13.620-639) Menelaus over Peisander (killed in the poem's one
    axe-against-sword exchange, his eyes dropped bloody in the
    dust): the wronged husband's theology. The Trojans are indicted
    under "the harsh wrath of loud-thundering Zeus of Guest-right"
    (Ζηνὸς ξεινίου... μῆνιν — the poem's theme-word "wrath" given
    to Zeus as protector of hospitality, the war's whole legal
    basis in one phrase); then the strange, wonderful complaint:
    "Of all things there is satiety — of sleep, of love, of sweet
    song and of blameless dancing; a man would sooner pray to take
    his fill of these than of war. But the Trojans are never sated
    with battle." The catalogue of things men can have enough of
    is the poem's shyest confession of what peace is for. Ancient
    critics athetized here (637); readers have loved the passage
    in exact proportion.

[29] (13.643-659) Harpalion dies "like a worm... stretched on the
    ground" — the poem's smallest death-simile, for a boy who
    followed his father to war. And then the famous impossibility:
    "among them went his father, shedding tears" — his father is
    Pylaemenes, killed by Menelaus at 5.576-579 in our own text.
    Ancient critics athetized 658-659 to heal the contradiction;
    moderns treat it as the clearest seam of oral composition —
    the poet reaching for the pathos-formula (a father behind a
    bier) without an audit of Book 5. The translation keeps the
    printed text and the wound. The closing line — "and there was
    no blood-price for his son who was dead" — prices the loss in
    the currency the poem uses everywhere (cf. 9.632-636, 18.498).

[30] (13.663-672) Euchenor of Corinth, the seer's son, who sailed
    "knowing well his deadly fate": his father had told him he
    would die of sickness at home or under Trojan hands at the
    ships. He chose the spear — "and so he shunned two things at
    once: the heavy fine of the Achaeans, and the loathsome
    sickness." The precise double of Achilles' two fates (9.410-
    416) at commoner's scale — and with the poem's driest touch,
    the war-death is also a tax dodge: the θωή, the fine levied on
    draft-evaders, listed in one breath with mortal disease. He is
    killed by Paris, absent-mindedly, in someone else's revenge.

[31] (13.685-700) The defense roster: Boeotians, Locrians,
    Phthians, Epeians, picked Athenians — and "the Ionians with
    their trailing tunics" (Ἰάονες ἑλκεχίτωνες), the name's only
    occurrence in Homer, applied to Athenian-led troops: a single
    audible seam between the poem's world and the poet's own
    Ionian one.

[32] (13.694-697) Medon, bastard son of Oïleus, exiled from his
    fatherland "having killed a man, a kinsman of his stepmother
    Eriopis": the homicide-exile is epic's standard hard-luck
    biography (cf. Patroclus, 23.85-88). These four lines recur
    verbatim at 15.333-336, where Medon dies — the poem files a
    man's whole story twice, once at his post and once at his
    death. Reuse this English there.

[33] (13.701-708) The two Ajaxes as "two wine-dark oxen" (βόε
    οἴνοπε — the sea's own color-word given to plow-beasts)
    straining at one plow, sweat welling at the roots of their
    horns, the polished yoke alone holding them apart: the poem's
    great image of joint labor — heroic partnership rendered as
    agriculture, down the furrow to the field's edge.

[34] (13.712-722) The Locrian way of war: no helmets, no shields,
    no ash spears — bows and wool slings, shooting "from behind,
    unseen," while the armored line fights in front. Combined
    arms, and the breaking of the Trojan rush credited to the
    missile troops the heroic code barely deigns to see (cf.
    11.385-390): the poem knows exactly what wins battles, and
    says so here without editorializing.

[35] (13.726-747) Polydamas's third counsel — accepted, like the
    first: the famous division of gifts. "To one man the god has
    given the works of war, to another the dance, to another the
    lyre and song, and in another's chest wide-seeing Zeus sets a
    mind of worth... But you cannot possibly take all things to
    yourself at once." (Line 731 — dance and song — was omitted by
    Aristarchus; Zenodotus kept it, Lucian quoted it, and the
    printed text prints it.) The tactical advice — regroup, count
    heads, decide — carries a shadow-clause: "a man waits by the
    ships insatiate of war," the book's only reference to
    Achilles as a standing threat, from the man who is always
    right.

[36] (13.754) Hector sets off "like a snow-shining mountain" —
    ὄρεϊ νιφόεντι ἐοικώς: a man like a mountain, and a moving one;
    ancient and modern readers have puzzled at it (a mass of
    glittering bronze? a peak walking?). It is the largest thing
    Hector is ever compared to, on the day his rush crests.

[37] (13.769) "Paris — evil Paris — beauty and nothing more,
    woman-mad, seducer": Hector's rebuke repeats 3.39 word for
    word, our English kept. There it opened the poem's first duel,
    here its last day of Trojan victory — and Paris's answer this
    time is measured, brave, and just ("your heart is set on
    blaming the blameless" — the very phrase Patroclus used of
    Achilles, 11.654), ending on the proverb "Beyond his strength
    no man can fight, however hot he burns." The poem allows Paris
    his best moment exactly where Hector repeats his worst line.

[38] (13.809-832) The exchange before the charge: Ajax's taunt
    promises Hector a prayer for horses "swifter than falcons,"
    and the eagle confirms it on the right; Hector's reply is the
    strangest oath-wish in the poem — "I wish I were as surely,
    all my days, the son of aegis-bearing Zeus... as this day is
    bringing evil on the Argives": certainty of today traded
    against an impossible divinity, from the man who cannot know
    his warrant expires at sundown. His threat gives Ajax "lily-
    soft skin" (χρόα λειριόεντα) — the flower-word for the flesh
    of the enemy's biggest man; and "you blundering ox of a
    braggart" (βουγάϊε) is one of the poem's rare pure insults,
    coarse as a barnyard.

[39] (13.837) The last line: "and the clamor of both reached the
    ether and the radiance of Zeus." The book that began with Zeus
    turning his shining eyes away ends with the battle arriving at
    him as sound — αἰθέρα καὶ Διὸς αὐγάς, the noise climbing into
    the very brightness he looked away with. The ring closes: he
    will look back down at 15.4, and the correction will be
    immediate and total.
