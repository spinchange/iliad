BOOK 17

The fight over the body of Patroclus: Hector puts on the armor of
Achilles, the immortal horses weep, and Ajax prays not for rescue
but for light.

TRANSLATOR'S COMMENTARY

Book 17 is the Iliad's longest tug-of-war: one dead man at the
center, and the whole day's war reorganized around the question of
who will hold him. The poem frames it in similes of guardianship
and labor rather than glory — Menelaus over the body "as a mother-
cow, lowing, stands over her first calf" (note [1]); the corpse
dragged this way and that "as men stretch a great bull's hide
soaked in fat," the tanning-yard come to the battlefield (note
[21]); the rescue at last like mules hauling a ship-timber down a
rocky path, the two Ajaxes behind it like a wooded ridge damming a
flood (note [37]). Against these stand the book's two heights of
theology. Zeus watches Hector put on the stripped armor of
Achilles and shakes his head: the gear "not taken by right," the
power granted as ποινή — blood-price paid in glory to a man who
will never come home from the battle, "nor will Andromache take
from you the famous armor of Peleus' son" (notes [12], [13]). And
Zeus watches the immortal horses stand like a grave-stone, manes
in the dust, weeping for their charioteer, and asks the poem's
deepest question of his own gift: why did we give you to a mortal,
"you who are ageless and deathless? Was it so that you too might
have sorrows among unhappy men? For there is nothing anywhere more
pitiful than man, of all things that breathe and creep upon the
earth" (notes [25], [26]).

The book's people are its second glory: Euphorbus dead with his
Graces' hair in the blood and the olive-sapling simile that
Pythagoras's biographers never stopped quoting (note [4]); Glaucus
proposing to trade corpse for corpse, Patroclus for Sarpedon —
bodies as the war's currency (note [10]); the anonymous soldiers'
paired vows (note [23]); Antilochus struck speechless by the news
he must carry (note [35]); and Ajax, in the god-sent mist, making
the request Longinus judged the most sublime in literature — not
life, but light: "and in the light — kill us, since that is now
your pleasure" (note [33]).

   1  Nor did it escape Atreus' son, Menelaus dear to Ares,
   2  that Patroclus had been broken by Trojans in the combat.
   3  He came through the front-fighters helmed in blazing bronze
   4  and stood over the body — as a mother-cow, lowing,
   5  stands over her first calf, who never knew birth before:[1]
   6  so tawny-haired Menelaus stood over Patroclus.
   7  He held his spear before him, and his shield, balanced every way,
   8  hot to kill whatever man might come against him.
   9  Nor was Panthous's son of the good ash spear careless
  10  of blameless Patroclus fallen: he came and stood
  11  close by, and spoke to Menelaus dear to Ares:
  12  "Son of Atreus — Zeus-nurtured Menelaus, marshal of the people —
  13  give way: leave the body; let the bloody spoils be.
  14  For no man of the Trojans or their famed allies
  15  hit Patroclus with the spear in the strong encounter before me:[2]
  16  so let me take my good fame among the Trojans,
  17  or I will strike you and take your honey-sweet life."
  18  Then, deeply angered, tawny-haired Menelaus answered him:
  19  "Father Zeus — how ugly a thing is arrogant boasting!
  20  Not the leopard's fury is so great, nor the lion's,
  21  nor the murderous wild boar's — whose heart in his chest
  22  is the biggest of all, glorying in his strength —
  23  as the pride of the sons of Panthous with their good ash spears.
  24  Yet the strength of Hyperenor, breaker of horses, had no joy
  25  of his youth, when he mocked me and stood against me
  26  and called me the sorriest fighter of the Danaans:
  27  I say his own feet never carried him home
  28  to delight his dear wife and his devoted parents.
  29  Just so, I promise you, I will unstring your strength, if you stand
  30  against me. Go back into the crowd — that is my counsel —
  31  and do not stand against me, before some evil
  32  takes you. When the thing is done, even the fool learns."
  33  So he spoke, and did not persuade him; and he answered:
  34  "Now, Zeus-nurtured Menelaus, now you will pay in full[3]
  35  for my brother, whom you killed — and boast of it in speeches —
  36  whose wife you widowed in the depth of her new bridal chamber,
  37  who brought his parents cursed mourning and grief.
  38  I could be some stay of weeping for those unhappy two
  39  if I brought back your head and your armor
  40  and laid them in the hands of Panthous and brilliant Phrontis.
  41  But not for long shall the labor go untried between us,
  42  and unfought — whether it end in courage or in flight."
  43  So speaking, he stabbed at the shield, balanced every way;
  44  but the bronze did not break through: the point bent back
  45  in the strong shield. And second, with the bronze,
  46  rose Atreus' son Menelaus, with a prayer to Zeus the father:
  47  as Euphorbus drew back, he stabbed the base of his throat,
  48  and leaned into the thrust, trusting his heavy hand;
  49  and clean through the soft neck the point ran out.
  50  He fell with a thud, and his armor clattered upon him.
  51  His hair was soaked with blood — hair like the Graces',[4]
  52  and the braids pinched tight in gold and silver.
  53  As a man rears a flourishing olive sapling
  54  in a lonely place, where water wells up in plenty —
  55  a fair shoot, and a thriving; and the breathing of every
  56  wind rocks it, and it breaks thick with white blossom;
  57  but suddenly a wind comes with a great squall
  58  and wrenches it out of its trench and lays it flat on the earth:
  59  such was Panthous's son of the good ash spear, Euphorbus,
  60  when Atreus' son Menelaus killed him, and set to stripping his armor.
  61  And as when some mountain-bred lion, sure of his strength,[5]
  62  snatches from a grazing herd the finest heifer:
  63  her neck he breaks first, seizing it in his strong teeth,
  64  and then he gulps down the blood and all the inward parts,
  65  rending her; and around him the dogs and the herdsmen
  66  raise clamor upon clamor — from a distance: they have no will
  67  to go against him, for green fear has hold of them:
  68  so the heart in the chest of not one of them dared
  69  to go against glorious Menelaus.
  70  And now Atreus' son would easily have carried off the famed armor
  71  of Panthous's son, had not Phoebus Apollo grudged it,
  72  and roused Hector against him, the equal of swift Ares,
  73  in the likeness of a man, Mentes, leader of the Cicones;
  74  and speaking winged words he addressed him:
  75  "Hector — you are running now, chasing what cannot be caught:[6]
  76  the horses of the war-minded son of Aeacus. They are hard
  77  for mortal men to break or to drive,
  78  for any man but Achilles, whom an immortal mother bore.
  79  And meanwhile Menelaus, warlike son of Atreus,
  80  standing over Patroclus, has killed the best of the Trojans —
  81  Panthous's son Euphorbus — and stopped his rushing courage."
  82  So speaking, the god went back into the toil of men;
  83  and terrible grief packed Hector's darkened heart.
  84  He peered along the ranks, and knew at once
  85  the one man stripping the famed armor, the other lying
  86  on the ground, the blood running from the open wound.
  87  He came through the front-fighters helmed in blazing bronze,
  88  screaming sharp, like the unquenchable flame
  89  of Hephaestus. And his sharp cry did not escape Atreus' son;
  90  troubled, he spoke to his own great-hearted spirit:
  91  "Ah me — if I abandon the fine armor,
  92  and Patroclus, who lies here for the sake of my honor,
  93  I fear any Danaan who sees it will call it my disgrace.
  94  But if, in shame, I fight Hector and the Trojans
  95  alone — I fear the many will surround me, one man:
  96  Hector of the flashing helmet is leading all of them here.
  97  But why does my own heart argue these things with me?
  98  When a man wills, against the god's face, to fight a man
  99  whom a god honors — a great woe rolls down on him quickly.
 100  So no Danaan who sees me give way before Hector
 101  will call it my disgrace: he fights out of heaven.
 102  But if I could only hear Ajax of the great war-cry —
 103  the two of us together might go back and remember our war-craft,
 104  even against the god's face, and haul the body clear
 105  for Achilles, Peleus' son. Of evils, that would be best."
 106  While he was turning this over in mind and heart,
 107  the Trojan battle-lines came on; and Hector led them.
 108  And he gave ground backward, and left the body,
 109  turning again and again — like a bearded lion[7]
 110  that dogs and men drive off from the fold
 111  with spears and shouting: the brave heart in his chest
 112  goes stiff with the cold, and unwilling he leaves the yard:
 113  so tawny-haired Menelaus went from Patroclus.
 114  He turned and stood, when he reached the band of his companions,
 115  peering everywhere for great Ajax, Telamon's son;
 116  and he saw him quickly, on the far left of the whole battle,
 117  heartening his companions and driving them to fight,
 118  for Phoebus Apollo had thrown unearthly panic among them.
 119  He set off at a run, and stood by him at once, and spoke:
 120  "Ajax — this way, old friend! Let us hurry for dead Patroclus,
 121  and see if we can bring the body at least to Achilles —
 122  naked: for Hector of the flashing helmet has the armor."
 123  So he spoke, and stirred the heart in war-minded Ajax,
 124  and he came through the front-fighters, and with him tawny-haired Menelaus.
 125  Hector, when he had stripped Patroclus of the famed armor,
 126  was dragging him, to shear the head from the shoulders with the sharp bronze[8]
 127  and haul the body off and give it to the dogs of Troy.
 128  But Ajax came up close, carrying his shield like a tower,
 129  and Hector drew back into the band of his companions
 130  and sprang into his chariot; and he gave the fine armor
 131  to Trojans to carry to the city, to be his own great glory.
 132  And Ajax covered Menoetius's son with his broad shield
 133  and stood — as a lion stands over his young,[9]
 134  a lion leading his cubs, when hunting men come on him
 135  in the timber: he glories in his strength,
 136  and draws the whole scowl of his brows down over his eyes:
 137  so Ajax bestrode the hero Patroclus.
 138  And on the other side Atreus' son, Menelaus dear to Ares,
 139  stood fast, nursing great grief in his chest.
 140  Then Glaucus, Hippolochus's son, leader of the Lycian men,
 141  glaring darkly at Hector, rebuked him with a hard word:
 142  "Hector — best of us all to look at: in battle you fall far short.[10]
 143  Fine fame indeed is yours — a runaway's.
 144  Consider now how you will save your town and citadel
 145  alone — you with the people born in Ilion;
 146  for no Lycian, at least, will go out to fight the Danaans
 147  for your city, since there was no thanks, it seems,
 148  for battling the enemy without respite, forever.
 149  How would you save a lesser man in the press of war,
 150  hard-hearted one, when Sarpedon — your guest-friend and companion —
 151  you abandoned to the Argives, their prey and their prize:
 152  a man who served you greatly, your city and yourself,
 153  while he lived; and now you have not dared to keep the dogs from him.
 154  So now, if any Lycian man will obey me,
 155  we go home — and let sheer destruction show over Troy.
 156  For if the Trojans now had daring fury in them,
 157  unshaking — the kind that comes into men who for their fatherland
 158  take on the toil and the grinding of war against their enemies —
 159  we would quickly haul Patroclus inside Ilion.
 160  And if that man came to the great city of lord Priam,
 161  dead — if we hauled him out of the fighting —
 162  the Argives would quickly ransom back the fine armor
 163  of Sarpedon, and we would bring the man himself into Ilion:
 164  for the aide of such a man lies killed — far the best
 165  of the Argives by the ships, he and his close-fighting aides.
 166  But you had not the courage to stand against great-hearted
 167  Ajax, eye to eye in the enemies' clamor,
 168  and fight him straight — since he is a better man than you."
 169  Glaring at him darkly, Hector of the flashing helmet answered:
 170  "Glaucus — why does a man like you talk so overweening?[11]
 171  Well now! And I always said you were first in judgment
 172  of all who live in Lycia of the deep soil;
 173  but now I scorn your wits outright — such a thing to say:
 174  you say I did not stand against gigantic Ajax.
 175  It is not battle I shudder at, nor the thunder of horses;
 176  but always the mind of Zeus who bears the aegis is stronger:
 177  he sends even the brave man running, and strips away victory
 178  easily — and then again himself drives a man to fight.
 179  Come stand beside me, old friend — watch my work:
 180  see whether all day long I prove the coward you call me,
 181  or whether I stop many a Danaan too, however hot
 182  his courage, from his defense of dead Patroclus."
 183  So speaking, he called to the Trojans with a great shout:
 184  "Trojans and Lycians and Dardanians who fight at close quarters —
 185  be men, my friends; remember your rushing courage,
 186  while I put on the armor of blameless Achilles,
 187  the fine armor I stripped from the strength of Patroclus when I killed him."
 188  So speaking, Hector of the flashing helmet went off
 189  out of the grim war; and running he overtook his companions,
 190  quickly, on swift feet, not yet far away —
 191  the men carrying toward the city the famed armor of Peleus' son.
 192  And standing apart from the battle of many tears, he changed his armor:[12]
 193  his own he gave to the war-loving Trojans to carry
 194  to holy Ilion; and he put on the immortal armor
 195  of Achilles, Peleus' son, which the heavenly gods
 196  had given his dear father; and the father, grown old, passed it
 197  to his son — but the son did not grow old in his father's armor.
 198  And when Zeus the cloud-gatherer saw him from afar
 199  arming himself in the gear of the godlike son of Peleus,
 200  he shook his head, and spoke to his own spirit:
 201  "Ah, poor man — death is nowhere in your thoughts,[13]
 202  and it stands close beside you. You are putting on the immortal
 203  armor of the best of men — a man the others shudder at.
 204  His companion you have killed, who was gentle and strong,
 205  and stripped the armor from his head and shoulders
 206  not by right. Yet for now I will hand you great power —
 207  as recompense for this: that never home out of the battle
 208  will you come, nor Andromache take from you the famous armor of Peleus' son."
 209  He spoke, and the son of Cronos bowed his head with his dark brows;
 210  and he fitted the armor to Hector's body, and Ares entered him,
 211  the terrible war-god, and his limbs were filled inside
 212  with courage and strength. He went out to the famed allies
 213  with a great shout, and showed himself before them all,
 214  blazing in the armor of the great-hearted son of Peleus.
 215  And going among them he stirred each man with his words —
 216  Mesthles and Glaucus and Medon and Thersilochus,
 217  Asteropaeus and Deisenor and Hippothous,
 218  Phorcys and Chromius and Ennomus the bird-reader;
 219  urging these on, he spoke winged words:
 220  "Hear me, you numberless tribes of allies who dwell around us:[14]
 221  it was not for a crowd's sake — not because I lacked numbers —
 222  that I gathered you here, each man from his own city,
 223  but so that you would guard, with a will, the Trojans' wives
 224  and little children from the war-loving Achaeans.
 225  With that in mind I wear down my own people
 226  for gifts and provisions, to feed the courage in each of you.
 227  So now — let every man turn straight ahead, and die
 228  or live: that is the trysting-talk of war.
 229  And whoever hauls Patroclus, dead though he is,
 230  in among the horse-taming Trojans, and Ajax yields to him —
 231  half the spoils I will share out to that man, and half I keep
 232  myself; and his glory shall be as great as mine."
 233  So he spoke; and they marched straight down on the Danaans, heavy,
 234  spears held high; and their hearts had great hope
 235  of hauling the body from under Ajax, Telamon's son —
 236  fools: over that body he was to rob many of life.[15]
 237  And then Ajax spoke to Menelaus, good at the war-cry:
 238  "Old friend — Zeus-nurtured Menelaus — I no longer count
 239  on the two of us, even ourselves, coming home from this war.
 240  It is not so much for the corpse of Patroclus I fear —
 241  he will glut the dogs and birds of Troy soon enough —
 242  as for my own head, that something may happen to it,
 243  and yours: for Hector, that cloud of war, has covered
 244  everything, and for us sheer destruction shows clear.
 245  Come — call the best of the Danaans, if any may hear."
 246  So he spoke, and Menelaus, good at the war-cry, did not disobey,
 247  and cried a piercing cry, to carry to the Danaans:
 248  "Friends — leaders and lords of the Argives —
 249  you who beside the two Atreus' sons, Agamemnon and Menelaus,
 250  drink at the public cost, and each give orders
 251  to your people — from Zeus come honor and glory.
 252  It is hard for me to pick out each one
 253  of the leaders — so great is the strife of war ablaze —
 254  so let each man come uncalled, and feel the outrage in his heart
 255  that Patroclus should be sport for the dogs of Troy."
 256  So he spoke; and the swift Ajax, Oïleus' son, heard him sharp,
 257  and came first to meet him, running through the combat,
 258  and after him Idomeneus, and Idomeneus's aide
 259  Meriones, the equal of Enyalius, slayer of men.
 260  But of the rest — who could speak their names out of his own mind,
 261  all who came up behind and woke the Achaean battle?[15]
 262  And the Trojans struck first, massed; Hector led them.
 263  As when at the mouth of a sky-fed river
 264  the great swell bellows against the current, and around it
 265  the jutting shores boom, as the salt sea belches beyond them:
 266  with such a roar came the Trojans on. But the Achaeans
 267  stood fast around Menoetius's son, one heart in all of them,
 268  fenced with their bronze-faced shields. And over their bright
 269  helmets the son of Cronos poured thick mist,[16]
 270  since never in the time before had he hated Menoetius's son,
 271  while he was alive and the aide of Aeacus' grandson;
 272  and he loathed that he become the prey of the dogs
 273  of Troy — and so he roused his companions to defend him.
 274  And at first the Trojans pushed back the bright-eyed Achaeans:
 275  they left the body and shrank away, and the high-hearted Trojans
 276  took not one of them with their spears, for all their striving,
 277  but set to hauling the corpse. Yet only a little while
 278  were the Achaeans to hold off from it: they were rallied fast
 279  by Ajax, who in build, and in what he wrought,
 280  surpassed all the Danaans after the blameless son of Peleus.
 281  He swept straight through the front-fighters, like a wild boar
 282  in his strength, that up in the mountains scatters hounds
 283  and strong young men with ease, wheeling through the glens:
 284  so the son of illustrious Telamon, shining Ajax,
 285  swept in and easily scattered the Trojan battle-lines
 286  that had taken their stand over Patroclus, minded above all
 287  to haul him to their own city and win the glory.
 288  Now Hippothous, the shining son of Pelasgian Lethus,[17]
 289  was dragging him by the foot through the strong encounter,
 290  a shield-strap bound around the ankle-tendons,
 291  doing a favor to Hector and the Trojans. But swiftly
 292  the evil came on him that no one could hold off, wish it as they might:
 293  the son of Telamon, springing at him through the crowd,
 294  struck close-in, through the helmet's bronze cheek-pieces;
 295  and the horsehair-crested helmet split around the spear-point,
 296  struck by the great spear and the heavy hand,
 297  and the brain came running out of the wound along the socket,
 298  bloody; and there and then his strength was unstrung, and from his hands
 299  he let the foot of great-hearted Patroclus drop
 300  to lie on the ground; and he fell face-down on the corpse beside it,
 301  far from deep-soiled Larisa; nor did he repay
 302  his dear parents the price of his rearing: his life was short,
 303  broken under the spear of great-hearted Ajax.
 304  And Hector in turn cast at Ajax with his shining spear;
 305  but he saw it coming and dodged the bronze spear —
 306  barely; and it struck Schedius, great-hearted Iphitus's son,
 307  far the best of the Phocians, whose home was in famed
 308  Panopeus, where he was king over many men:
 309  it struck him under the middle of the collarbone, and through it
 310  the bronze point drove, and came out at the base of the shoulder.
 311  He fell with a thud, and his armor clattered upon him.
 312  And Ajax struck war-minded Phorcys, Phaenops's son,
 313  full in the belly as he bestrode Hippothous,
 314  and burst the plate of his corslet, and the bronze
 315  spilled his bowels through; and falling in the dust he clutched the earth in his palm.
 316  And the front-fighters gave ground, and glorious Hector;
 317  and the Argives shouted loud, and dragged away the corpses,
 318  Phorcys and Hippothous, and loosed the armor from their shoulders.
 319  And then once more the Trojans, before the Achaeans dear to Ares,
 320  would have climbed into Ilion, broken by their own weakness,
 321  and the Argives would have won glory even beyond the portion of Zeus[18]
 322  by their own power and strength — had not Apollo himself
 323  stirred up Aeneas, in the likeness of the herald
 324  Periphas, Epytus's son, who at his old father's side
 325  had grown grey in his heraldry, his heart a well of kindly counsel;
 326  in his likeness Apollo, son of Zeus, spoke to him:
 327  "Aeneas — how could you and yours hold steep Ilion
 328  even against a god? — as I have seen other men do,
 329  trusting their power and strength, their manhood
 330  and their own numbers, though their people were too few.
 331  But Zeus wills the victory to us, far rather
 332  than to the Danaans — and you flinch unspeakably, and will not fight."
 333  So he spoke; and Aeneas knew Apollo who strikes from afar,
 334  looking him in the face, and called aloud to Hector:
 335  "Hector — and you other leaders of Trojans and allies —
 336  this now is disgrace: to climb back into Ilion
 337  before the Achaeans dear to Ares, broken by our own weakness.
 338  No — for a god has just now stood beside me and said
 339  Zeus, highest deviser of battle, is still our helper.
 340  So let us go straight for the Danaans, and not let them
 341  bring dead Patroclus to their ships at their ease."
 342  So he spoke, and sprang far out beyond the front-fighters, and stood;
 343  and they wheeled about, and stood to face the Achaeans.
 344  Then Aeneas wounded Leiocritus with his spear,
 345  the son of Arisbas, brave companion of Lycomedes;
 346  and as he fell, Lycomedes dear to Ares pitied him,
 347  and came and stood very close, and cast with his shining spear,
 348  and struck Apisaon, Hippasus's son, shepherd of the people,
 349  in the liver below the midriff, and unstrung his knees on the instant —
 350  the man who had come out of deep-soiled Paeonia,
 351  and was their best in battle after Asteropaeus.
 352  And as he fell, warlike Asteropaeus pitied him,
 353  and he too drove forward, willing to fight the Danaans;
 354  but there was no way any longer: fenced with shields on every side
 355  they stood around Patroclus, spears held out before them.
 356  For Ajax ranged among them all with strict command:[19]
 357  no man to give back a step from the body,
 358  no man to fight out in front of the other Achaeans,
 359  but to stand close over him, and fight hand to hand.
 360  So gigantic Ajax commanded; and the earth grew soaked
 361  with purple blood, and they fell in heaps together,
 362  dead men of the Trojans and their overmastering allies
 363  and Danaans too — for not without blood did these defend —
 364  but far fewer of them perished: they remembered always
 365  to ward sheer ruin from one another in the crowd.
 366  So they fought on, like fire; nor would you have said[20]
 367  the sun was safe in the sky, or the moon:
 368  for they were held in mist, all the best men in the battle
 369  who stood around the dead son of Menoetius.
 370  But the other Trojans and strong-greaved Achaeans
 371  fought at their ease under clear air: the piercing light
 372  of the sun lay spread, and no cloud showed over all
 373  the earth or the mountains; and they fought in pauses,
 374  keeping well apart, dodging each other's
 375  missiles of groaning. But those in the middle suffered
 376  in the mist and the war, worn down by the pitiless bronze —
 377  all who were the best. And two men had not yet heard —
 378  two famous men, Thrasymedes and Antilochus —
 379  that blameless Patroclus was dead: they thought him
 380  living still, fighting Trojans in the front of the crowd;
 381  and watching against the death and rout of their companions
 382  they fought apart, since Nestor had so ordered them,
 383  when he drove them to the war from the black ships.
 384  But for the others, all day long the great struggle of grinding
 385  strife stood taut: with the weariness and the sweat of it,
 386  without respite, the knees and shins, the feet beneath,
 387  the hands and eyes of every man were spattered
 388  in the fight around the brave aide of the swift-footed son of Aeacus.
 389  As when a man gives his people the hide of a great bull[21]
 390  to stretch, soaked through with fat:
 391  they take it and stand in a circle and stretch it
 392  outward; and quickly the moisture goes, and the fat sinks in
 393  with so many pulling, and the whole hide is stretched taut through:
 394  so this way and that, in a little space, both sides hauled
 395  the corpse; and their hearts had great hope —
 396  the Trojans, of dragging him to Ilion; the Achaeans,
 397  to the hollow ships. And around him the brawl rose
 398  savage: not Ares driver of armies, not Athena
 399  could have looked at it and scorned it, however great their anger:
 400  such was the evil toil of men and horses Zeus
 401  stretched taut over Patroclus on that day.
 402  And brilliant Achilles knew nothing at all, yet, of Patroclus dead:[22]
 403  for the battle was fought far from the swift ships,
 404  under the wall of the Trojans. So never in his heart
 405  did he think him dead — but living, pressed hard on the gates,
 406  and coming home again; since he never thought at all
 407  that Patroclus would sack the city without him — nor with him:
 408  for often he had learned as much from his mother, hearing her apart,
 409  when she brought him word of the purpose of great Zeus.
 410  But now his mother told him nothing of the evil that had happened —
 411  so great — that his far-dearest companion had perished.
 412  And they around the corpse, holding their sharpened spears,
 413  pressed close without respite, and killed each other;
 414  and thus would one of the bronze-shirted Achaeans say:
 415  "Friends — no glory for us in going back[23]
 416  to the hollow ships. Sooner let the black earth gape
 417  for all of us here. That would be better by far, at once,
 418  than to leave this man to the horse-taming Trojans
 419  to haul to their own city, and win the glory."
 420  And thus in turn would one of the great-hearted Trojans say:
 421  "Friends — even if it is fate that we be broken beside this man,
 422  all of us alike, let no one yet give way from the war."
 423  So a man would speak, and stir the fury of each.
 424  So they fought on; and the iron din went up
 425  through the barren air to the brazen sky.[24]
 426  But the horses of Aeacus' grandson, apart from the battle,[25]
 427  were weeping, ever since they first had learned their charioteer
 428  was fallen in the dust at the hands of man-slaughtering Hector.
 429  Automedon, Diores' stout son, tried them —
 430  lashing them again and again with the quick whip's stroke,
 431  again and again with honeyed words, and again with curses:
 432  but the pair would not go back to the ships and the broad Hellespont,
 433  nor into the battle after the Achaeans.
 434  As a grave-stone stands unmoving, set on the barrow
 435  of a dead man or a woman,
 436  so they stood, unstirring, with the beautiful chariot,
 437  drooping their heads to the earth; and hot tears
 438  ran from their eyelids to the ground as they mourned
 439  in longing for their charioteer; and their rich manes were fouled,
 440  streaming down from the yoke-pad on both sides of the yoke.
 441  And seeing the two of them mourning, the son of Cronos pitied them,
 442  and he shook his head, and spoke to his own spirit:
 443  "Ah, poor pair — why did we give you to king Peleus,[26]
 444  a mortal — you who are ageless and deathless?
 445  Was it so that you too might have sorrows among unhappy men?
 446  For there is nothing anywhere more pitiful than man,
 447  of all things that breathe and creep upon the earth.
 448  But not on you, at least, and your intricate chariot
 449  shall Hector, Priam's son, ride. I will not allow it.
 450  Is it not enough that he has the armor, and vaunts in it — just so?
 451  Into your knees and into your hearts I will throw fury,
 452  so you may carry Automedon safe out of the war
 453  to the hollow ships. For I will still hand the enemy glory,
 454  to kill, until they reach the well-benched ships,
 455  and the sun goes down and the sacred darkness comes on."
 456  So speaking, he breathed brave fury into the horses;
 457  and the two of them, shaking the dust from their manes to the ground,
 458  swiftly carried the quick chariot among Trojans and Achaeans.
 459  And behind them Automedon fought, grieving for his companion,[27]
 460  swooping with the horses like a vulture on geese:
 461  easily he would run back out of the Trojans' clamor,
 462  and easily charge in again, pressing through the thick crowd.
 463  But he took no men when he swept in pursuit:
 464  it was not possible, alone in the sacred chariot,
 465  to go at them with the spear and hold the swift horses.
 466  At last a companion saw him with his own eyes —
 467  Alcimedon, the son of Laerces, Haemon's son;
 468  he stood behind the chariot and spoke to Automedon:
 469  "Automedon — which of the gods put this profitless plan
 470  in your chest, and took your good wits from you?
 471  — fighting the Trojans alone, in the front of the crowd,
 472  while your companion lies killed, and Hector himself
 473  glories with the armor of Aeacus' grandson on his own shoulders."
 474  And Automedon, Diores' son, answered him:
 475  "Alcimedon — what other Achaean is your equal
 476  at holding the fury of immortal horses in hand —
 477  except Patroclus, the equal of the gods in counsel,
 478  while he lived? Now death and fate have overtaken him.
 479  So: you take over the whip and the shining reins,
 480  and I will step down from the horses, and fight."
 481  So he spoke; and Alcimedon, springing onto the rescue-chariot,
 482  quickly caught up the whip and reins in his hands,
 483  and Automedon leapt down. And glorious Hector saw it,
 484  and at once he spoke to Aeneas, who was near:
 485  "Aeneas — counselor of the bronze-shirted Trojans —
 486  I see the two horses of the swift-footed son of Aeacus
 487  come out into the battle with feeble charioteers.
 488  I could hope to take them, if you in your heart
 489  are willing; since if the two of us charged, they would not
 490  dare stand against us face to face, and fight the war-god."
 491  So he spoke, and the brave son of Anchises did not disobey.
 492  The two went straight on, their shoulders wrapped in oxhides
 493  dry and stiff, with heavy bronze hammered over them;
 494  and with them went Chromius, and godlike Aretus,
 495  the two together; and their hearts had great hope
 496  of killing the men, and driving off the strong-necked horses —
 497  fools: not without blood were they to come away
 498  again from Automedon. He, with a prayer to Zeus the father,
 499  was filled with courage and strength in his darkened heart;
 500  and at once he spoke to Alcimedon, his trusted companion:
 501  "Alcimedon — hold the horses not far off from me,
 502  but breathing right on my back. For I do not think
 503  Hector, Priam's son, will be checked in his fury
 504  before he has mounted behind the fair-maned horses of Achilles —
 505  with the two of us killed — and routed the ranks of the Argive
 506  men; or else been taken himself among the first.
 507  So speaking he called the two Ajaxes and Menelaus:
 508  "You two Ajaxes, leaders of the Argives, and Menelaus —
 509  leave the corpse to the best men you have, to stand
 510  and bestride it and hold off the enemy ranks,
 511  and from the two of us — living men — beat off the pitiless day.
 512  For here they bear down hard through the war of tears,
 513  Hector and Aeneas, the best men of the Trojans.
 514  But these things lie on the knees of the gods:
 515  I too will let fly — and all the rest will be Zeus's care."
 516  He spoke, and balanced his long-shadowed spear and hurled it,
 517  and struck the shield of Aretus, balanced every way;
 518  and the shield did not hold the spear: the bronze drove clean through,
 519  and through the war-belt it tore into the pit of his belly.
 520  As when a strong-grown man with a sharp axe,[27]
 521  striking behind the horns of an ox of the field,
 522  shears the tendon clean through, and the ox springs forward and drops:
 523  so Aretus sprang forward and fell on his back; and the spear,
 524  quivering razor-sharp in his bowels, unstrung his limbs.
 525  And Hector cast at Automedon with his shining spear;
 526  but he saw it coming and dodged the bronze spear,
 527  ducking forward; and behind him the long spear
 528  stuck fast in the ground, and the butt-end stood quivering;
 529  and there at last massive Ares stilled its fury.
 530  And now they would have gone at each other with swords, close in,
 531  had not the two Ajaxes parted them for all their heat,
 532  coming through the crowd at their companion's call.
 533  In dread of them the others drew back again —
 534  Hector and Aeneas and godlike Chromius —
 535  and left Aretus lying there, torn through the heart.
 536  And Automedon, the equal of swift Ares,
 537  stripped the armor, and spoke his triumph over him:
 538  "Now I have eased my heart a little of its grief
 539  for Menoetius's dead son — though the man I killed was worse."
 540  So speaking, he took up the bloody spoils and set them
 541  in the chariot, and mounted, feet and hands
 542  all bloody, like a lion that has fed on a bull.
 543  And back over Patroclus the strong encounter was stretched again,
 544  grinding, of many tears; and Athena woke the struggle,
 545  come down from heaven: for wide-seeing Zeus had sent her
 546  to drive on the Danaans — for now his mind was turned.
 547  As Zeus stretches a shimmering rainbow out of heaven[28]
 548  for mortals to see, a portent either of war
 549  or of a chill storm — the kind that stops men's work
 550  on the face of the earth, and troubles the flocks:
 551  so she, wrapping herself in a shimmering cloud,
 552  sank into the Achaean host, and woke each man.
 553  First she spoke to Atreus' son, urging him on —
 554  strong Menelaus, for he was nearest her —
 555  likened to Phoenix in body and tireless voice:
 556  "Yours will be the hanging head, Menelaus, yours the disgrace,
 557  if under the wall of the Trojans the running dogs
 558  drag the trusted companion of illustrious Achilles.
 559  Hold on with all your strength, and drive on the whole army."
 560  Then answered her Menelaus, good at the war-cry:
 561  "Phoenix — old father, elder born long ago — if only Athena
 562  would give me strength, and hold off the rush of missiles!
 563  Then I would be willing indeed to stand and defend
 564  Patroclus: for his death has touched me to the heart.
 565  But Hector has the terrible fury of fire, and will not stop
 566  cutting men down with the bronze; for Zeus hands him the glory."
 567  So he spoke; and the goddess grey-eyed Athena was glad
 568  that he had prayed to her first of all the gods.
 569  She put strength into his shoulders and his knees,
 570  and set in his chest the boldness of the fly,[29]
 571  which — beaten off again and again from a man's skin —
 572  persists in biting; and human blood is sweet to it:
 573  with boldness like that she filled his darkened heart,
 574  and he strode over Patroclus and cast with his shining spear.
 575  Now there was among the Trojans one Podes, Eëtion's son,[30]
 576  a rich man and a brave one; and Hector honored him
 577  above the people, for he was his dear companion at the feast.
 578  Him tawny-haired Menelaus struck on the war-belt
 579  as he sprang away in flight, and drove the bronze clean through:
 580  he fell with a thud; and Atreus' son Menelaus
 581  dragged the corpse away from the Trojans into the band of his companions.
 582  But Apollo came and stood by Hector, and stirred him,
 583  in the likeness of Phaenops, Asius's son, who of all
 584  his guest-friends was dearest to him, with his house at Abydos;
 585  in his likeness Apollo the worker-from-afar spoke to him:
 586  "Hector — what other Achaean will fear you now?
 587  Look how you flinched from Menelaus, who before this
 588  was a soft spearman — and now he is gone, alone, lifting
 589  the corpse away from under the Trojans: he has killed your trusted
 590  companion, brave among the front-fighters — Podes, Eëtion's son."
 591  So he spoke; and the black cloud of grief wrapped Hector round,
 592  and he came through the front-fighters helmed in blazing bronze.
 593  And then the son of Cronos took up the tasseled aegis,[31]
 594  glittering, and hid Ida under clouds,
 595  and lightened and thundered hugely, and shook it,
 596  and gave victory to the Trojans, and routed the Achaeans.
 597  First to lead the rout was Peneleos the Boeotian:[32]
 598  turned forever toward the enemy, he was hit in the shoulder,
 599  a grazing hit on the crest of it; but the spear-point of Polydamas
 600  scored clean to the bone — for he had come close when he threw.
 601  Then Hector wounded Leïtus, close in, on the wrist —
 602  the son of great-hearted Alectryon — and stopped his fighting;
 603  he shrank back, glancing round, for his heart no longer hoped
 604  to hold a spear steady in hand and fight the Trojans.
 605  And as Hector chased Leïtus, Idomeneus
 606  struck him on the corslet, on the chest beside the nipple;
 607  but the long spear snapped at the socket, and the Trojans
 608  shouted. And Hector cast at Idomeneus, Deucalion's son,
 609  standing in his chariot; and missed him by a little,
 610  but struck the aide and charioteer of Meriones,
 611  Coeranus, who had followed him from well-founded Lyctus.
 612  (Idomeneus had come on foot at first, leaving the double-ended
 613  ships, and would have handed the Trojans a great triumph
 614  had not Coeranus driven up fast with the swift horses:
 615  to him he came as light, and beat off the pitiless day —
 616  and lost his own life under man-slaughtering Hector.)
 617  Him Hector struck below the jaw, under the ear, and the spear-butt
 618  dashed his teeth out at the roots, and cut the tongue through the middle:
 619  he crashed from the chariot, and poured the reins to the ground.
 620  And Meriones stooped and gathered them up off the plain
 621  in his own hands, and called to Idomeneus:
 622  "Lay on the whip now, till you come to the swift ships!
 623  You see it yourself: the power is no longer with the Achaeans."
 624  So he spoke; and Idomeneus whipped the fair-maned horses
 625  back to the hollow ships; for fear had fallen on his heart.
 626  Nor did great-hearted Ajax fail to see it, and Menelaus —
 627  that Zeus was giving the Trojans the tide-turning victory;
 628  and great Telamonian Ajax began to speak among them:
 629  "Shame! By now even a man who was a great fool[33]
 630  could see that father Zeus himself is helping the Trojans.
 631  Every missile of theirs strikes home, whoever lets fly,
 632  coward or brave man: Zeus guides them all alike;
 633  and ours drop to the ground, every one, spent for nothing.
 634  Come then — let us ourselves think out the best plan,
 635  both how to haul the corpse away, and how we ourselves
 636  may come home to be a joy to our dear companions,
 637  who look this way, grieving, and say that no longer
 638  can the fury of man-slaughtering Hector and his invincible
 639  hands be held — that he will fall on the black ships.
 640  And would there were some companion to carry word quickly
 641  to Peleus' son; for I do not think he has even heard
 642  the wretched news — that his dear companion has perished.
 643  But nowhere can I see such a man among the Achaeans:
 644  they are held in the mist, themselves and their horses alike.
 645  Father Zeus — deliver the sons of the Achaeans out of the mist;
 646  make clear sky; grant our eyes to see;
 647  and in the light — kill us, since that is now your pleasure."
 648  So he spoke; and the father pitied him as he wept,[34]
 649  and at once he scattered the mist and thrust the fog away,
 650  and the sun blazed out, and the whole battle came clear.
 651  And then Ajax spoke to Menelaus, good at the war-cry:
 652  "Look about you now, Zeus-nurtured Menelaus — see if you can
 653  sight Antilochus alive, great-hearted Nestor's son,
 654  and send him running to war-minded Achilles
 655  with word that his far-dearest companion has perished."
 656  So he spoke, and Menelaus, good at the war-cry, did not disobey,
 657  and set out — like a lion from a farmstead[35]
 658  that has grown weary at last of provoking the dogs and the men,
 659  who will not let him take the fat of the herd,
 660  watching all night long; and he, craving the meat,
 661  charges in but gets nowhere, for javelins thick and fast
 662  fly out to meet him from daring hands,
 663  and burning brands besides, and he flinches at those for all his fury,
 664  and at dawn he goes off apart, sullen at heart:
 665  so from Patroclus went Menelaus, good at the war-cry,
 666  much against his will; for he feared the Achaeans
 667  in the grim rout would leave him, prey for the enemy;
 668  and long he charged Meriones and the two Ajaxes:
 669  "You two Ajaxes, leaders of the Argives, and Meriones —
 670  now let each man remember the gentleness
 671  of poor Patroclus: to every one of us he knew how
 672  to be kind, while he lived. Now death and fate have overtaken him."
 673  So speaking, tawny-haired Menelaus went off,
 674  glancing on every side, like an eagle — the bird
 675  men say has the sharpest sight of all things under heaven:
 676  high as he rides, the swift-footed hare does not escape him
 677  crouched under a leafy bush, but down upon it
 678  he swoops, and seizes it fast, and takes its life:
 679  so then, Zeus-nurtured Menelaus, your shining eyes
 680  went ranging everywhere across the crowd of your companions,
 681  hunting Nestor's son, if you might see him still alive.
 682  And he saw him quickly, on the far left of the whole battle,
 683  heartening his companions and driving them to fight;
 684  and standing close, tawny-haired Menelaus spoke:
 685  "Antilochus — come here, Zeus-nurtured; hear
 686  the wretched news — the thing that should never have happened.
 687  Already, I think, you see it with your own eyes
 688  and know it: a god is rolling calamity on the Danaans,
 689  and victory is the Trojans'. And killed is the best of the Achaeans —
 690  Patroclus; and a great longing is built for the Danaans.
 691  Run to the ships of the Achaeans, quick, and tell
 692  Achilles — he may yet bring the body safe to his ship,
 693  naked: for Hector of the flashing helmet has the armor."
 694  So he spoke; and Antilochus heard the word with horror.[35]
 695  For a long time speechlessness held him: his eyes
 696  filled with tears, and his strong voice was checked.
 697  Yet not even so did he neglect Menelaus's charge:
 698  he set off at a run, and gave his armor to his blameless companion
 699  Laodocus, who wheeled the whole-hoofed horses close beside him.
 700  And his feet carried him weeping out of the battle,
 701  bearing the evil word to Achilles, Peleus' son.
 702  And your heart, Zeus-nurtured Menelaus, had no will[36]
 703  to defend the worn companions there where Antilochus
 704  had left them, and great longing was built for the Pylians;
 705  but he sent them brilliant Thrasymedes,
 706  and himself went back again to bestride the hero Patroclus;
 707  and running he stood beside the two Ajaxes, and spoke at once:
 708  "That man I have sent off to the swift ships,
 709  to go to Achilles of the swift feet. But I do not think
 710  Achilles will come now, however great his anger at brilliant Hector:
 711  there is no way he could fight the Trojans naked.
 712  So let us ourselves think out the best plan,
 713  both how to haul the corpse away, and how we ourselves
 714  may escape death and doom out of the Trojans' clamor."
 715  Then great Telamonian Ajax answered him:
 716  "All you have said is right and true, far-famed Menelaus.
 717  You and Meriones — stoop down, quickly, get under him,
 718  lift the corpse and carry him out of the toil. And behind you
 719  we two will fight the Trojans and brilliant Hector,
 720  one heart in us as we share one name, we two who before now
 721  have stood the sharp war-god side by side, together."
 722  So he spoke; and they took the corpse up off the ground in their arms,
 723  high and mightily; and behind them the Trojan army
 724  screamed, when they saw the Achaeans lifting the body,
 725  and charged like hounds that spring on a wounded boar[37]
 726  ahead of the young hunters:
 727  for a while they race in, furious to tear him —
 728  but when he wheels among them, sure of his strength,
 729  back they shrink, and scatter in terror this way and that:
 730  so the Trojans for a while kept up the chase in a mass,
 731  stabbing with swords and double-curved spears;
 732  but whenever the two Ajaxes turned about and stood
 733  against them, their color changed, and not one dared
 734  dart forward and fight for the corpse.
 735  So the two of them, straining, carried the body out of the battle
 736  toward the hollow ships; and the war stretched taut behind them,
 737  savage as fire that leaps on a city of men,
 738  rising suddenly, and blazes, and the houses crumble
 739  in the great glare, and the wind's strength roars across it:
 740  so the relentless din of horses and of spearmen
 741  came on behind them as they went.
 742  And they — like mules who have thrown their great strength into the work,
 743  dragging down from the mountain along a rugged track
 744  a roof-beam or a huge ship-timber, and inside them the heart
 745  is worn with weariness and sweat together as they strain:
 746  so the two of them, straining, carried the body. And behind them
 747  the two Ajaxes held the enemy — as a wooded ridge
 748  holds back water, a ridge lying clean across a plain,
 749  which checks even the punishing currents of powerful rivers,
 750  and at once turns the flood of them all to wander
 751  loose across the lowland; and all their strength cannot break it:
 752  so the two Ajaxes, behind, kept fending off the battle
 753  of the Trojans. But they followed hard, and two above all,
 754  Aeneas, Anchises' son, and glorious Hector.
 755  And as a cloud of starlings goes, or of jackdaws,[38]
 756  shrieking ruin, when they see coming at them
 757  a falcon, that carries death to small birds:
 758  so before Aeneas and Hector the young Achaean men
 759  went shrieking ruin, and forgot their war-craft.
 760  And much fine armor fell around and about the ditch
 761  as the Danaans fled; and there was no pause in the war.

NOTES

[1] (17.1-6) Menelaus over the body "as a mother-cow, lowing,
    stands over her first calf, who never knew birth before"
    (πρωτοτόκος κινυρή — the lowing of a first-time mother). The
    poem opens its longest battle with its most maternal image:
    the corpse-defense that will consume the book is presented
    first not as war but as instinct — and given to Menelaus, the
    poem's specialist in loyal, unglamorous care.

[2] (17.9-32) Euphorbus claims the spoils by right of first blood
    (his dart at 16.806-815); Menelaus's answer settles the
    Hyperenor account opened at 14.516 — with details the earlier
    narrative never gave (the mockery, "sorriest fighter of the
    Danaans"): the poem's ledgers, as at 17.24-28, remember more
    than they recorded. His closing proverb — "When the thing is
    done, even the fool learns" (ῥεχθὲν δέ τε νήπιος ἔγνω) —
    became Greek proverbial stock permanently.

[3] (17.33-42) Euphorbus's revenge-speech is a family portrait:
    the brother's widow "in the depth of her new bridal chamber,"
    the parents' "cursed mourning," and the imagined homecoming —
    your head and armor "in the hands of Panthous and brilliant
    Phrontis." He is the third Panthoid the war has engaged
    (Polydamas, Hyperenor); by naming his own parents he writes
    the address on his own death-notice.

[4] (17.43-60) The kill and the double image. First the hair:
    "soaked with blood — hair like the Graces', and the braids
    pinched tight in gold and silver" — the poem's dandy-warrior,
    beautiful even in the detail of his hair-clasps. Then the
    olive sapling: reared by hand in a lonely place by welling
    water, rocked by every breathing of wind, white with blossom
    — torn out by one squall and laid flat. The most tender
    death-simile in the Iliad, spent on a minor Trojan — and made
    immortal twice over: Porphyry's Life of Pythagoras quotes the
    passage because Pythagoras claimed to have been Euphorbus
    (16.[43]); the sage's remembered death arrived pre-fitted
    with the loveliest lines in Homer.

[5] (17.61-69) The lion takes the finest heifer: lines 63-64
    repeat 11.175-176 verbatim (our English kept) — the neck
    broken first, the blood and inward parts gulped down — while
    dogs and herdsmen clamor "from a distance," held by green
    fear. Menelaus, so often the poem's second-rank fighter, is
    for once the beast nobody dares approach.

[6] (17.70-105) Apollo-as-Mentes turns Hector back with the
    horses-taunt that reuses Dolon's dream (10.402-404, our
    English kept): the unattainable team as the measure of Trojan
    overreach, third time offered, never possessed. Menelaus's
    soliloquy that follows is the poem's clearest statement of
    the shame-calculus (fixed frame lines kept: "Troubled, he
    spoke..."; "But why does my own heart argue..."): abandoning
    the body is disgrace, fighting a god-backed man is doom —
    "When a man wills, against the god's face, to fight a man
    whom a god honors, a great woe rolls down on him quickly" —
    and the resolution is neither: withdraw, find Ajax, return.
    Prudence with a plan is not cowardice; the poem lets the
    calculation stand unmocked.

[7] (17.109-113) The reluctant lion — driven from the fold, "the
    brave heart in his chest goes stiff with the cold" — the
    first of the book's two borrowings from Ajax's great retreat-
    simile family of Book 11 (see note [35] for the second): the
    poem now clothes Menelaus in the images it minted for its
    strongest defender.

[8] (17.125-127) Hector's intention, stated flat: to shear the
    head from the shoulders and give the trunk "to the dogs of
    Troy." The corpse-outrage theme crosses a line no one has yet
    crossed in act — and it is Hector, the poem's most humane
    warrior, who first conceives it. Books 22-24 will weigh
    everything that follows against these three lines.

[9] (17.132-139) Ajax over the corpse as a lion over his cubs,
    the whole scowl of his brows drawn down over his eyes —
    guardianship again (note [1]), now paternal; and Menelaus
    beside him "nursing great grief." The two images of the
    book's opening — mother-cow and lion-father — bracket the
    dead man like parents.

[10] (17.140-168) Glaucus's rebuke — the harshest any ally gives
    Hector — climaxes in the book's most startling proposal: haul
    Patroclus into Ilion and trade him — "the Argives would
    quickly ransom back the fine armor of Sarpedon, and we would
    bring the man himself into Ilion." Corpse for corpse, gear
    for gear: the dead as exchangeable stores of value. The
    poem's whole final movement (Hector's body ransomed for
    weighed gold) is glimpsed here first as an ally's grievance;
    and the grievance itself — "there was never any thanks" — is
    the mercenary's ledger Hector must answer with his own
    accounting at 220-232.

[11] (17.169-182) Hector's answer reuses the theology of
    16.688-692 (our English kept): "always the mind of Zeus...
    is stronger: he sends even the brave man running... and then
    again himself drives a man to fight." True doctrine — and,
    as ever with Hector, deployed exactly where it excuses him.
    The challenge ("stand beside me, old friend — watch my
    work") converts the quarrel into witness.

[12] (17.183-197) The changing of the armor, staged "apart from
    the battle of many tears" like a rite: his own gear sent
    into Ilion, the immortal gear put on — and the poem recites
    its provenance as elegy: gods to Peleus; Peleus, "grown
    old," to his son; "but the son did not grow old in his
    father's armor." The line every reader remembers: the
    armor's history is a history of interrupted inheritance, and
    Hector is now inside it.

[13] (17.198-214) Zeus's head-shake over Hector is the poem's
    theology of recompense at its most precise: the armor taken
    "not by right" (οὐ κατὰ κόσμον); the grant of "great power"
    made explicitly τῶν ποινήν — as blood-price, compensation
    paid in advance for the death already decreed: "never home
    out of the battle will you come, for Andromache to take from
    you the famous armor of Peleus' son." Andromache, who begged
    him to fight from the wall (6.431), enters the sentence as
    the person who will not perform the homecoming ritual. Then
    the uncanny grace-note: the armor fits — Zeus himself
    "fitted the armor to Hector's body, and Ares entered him."
    The gift, the doom, and the ecstasy are one act.

[14] (17.215-232) Hector's speech to the allies is the epic's
    frankest look at coalition warfare: "it was not for a
    crowd's sake I gathered you... I wear down my own people for
    gifts and provisions, to feed the courage in each of you" —
    the war economy Glaucus invoked (147-148) conceded in full.
    Then the third and strangest use of the intimacy-word: die
    or live — "that is the trysting-talk of war" (πολέμου
    ὀαριστύς; 13.291, 14.216). And the bounty: half-shares with
    Hector for whoever drags Patroclus in — glory made divisible
    and priced.

[15] (17.233-261, 260-261) The advance is stamped "fools" (over
    that body Ajax "was to rob many of life"); Ajax's grim
    realism ("It is not so much for the corpse I fear... as for
    my own head"); Menelaus's call to the leaders "who drink at
    the public cost" — rank invoked through its perquisites; and
    then the narrator's own confession, athetized by Zenodotus:
    "But of the rest — who could speak their names out of his
    own mind...?" The inverted Muse-invocation: where 2.484
    called on the goddesses because memory fails, here the
    singer simply shows the failure. The ancients heard a lapse;
    it reads as the book's honesty about crowds.

[16] (17.262-273) Zeus pours mist over the body's defenders with
    the poem's most carefully argued motive: "never in the time
    before had he hated Menoetius's son, while he was alive...
    and he loathed that he become the prey of the dogs of Troy."
    The god who engineered the death (16.644-655) now spends the
    day protecting the corpse: the two attitudes are not
    reconciled — they are both true, and the poem leaves them
    side by side.

[17] (17.274-318) The first full swing of the tug: Hippothous
    drags the body by a shield-strap bound round the ankle-
    tendons — the technical, butcher's detail — and dies across
    it, "far from deep-soiled Larisa; nor did he repay his dear
    parents the price of his rearing" (θρέπτρα — child-care as a
    debt the dead leave unpaid; cf. 4.477-478). Schedius the
    Phocian falls to Hector — the death our register logged when
    his namesake died at 15.515: the poem keeps both Schedii and
    spends them in different books. The exchange ends with the
    Argives dragging away not Patroclus but the Trojan dead:
    every body on this field is now cargo.

[18] (17.319-334) The counterfactual — Troy broken "even beyond
    the portion of Zeus" (ὑπὲρ Διὸς αἶσαν) by mortal strength
    alone — is the strongest such clause in the poem: fate has
    slack in it, and only Apollo's intervention takes it up. The
    god borrows the shape of Periphas the herald, "grown grey in
    his heraldry" beside old Epytus — the poem's tenderness for
    professions again — and Aeneas knows him at once ("looking
    him in the face"): the recognition granted to the pious
    Aeneas and almost no one else.

[19] (17.356-365) Ajax's field-order is the tactical heart of
    the book: no retreat from the body, no heroics out in front,
    "stand close over him, and fight hand to hand." The result
    the poet audits precisely: the ground purple, dead on both
    sides — "but far fewer of them perished: they remembered
    always to ward sheer ruin from one another in the crowd."
    The phalanx-discipline of Books 13 and 16 has become
    doctrine, stated and costed: cohesion is casualty-arithmetic.

[20] (17.366-383) The doubled sky: over Patroclus, mist and
    night — "you would not have said the sun was safe, or the
    moon"; everywhere else "the piercing light of the sun lay
    spread, and no cloud showed over all the earth or the
    mountains," where men fight "in pauses, keeping well apart."
    A private midnight for the best, sunshine for the rest — the
    poem's most surreal battlefield image, ending on the two who
    fight in the light and do not know (Thrasymedes and
    Antilochus): light as ignorance, darkness as the place where
    the truth is.

[21] (17.384-401) The tanning simile: the great bull's hide,
    soaked in fat, stretched in a circle by many hands "until
    the moisture goes and the fat sinks in... and the whole hide
    is stretched taut through" — the day's whole war compressed
    into a work-yard: the corpse pulled "this way and that, in a
    little space," by professionals, patiently. Even Ares and
    Athena "could not have scorned" the toil — the gods invoked
    as connoisseurs of labor. Nothing in the poem is grimmer
    than the craftsmanship of this comparison.

[22] (17.401-411) And Achilles does not know. The passage
    Zenodotus cut (404-425 in his edition): Thetis "often" told
    him the purpose of Zeus, "but now his mother told him
    nothing of the evil that had happened." Inside it, the
    strangest clause in the poem's plotting: Achilles never
    hoped Patroclus would sack the city "without him — nor with
    him": he has carried his mother's prophecies far enough to
    know Troy is not theirs to take together. The ignorance is
    thus doubly cruel: he knows the large doom and not the small
    one that is running toward him on Antilochus's feet.

[23] (17.412-425) The paired anonymous vows (τις-speeches): the
    Achaean — "Sooner let the black earth gape for all of us
    here"; the Trojan — "even if it is fate that we be broken
    beside this man, all of us alike, let no one yet give way."
    The two armies, nameless, say the same sentence from
    opposite sides.

[24] (17.424-425) "the iron din went up through the barren air
    to the brazen sky" — iron, bronze, and the barren middle
    air (ἀτρύγετος, the sea's own epithet lent to the ether):
    the war as metallurgy filling the whole space between earth
    and heaven.

[25] (17.426-440) The weeping horses. Immortal Xanthus and
    Balius stand apart from the battle "as a grave-stone stands
    unmoving, set on the barrow of a dead man or a woman" —
    creatures of wind become their charioteer's own monument —
    heads to the earth, "hot tears" running, "their rich manes
    fouled, streaming down from the yoke-pad." Automedon tries
    whip, honey, and curses; grief does not answer to any of the
    three. It is the poem's only animal mourning, and among its
    greatest inventions: sorrow without speech, loyalty without
    hope, in bodies that cannot die.

[26] (17.441-455) Zeus's pity — the sentence at the poem's
    center of gravity: "why did we give you to king Peleus, a
    mortal — you who are ageless and deathless? Was it so that
    you too might have sorrows among unhappy men? For there is
    nothing anywhere more pitiful than man, of all things that
    breathe and creep upon the earth." The god who runs the war
    states its final metaphysics: mortality itself, not any
    battle, is the pity of it; the immortal horses' one flaw is
    contact with us. Then the practical rider: Hector shall not
    have them ("Is it not enough that he has the armor...?"),
    and the sunset clause of the day repeated word for word from
    11.193-194 — the doom still running on schedule beneath the
    compassion.

[27] (17.456-542) The Automedon sequence: the charioteer fights
    "like a vulture among geese," is talked into sense by
    Alcimedon (the Myrmidon fifth-captain of 16.197), and the
    two-man crew re-forms — driver breathing on his back —
    against Hector, Aeneas, Chromius, and Aretus. Aretus takes
    the spear "as when a strong-grown man with a sharp axe,
    striking behind the horns of an ox of the field, shears the
    tendon clean through, and the ox springs forward and drops"
    — the sacrificial slaughter-stroke on the battlefield. And
    Automedon's vaunt is the book's grief-economy in one line:
    "Now I have eased my heart a little of its grief... though
    the man I killed was worse." Vengeance as approximate
    payment; the ledger never balances, and he says so.

[28] (17.543-552) Athena comes down wrapped in "a shimmering
    cloud" like the rainbow Zeus stretches "for mortals to see,
    a portent either of war or of a chill storm that stops
    men's work on the face of the earth, and troubles the
    flocks." The rainbow as omen (11.27-28 made it armor-
    decoration) — beauty that means interruption: war for men,
    weather for the sheep, the two disasters given equal
    standing in the farmer's sky.

[29] (17.553-573) Athena-as-Phoenix, and the fly. She fills
    Menelaus "with the boldness of the fly, which — beaten off
    again and again from a man's skin — persists in biting; and
    human blood is sweet to it." The smallest courage-simile in
    epic and the most audacious: persistence stripped of all
    dignity and honored anyway — appetite as valor. Plutarch and
    Galen both quote it; no other poet would have dared attach
    it to a king at the moment a goddess strengthens him.

[30] (17.575-591) Podes, Eëtion's son — "his dear companion at
    the feast" (εἰλαπιναστής): Hector's table-intimate, rich and
    brave, killed in flight by the man Apollo then calls "before
    this a soft spearman" (μαλθακὸς αἰχμητής). The grief-cloud
    that wraps Hector is personal — the war has finally reached
    his own mess-table. The name Eëtion tolls again (Andromache's
    father, 6.396; Pedasus's city, 16.153): everything Hector
    loves seems to descend from men of that name.

[31] (17.593-596) Zeus ends the argument by hand: the tasseled
    aegis taken up, Ida hidden in cloud, lightning, thunder, the
    shake — "and gave victory to the Trojans, and routed the
    Achaeans." The instrument Apollo borrowed at 15.229 is back
    with its owner; four verbs, and the day tilts.

[32] (17.597-625) The rout ledger: Peneleos and Leïtus wounded
    (the two Boeotian catalogue-leaders of 2.494 subtracted in
    two lines); Idomeneus's spear snapping on Hector's corslet;
    and Coeranus of Lyctus, who dies taking the cast meant for
    his king — "to him he came as light, and beat off the
    pitiless day — and lost his own life": the poem's formula
    of salvation (φάος, light) priced at one charioteer.
    Meriones stooping to gather the fallen reins off the plain
    while the world collapses is the kind of detail this poet
    never omits: someone must always pick up the reins.

[33] (17.626-647) Ajax's prayer. First the observation ("even a
    man who was a great fool could see that father Zeus himself
    is helping the Trojans — every missile of theirs strikes
    home... and ours drop to the ground, every one"), then the
    double need (save the body; send word to Achilles), and
    then the request that Longinus (On the Sublime 9.10) set
    above nearly everything in literature: "Father Zeus —
    deliver the sons of the Achaeans out of the mist; make
    clear sky; grant our eyes to see; and in the light — kill
    us, since that is now your pleasure" (ἐν δὲ φάει καὶ
    ὄλεσσον). He does not pray to live; he prays to see. The
    demand is worthy of the fighter the poem has built across
    seventeen books: no complaint, no bargain — visibility, and
    then whatever Zeus likes.

[34] (17.648-650) "And the father pitied him as he wept" — the
    tears are Ajax's (the strongest man in the army weeping
    inside his prayer, a detail easy to miss and impossible to
    forget), and Zeus's answer is instant and exact: mist
    scattered, fog thrust away, "the sun blazed out, and the
    whole battle came clear." He grants the seeing. The killing
    he keeps on its schedule.

[35] (17.651-701) The message chain. Menelaus leaves the body
    "much against his will," in the reluctant-lion simile
    repeated from 11.550-555 (our English kept — the simile
    that carried Ajax's retreat now carries Menelaus's errand);
    his charge to the defenders is the dead man's true eulogy:
    "let each man remember the gentleness of poor Patroclus: to
    every one of us he knew how to be kind, while he lived."
    Then the eagle-eye search, and the delivery to Antilochus —
    who answers with the poem's most complete silence: "For a
    long time speechlessness held him: his eyes filled with
    tears, and his strong voice was checked." He hands his
    armor to Laodocus and runs weeping — the fastest of the
    young Achaeans carrying the heaviest sentence in the poem,
    unarmed, "bearing the evil word." Book 18 opens on his
    arrival.

[36] (17.702-721) Menelaus returns — the apostrophe turned on
    him twice in fifty lines (679, 702): the book's faithful
    laborer earns the second person too. Ajax's plan divides
    the last work: Menelaus and Meriones to carry, the two
    Ajaxes — "one heart in us as we share one name" — to hold
    the rear: the pun on Αἴαντε as a fighting unit, two men
    conjugated as a dual.

[37] (17.722-753) The carry: the body hoisted "high and
    mightily"; the boar wheeling on the hounds; the fire
    engulfing a city ("the houses crumble in the great glare");
    and then the pair of similes that turn rescue into labor
    and landscape — the mules dragging beam or ship-timber
    "down from the mountain along a rugged track... the heart
    worn with weariness and sweat"; the Ajaxes as "a wooded
    ridge... lying clean across a plain," damming the flood of
    powerful rivers and turning it loose over the lowland. Men
    as terrain: the defensive fighter's apotheosis is to become
    geography.

[38] (17.755-761) The end: starlings and jackdaws "shrieking
    ruin" before the falcon — the young Achaeans in flight
    before Aeneas and Hector — "and much fine armor fell around
    and about the ditch as the Danaans fled; and there was no
    pause in the war." The book that fought all day for one
    man's body closes with armor strewn like litter and the
    fighting unpaused: no cadence, no rest — because the poem's
    next sound is Antilochus's voice in Achilles' hearing, and
    nothing in the war will ever be the same after it.
