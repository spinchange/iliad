BOOK 7

The duel of Hector and Ajax, the burial truce, and the building of the
Achaean wall.

TRANSLATOR'S COMMENTARY

Book 7 is the poem's book of institutions. Its duel replays Book 3 with
the war's real fighters — and the poem says so in its own medium: the
spear-exchange at 244-254 reuses the Paris-Menelaus lines of 3.355-360
verbatim, and this translation repeats its own Book 3 English word for
word (note [16]). Around the duel stand the machinery of managed violence:
the challenge with terms for the corpse, the marked lots, the heralds
parting the fighters at dusk ("It is good to yield to night as well"),
the exchange of gifts — Hector's sword and Ajax's belt, on which later
tradition hung both their deaths (note [20]) — the burial truce, the
diplomatic offer and its refusal, and at last the wall and ditch that
will be the stage-set of Books 8-15.

The wall carries the book's strangest scene: Poseidon's complaint that
the Achaeans built it "and gave the gods no glorious hecatombs," and
Zeus's answer licensing its future erasure — sweep it into the sea, hide
the beach under sand again. All three Alexandrian critics athetized the
exchange (443-464), and it is easy to see why it unsettled them: the poem
pauses to arrange the demolition of its own scenery, as if to certify
that no trace of the fiction will contradict the visible coastline (note
[27]; the demolition is narrated at 12.13-33). Nestor's proposal that
each man's bones be carried "home to the children" (334-335) was also
athetized — the custom looks Athenian, not Homeric — and Antenor's motion
to hand Helen back completes the pattern of a book in which every
institution for ending the war is tried and fails (notes [22], [23]).

Small register notes: αἴσιμα παρειπών, the disputed narratorial phrase
from the Adrestus scene (6.62), recurs at 120-121 in a context where it
is plainly benign — evidence worth weighing back into that dispute.
Poseidon "the earth-shaker" enters the register, with "green fear," the
"bronze-greaved" hapax at 41, and the slave-trade line at 475 — the only
occurrence of ἀνδράποδα in Homer, athetized by all three critics, and
rendered "slaves" under the project's policy (note [28]).

   1  So speaking, glorious Hector swept out through the gates,
   2  and with him went Alexandros his brother; and in their hearts
   3  both were raging for war and for battle.
   4  As a god gives to longing sailors
   5  a following wind, when they have worn themselves out at the polished
   6  pine oars, driving the sea, and their limbs are unstrung with weariness:
   7  so those two appeared to the longing Trojans.
   8  Then each took his man: Paris the son of lord Areithous,
   9  Menesthius, who had his home in Arne — whom the club-man[1]

  11  and Hector hit Eioneus with his sharp spear
  12  in the neck, under the fine bronze helmet-rim, and unstrung his limbs.
  13  And Glaucus, Hippolochus' son, captain of the Lycian men,
  14  hit Iphinous, Dexias' son, with his spear in the strong encounter
  15  as he leapt up behind his fast mares,
  16  in the shoulder; he dropped from the car to the ground, and his limbs were unstrung.

  17  Now when the goddess grey-eyed Athena marked them
  18  killing Argives in the strong encounter,
  19  down from the peaks of Olympus she went darting
  20  to holy Ilion; and Apollo rose to meet her,
  21  looking out from Pergamos, wanting victory for the Trojans;
  22  and the two of them met each other beside the oak.
  23  Lord Apollo, Zeus's son, spoke to her first:
  24  "Why so eager, this time, daughter of great Zeus?
  25  Why come from Olympus? What great passion sent you?
  26  Is it to give the Danaans victory that turns the battle's tide?
  27  You have no pity, none, for the Trojans as they die.
  28  But if you would let me persuade you, it would be far better:
  29  let us stop the war and the fighting
  30  for today. Later they will fight again, until they find
  31  the appointed end of Ilion — since it is so dear to the hearts
  32  of you immortal goddesses to sack this city."
  33  Then the goddess grey-eyed Athena answered him:
  34  "So be it, worker-from-afar. With that very thought
  35  I came from Olympus among the Trojans and Achaeans.
  36  Come then — how do you mean to stop the men's fighting?"
  37  And lord Apollo, Zeus's son, answered her:
  38  "Let us rouse the strong fury of Hector, breaker of horses,
  39  to see if he will challenge some one of the Danaans
  40  to fight him man to man in dread combat,
  41  and the bronze-greaved Achaeans, stung to honor,
  42  will send out one man alone to fight brilliant Hector."
  43  So he spoke, and the goddess grey-eyed Athena did not disobey.
  44  And Helenus, Priam's dear son, put together in his heart
  45  their counsel — the plan that had pleased the plotting gods;
  46  and he came and stood by Hector and spoke his word:
  47  "Hector, son of Priam, the equal of Zeus in counsel,
  48  would you let me persuade you? I am your brother.
  49  Make the other Trojans sit down, and all the Achaeans,
  50  and yourself challenge whoever is best of the Achaeans
  51  to fight you man to man in dread combat.
  52  It is not yet your fate to die and meet your doom:
  53  so I heard it in the voice of the gods who are forever."[2]
  54  So he spoke, and Hector rejoiced greatly to hear the word,
  55  and he went into the middle and held back the Trojan lines,
  56  gripping his spear by the middle; and they all sat down,
  57  and Agamemnon seated the strong-greaved Achaeans.
  58  And Athena and Apollo of the silver bow,
  59  in the likeness of vulture birds, settled
  60  on the tall oak of father Zeus who bears the aegis,[3]
  61  delighting in the men; and the ranks of them sat close,
  62  bristling with shields and helmets and spears.
  63  As a shiver from the West Wind spreads down over the deep
  64  when the wind first rises, and the sea darkens beneath it,
  65  such were the ranks of the Achaeans and Trojans sitting
  66  in the plain. And Hector spoke between the two armies:
  67  "Hear from me, Trojans and strong-greaved Achaeans,
  68  so I may say what the heart in my chest commands me.
  69  Our sworn oaths — the high-throned son of Cronos did not seal them,
  70  but plans evil for both sides, and marks it out,
  71  until either you take fair-towered Troy
  72  or are broken yourselves beside your seagoing ships.
  73  Among you are the chief men of all the Achaeans:
  74  of these, let the man whose heart bids him fight me
  75  come out here now, before you all, to champion you against brilliant Hector.
  76  And this is my word — let Zeus be our witness to it:
  77  if that man takes my life with the long-edged bronze,
  78  let him strip my armor and carry it to the hollow ships,
  79  but give my body back to my home, so that in death
  80  the Trojans and the Trojans' wives may give me my due of fire.
  81  But if I take him, and Apollo gives me the glory,
  82  I will strip his armor and carry it to holy Ilion
  83  and hang it before the temple of Apollo the far-striker;
  84  but the corpse I will give back to the well-benched ships,
  85  so the long-haired Achaeans may give him burial
  86  and heap him a mound beside the wide Hellespont.
  87  And someday a man will say, even among men born late,
  88  sailing his many-benched ship over the wine-dark sea:
  89  'This is the mound of a man who died long ago,
  90  whom once, fighting his best, glorious Hector killed.'
  91  So someone will say; and my fame will never perish."[4]
  92  So he spoke, and they all went still in silence:
  93  ashamed to refuse, and afraid to accept.
  94  At long last Menelaus stood up and spoke among them,
  95  raking them with his scorn, groaning deep at heart:
  96  "Ah me — you threateners! Women of Achaea, men of Achaea no longer![5]
  97  Surely this disgrace will be grim beyond grimness
  98  if no Danaan now goes out to meet Hector.
  99  May every one of you turn to water and earth,
 100  sitting there where you sit, heartless, fameless.
 101  Against this man I will arm myself; the ends of victory
 102  are held above us, in the hands of the immortal gods."
 103  So speaking he began to put on his beautiful armor.
 104  And then, Menelaus, the end of your life would have shown
 105  at Hector's hands, since he was far the stronger,
 106  had not the kings of the Achaeans sprung up and caught you —
 107  and Atreus' son himself, wide-ruling Agamemnon,
 108  took him by the right hand, and spoke a word, and named him:
 109  "You are raving, Zeus-nurtured Menelaus — and this raving
 110  is not required of you. Hold back, though it stings,
 111  and do not, for pride's sake, fight a better man than you,
 112  Hector, Priam's son — a man the others shudder at.
 113  Even Achilles, in the battle where men win glory,
 114  shivered to meet this man — and he is far better than you.[6]
 115  Go now and sit among the company of your companions;
 116  the Achaeans will put up another champion against this one.
 117  Fearless though he is, and never sated with the grind of battle,
 118  I say he will be glad to bend his knee in rest, if he escapes
 119  out of the fury of war and the dread combat."
 120  So speaking the hero turned his brother's mind,
 121  for his urging was of things due; and he obeyed. Then his aides,[7]
 122  rejoicing, lifted the armor from his shoulders;
 123  and Nestor stood up among the Argives and spoke:
 124  "Shame! Great grief comes upon the Achaean land.
 125  How the old horseman Peleus would groan aloud,[8]
 126  the good counselor of the Myrmidons, their speaker —
 127  who once in his own house delighted in questioning me
 128  for the lineage and the birth of every man of the Argives.
 129  If he heard of them now, cowering, every one, before Hector,
 130  he would lift his dear hands to the immortals, over and over,
 131  praying his spirit down out of his body into Hades' house.
 132  O father Zeus, Athena, Apollo —
 133  would I were young, as when by quick-running Celadon
 134  the Pylians and the spear-mad Arcadians met and fought
 135  by the walls of Pheia, around the streams of Iardanus.
 136  Their champion stood forth: Ereuthalion, a man like a god,[9]
 137  wearing on his shoulders the armor of lord Areithous —
 138  brilliant Areithous, whom men called by his surname
 139  Club-man — the men, and the fair-belted women —
 140  because he fought with no bow, no long spear:
 141  with a club of iron he smashed the companies apart.
 142  Lycurgus killed him — by craft, not force at all —
 143  in a narrow lane, where the iron club was no use
 144  to ward destruction off: Lycurgus was in first,
 145  pinned him through the middle with his spear, and he crashed back on the earth;
 146  and he stripped the armor brazen Ares had given him.
 147  That armor he wore himself, after, in the grind of Ares;
 148  and when Lycurgus was growing old in his halls
 149  he gave it to Ereuthalion, his dear aide, to wear —
 150  and wearing it, Ereuthalion challenged all our best.
 151  They trembled, they were terrified; no man dared.
 152  But my much-enduring heart drove me on
 153  to fight him with its daring — and I was youngest-born of all —
 154  and I fought him, and Athena gave me my glory.
 155  The tallest and the strongest man I ever killed:
 156  a great sprawl of him lay there, this way and that.
 157  Would that I were young like that, my strength still steady!
 158  Hector of the flashing helmet would soon have his fight.
 159  But you — you who are the chief men of all the Achaeans —
 160  not even you are heartily eager to go and face Hector."
 161  So the old man raked them; and nine men in all stood up.[10]
 162  First by far to rise was the lord of men Agamemnon;
 163  after him rose Tydeus' son, strong Diomedes;
 164  after them the two Ajaxes, clothed in rushing courage;
 165  after them Idomeneus, and Idomeneus' comrade
 166  Meriones, the equal of Enyalius, slayer of men;
 167  after them Eurypylus, Euaemon's shining son;
 168  and up rose Thoas, Andraemon's son, and brilliant Odysseus:
 169  all these were willing to fight brilliant Hector.
 170  And among them spoke the Gerenian horseman Nestor:
 171  "Shake now the lots, all of you, for the man who draws it:
 172  he is the one who will profit the strong-greaved Achaeans,
 173  and he will profit his own heart too, if he escapes
 174  out of the fury of war and the dread combat."
 175  So he spoke, and each man marked his lot
 176  and they threw them into the helmet of Atreus' son Agamemnon;
 177  and the armies prayed, and lifted their hands to the gods,
 178  and thus would one say, looking into the wide sky:
 179  "Father Zeus, let it be Ajax, or Tydeus' son,
 180  or the king himself of Mycenae rich in gold."
 181  So they spoke; and the Gerenian horseman Nestor shook,
 182  and out of the helmet leapt the lot they had wished for —
 183  Ajax's. And a herald carried it everywhere through the crowd,
 184  showing it left to right to all the chief men of the Achaeans;
 185  and each, not knowing the mark, denied it was his.
 186  But when, carrying it everywhere through the crowd, he came
 187  to the man who had marked it and thrown it in the helmet — glorious Ajax —
 188  Ajax held out his hand, and the herald stood by him and dropped it in,
 189  and he knew his lot's mark at a glance, and rejoiced at heart.[11]
 190  He threw it to the ground beside his foot, and called out:
 191  "Friends — the lot is mine! And I am glad
 192  with my whole heart, for I think I shall beat brilliant Hector.
 193  Come then: while I put on my battle armor,
 194  you pray to lord Zeus, the son of Cronos —
 195  in silence, to yourselves, so the Trojans do not hear —
 196  or aloud, for that matter, since we fear no man in any case.[12]
 197  For no man by force, by his will against my will, shall drive me off,
 198  nor by know-how either; I hope I was not born in Salamis
 199  and raised there to be such a know-nothing as that."
 200  So he spoke, and they prayed to lord Zeus, son of Cronos,
 201  and thus would one say, looking into the wide sky:
 202  "Father Zeus, ruling from Ida, most glorious, greatest:
 203  grant Ajax the victory and the winning of shining glory;
 204  but if you love Hector too, and care for him,
 205  then give to both of them equal strength and glory."
 206  So they spoke; and Ajax armed himself in gleaming bronze.
 207  And when he had put all his armor about his body,
 208  he charged out, huge as Ares comes on
 209  when he goes to war among men whom the son of Cronos
 210  has thrown together to fight in the fury of heart-devouring strife.
 211  Such was gigantic Ajax as he rose, wall of the Achaeans,
 212  smiling with his grim face; and beneath him his feet
 213  went in long strides, and he shook his long-shadowed spear.
 214  And the Argives rejoiced to look at him,
 215  but a dread trembling came into the limbs of every Trojan,
 216  and Hector's own heart hammered in his chest;
 217  but there was no way now to shrink back or retreat
 218  into the crowd of his people: he had made the challenge.
 219  Ajax came near, carrying his shield like a tower,
 220  bronze, of seven oxhides, which Tychius labored to make,[13]
 221  far the best of leather-cutters, whose home was in Hyle:
 222  he made him the glancing shield of seven hides
 223  from well-fed bulls, and hammered on an eighth layer of bronze.
 224  Carrying it before his chest, Telamonian Ajax
 225  stood close in front of Hector, and spoke to him in threat:
 226  "Hector, now you will learn, plainly, man to man,
 227  what sort of chief men the Danaans have among them
 228  even after Achilles, breaker of men, the lion-heart.[14]
 229  He lies among his beaked seagoing ships,
 230  nursing his rage at Agamemnon, shepherd of the people;
 231  but we are such as can stand up against you —
 232  and there are many of us. Begin the fight."
 233  Then great Hector of the flashing helmet answered him:
 234  "Ajax, Zeus-sprung son of Telamon, marshal of the people:
 235  do not test me like some feeble boy
 236  or a woman, who does not know the works of war.
 237  I know battles; I know the killings of men.
 238  I know how to swing my dried oxhide to the right,
 239  I know how to swing it left — that, to me, is shieldcraft that lasts;
 240  I know how to charge into the tumult of fast mares;
 241  and I know, at close quarters, the dance of hostile Ares.[15]
 242  But I have no wish to hit a man of your size
 243  by stealth, watching my moment: openly, if I can."
 244  He spoke, and balancing his long-shadowed spear he hurled it,
 245  and struck the terrible sevenhide shield of Ajax
 246  on the outermost bronze, the eighth layer upon it:
 247  through six folds the unwearying bronze went shearing,
 248  and in the seventh hide it held. Second in turn,
 249  Zeus-sprung Ajax hurled his long-shadowed spear
 250  and struck the shield of Priam's son, balanced every way.[16]
 251  Through the shining shield the heavy spear went,
 252  and through the richly worked corslet it drove;
 253  and straight on beside his flank the spear sheared through
 254  the tunic — but he had swerved aside, and dodged black doom.
 255  Then the two of them, pulling out the long spears with their hands,
 256  fell on each other like lions that eat flesh raw
 257  or wild boars, whose strength does not give out.[17]
 258  Priam's son stabbed the shield full in the middle with his spear,
 259  but the bronze did not break through: its point was bent back.
 260  And Ajax, leaping in, pierced the shield: straight through
 261  the spear went, and staggered him in his fury;
 262  it came cutting in a graze at his neck, and the black blood welled up.
 263  But even so Hector of the flashing helmet did not leave the fight:
 264  he gave ground, and seized in his thick hand a stone
 265  lying in the plain, black and rough and huge;
 266  with it he struck the terrible sevenhide shield of Ajax
 267  full on the central boss, and the bronze rang out around.
 268  Then Ajax in turn lifted a far greater stone,
 269  whirled, and threw it, putting measureless force behind it,
 270  and broke the shield inward with the millstone rock
 271  and hurt his knees under him; and he was stretched on his back,
 272  pinned to the shield. But Apollo straightened him at once.[18]
 273  And now they would have been stabbing hand to hand with swords,
 274  had not the heralds, messengers of Zeus and of men,
 275  come between — one from the Trojans, one from the bronze-shirted
 276  Achaeans: Talthybius and Idaeus, wise men both.
 277  They held their staffs between the two; and the herald Idaeus,
 278  whose mind was full of wise counsel, spoke his word:
 279  "Fight no more, dear sons; battle no longer.
 280  Zeus the cloud-gatherer loves you both alike,
 281  and both of you are spearmen: that we all know.
 282  And night is coming on. It is good to yield to night as well."[19]
 283  Answering him spoke Telamonian Ajax:
 284  "Idaeus, tell Hector to say those things:
 285  it was he who challenged all our best men to the fight.
 286  Let him lead; and I will follow whatever he does."
 287  Then great Hector of the flashing helmet answered him:
 288  "Ajax — since a god has given you stature and strength
 289  and good sense, and with the spear you are strongest of the Achaeans,
 290  let us stop our fighting and our combat
 291  for today. Later we will fight again, until the god
 292  decides between us, and hands victory to one side or the other.
 293  And night is coming on. It is good to yield to night as well —
 294  so you may gladden all the Achaeans by the ships,
 295  your kinsmen above all, and the companions who are yours;
 296  and I, through the great city of lord Priam,
 297  will gladden the Trojans and the Trojan women with their trailing gowns,
 298  who will go into the gods' assembly-place with prayers for me.
 299  Come — let us give each other glorious gifts,
 300  so that Achaeans and Trojans alike will say:
 301  'The two of them fought in heart-devouring strife,
 302  and parted again joined together in friendship.'"
 303  So speaking, he gave him his silver-studded sword,
 304  bringing it with its sheath and its well-cut baldric;
 305  and Ajax gave his war-belt, bright with crimson.[20]
 306  So the two parted: one went to the Achaean host,
 307  the other to the crowd of the Trojans. And they rejoiced
 308  to see him come to them alive and unhurt,
 309  escaped from the fury of Ajax and his untouchable hands,
 310  and led him to the city, scarcely believing him safe.
 311  And on the other side the strong-greaved Achaeans led Ajax,
 312  glad in his victory, to brilliant Agamemnon.

 313  And when they were gathered in the huts of Atreus' son,
 314  the lord of men Agamemnon slaughtered an ox for them,
 315  a male of five years, to the overmastering son of Cronos.
 316  They flayed it and dressed it, and cut it all apart,
 317  and deftly chopped it small and threaded it on spits,
 318  roasted it with care, and drew it all off.
 319  Then when they had finished the work and made the meal ready,
 320  they feasted, and no heart lacked a fair share of the feast;
 321  and Ajax was honored with the long cuts of the chine[21]
 322  by the hero, Atreus' son, wide-ruling Agamemnon.
 323  And when they had put away desire for eating and drinking,
 324  the old man first of all began to weave his counsel —
 325  Nestor, whose plan had shown itself best before as well.
 326  With good will toward them he spoke and addressed them:
 327  "Son of Atreus, and you other chief men of all the Achaeans:
 328  many of the long-haired Achaeans are dead,
 329  whose dark blood sharp Ares has scattered now
 330  along fair-flowing Scamander, and their souls have gone down to Hades.
 331  Therefore with dawn have the Achaeans stop the fighting;
 332  and let us gather and wheel the corpses back here
 333  with oxen and mules, and burn them
 334  a little off from the ships, so each man may carry the bones
 335  home to the children, when we return to our fatherland.[22]
 336  And around the pyre let us heap a single mound, drawn up
 337  from the plain, one mound for all alike; and against it build
 338  with all speed high towers, a shelter for the ships and ourselves.
 339  And in them let us make close-fitted gates,
 340  so that a road for chariots runs through;
 341  and outside, close by, let us dig a deep ditch
 342  to circle it, and hold off chariots and men,
 343  so the war of the proud Trojans never breaks over us."
 344  So he spoke, and all the kings approved.

 345  Now the Trojans held assembly on the height of Ilion,
 346  a dread assembly, all in tumult, at Priam's doors;
 347  and among them wise Antenor began to speak:
 348  "Hear from me, Trojans and Dardanians and allies,
 349  so I may say what the heart in my chest commands me.
 350  Come now: Argive Helen and her possessions with her
 351  let us give to the sons of Atreus to lead away. Now it is with sworn
 352  oaths made false that we fight; and so I have no hope
 353  that anything will end well for us, unless we do this."[23]
 354  So he spoke and sat down; and among them rose
 355  brilliant Alexandros, husband of lovely-haired Helen,
 356  who spoke to him in answer with winged words:
 357  "Antenor, this talk of yours pleases me no longer;
 358  you know how to think another word, better than this one.
 359  But if you truly say this thing in earnest,
 360  then the gods themselves have destroyed your wits.
 361  And I will speak out among the horse-taming Trojans:
 362  I say it straight out — the woman I will not give back.
 363  But the goods, all I brought from Argos to our house —
 364  all these I am willing to give, and to add more of my own."
 365  So he spoke and sat down; and among them rose
 366  Priam, Dardanus' descendant, the equal of the gods in counsel.
 367  With good will toward them he spoke and addressed them:
 368  "Hear from me, Trojans and Dardanians and allies,
 369  so I may say what the heart in my chest commands me.
 370  Now take your supper through the city, as before,
 371  and remember the watch, and stay awake, every man.
 372  At dawn let Idaeus go to the hollow ships
 373  to tell the sons of Atreus, Agamemnon and Menelaus,
 374  the word of Alexandros, for whose sake this quarrel rose;
 375  and to say this shrewd word too: whether they are willing
 376  to rest from the harsh-crying war until we burn
 377  the dead. Later we will fight again, until the god
 378  decides between us, and hands victory to one side or the other."
 379  So he spoke, and they listened well to him and obeyed;
 380  and they took their supper through the army, in their companies.
 381  At dawn Idaeus went to the hollow ships,
 382  and found the Danaans, squires of Ares, in assembly
 383  by the stern of Agamemnon's ship; and among them,
 384  standing in the middle, the far-calling herald spoke:
 385  "Son of Atreus, and you other chief men of all the Achaeans:
 386  Priam and the other proud Trojans command me
 387  to tell you — if it should be welcome and sweet to you —
 388  the word of Alexandros, for whose sake this quarrel rose.
 389  The goods that Alexandros brought to Troy in his hollow
 390  ships — how he should have perished first! —
 391  all these he is willing to give, and to add more of his own.
 392  But the wedded wife of glorious Menelaus
 393  he says he will not give — though the Trojans, I tell you, command it.
 394  And they told me to say this too: whether you are willing
 395  to rest from the harsh-crying war until we burn
 396  the dead. Later we will fight again, until the god
 397  decides between us, and hands victory to one side or the other."
 398  So he spoke, and they all went still in silence.
 399  At long last Diomedes, good at the war-cry, spoke:
 400  "Let no man now accept the goods of Alexandros —
 401  nor Helen either. It is known, even to a plain fool,
 402  that the cables of destruction are fastened on the Trojans already."[24]
 403  So he spoke, and all the sons of the Achaeans shouted,
 404  marveling at the word of Diomedes, breaker of horses;
 405  and then the lord Agamemnon spoke to Idaeus:
 406  "Idaeus — you hear the word of the Achaeans yourself,
 407  how they give you their answer. And it pleases me so.
 408  But as for the dead — I do not grudge their burning.
 409  There is no stinting toward the bodies of the dead:
 410  once they are gone, be quick with the fire's appeasement.
 411  And let Zeus the loud-thunderer, Hera's lord, witness the oaths."
 412  So speaking he lifted up the scepter to all the gods,
 413  and Idaeus went back toward holy Ilion.
 414  The Trojans and Dardanians were sitting in assembly,
 415  all gathered together, waiting for the moment
 416  Idaeus should come; and he came, and delivered his message,
 417  standing in the middle. Then they made ready with all speed,
 418  in two parties: one to bring the corpses, one to fetch wood;
 419  and the Argives on their side, from the well-benched ships,
 420  hurried too — some to bring the corpses, others to fetch wood.

 421  The sun was just striking the fields with its first light,
 422  climbing into the sky out of the deep, soft-flowing
 423  stream of Ocean, when the two sides met each other.
 424  Then it was hard to know each man again;
 425  but washing off the clotted gore with water,
 426  and letting warm tears fall, they lifted them onto the wagons.
 427  But great Priam would not let them wail; so in silence[25]
 428  they heaped the corpses on the pyre, grieving at heart,
 429  and when they had burned them in the fire, went to holy Ilion.
 430  And just so, on the other side, the strong-greaved Achaeans
 431  heaped the corpses on the pyre, grieving at heart,
 432  and when they had burned them in the fire, went to the hollow ships.

 433  And when it was not yet dawn, but the night still half-light,[26]
 434  then a picked company of Achaeans woke around the pyre,
 435  and they heaped around it a single mound, drawn up
 436  from the plain, one mound for all alike; and against it they built
 437  a wall with high towers, a shelter for the ships and themselves.
 438  And in them they set close-fitted gates,
 439  so that a road for chariots ran through;
 440  and outside they dug a deep ditch hard against it,
 441  wide and great, and planted a row of stakes within it.
 442  So the long-haired Achaeans labored;
 443  and the gods, sitting beside Zeus the lord of lightning,
 444  watched the great work of the bronze-shirted Achaeans.
 445  And Poseidon the earth-shaker began to speak among them:
 446  "Father Zeus, is there a mortal left on the boundless earth
 447  who will still tell the immortals his mind and purpose?
 448  Do you not see? The long-haired Achaeans
 449  have built themselves a wall to landward of the ships, and driven
 450  a ditch around it — and gave the gods no glorious hecatombs!
 451  The fame of this will spread as far as the dawn-light scatters,
 452  and men will forget that wall which I and Phoebus Apollo
 453  built for the hero Laomedon, laboring hard."
 454  Deeply troubled, Zeus the cloud-gatherer answered him:
 455  "Shame, wide-ruling earth-shaker — what a thing to say!
 456  Some other god might tremble at this thought,
 457  one far feebler than you in hand and fury:
 458  your fame will spread as far as the dawn-light scatters.
 459  Come now: when the long-haired Achaeans are gone
 460  again with their ships to their dear fatherland,
 461  break the wall up, sweep it all into the sea,
 462  and hide the wide beach over again with sand,
 463  so the great wall of the Achaeans may be effaced."[27]
 464  So they spoke such things to one another;
 465  and the sun went down, and the Achaeans' work was finished,
 466  and they slaughtered oxen through the huts, and took their supper.
 467  And ships had come in from Lemnos, carrying wine,
 468  many of them, sent out by Jason's son Euneus,
 469  whom Hypsipyle bore to Jason, shepherd of the people.
 470  Separately, for the sons of Atreus, Agamemnon and Menelaus,
 471  Jason's son had given a thousand measures of wine to carry.
 472  From these ships the long-haired Achaeans bought their wine,
 473  some with bronze, and some with gleaming iron,
 474  some with hides, and some with the live cattle themselves,
 475  and some with slaves; and they set the rich feast going.[28]
 476  All night long the long-haired Achaeans
 477  feasted, and the Trojans through the city, and their allies;
 478  and all night long Zeus the counselor devised evil against them,
 479  thundering terribly. And green fear took hold of them:[29]
 480  they poured the wine from their cups onto the ground, and no man
 481  dared drink before he poured to the overmastering son of Cronos.
 482  Then they lay down, and took the gift of sleep.

Notes

[1] (7.8-10) Paris kills Menesthius, son of Areithous "the club-man" —
    the very Areithous whose story Nestor will tell in full at 136-149:
    the son dies at the book's door, the father's legend follows. A
    plant-and-payoff inside a single book.

[2] (7.44-53) Helenus "put together in his heart" the gods' private
    plan — how the seer overheard a conversation between Apollo and
    Athena was already a problem for ancient critics; Aristarchus
    athetized 53 ("so I heard it in the voice of the gods"), the line
    that makes the eavesdropping explicit (apparatus, Line 53). Kept:
    seercraft as reception of divine speech is exactly Helenus's office
    (6.76).

[3] (7.58-61) Athena and Apollo settle on the oak of Zeus "in the
    likeness of vulture birds, delighting in the men" — the gods as
    spectators, perched carrion-fashion above the duel they arranged.
    The poem's frankest image of the divine audience.

[4] (7.76-91) Hector's terms and the mound-speech: armor to the victor,
    the corpse home for its "due of fire" — and then the imagined
    sailor of a later age reading the tomb by the Hellespont. The
    poem's clearest statement of the kleos-economy, spoken from the
    winner's side of an imagined victory: even the man Hector kills is
    conscripted into Hector's undying fame. (The Iliad's audience knew
    such mounds on that shore.)

[5] (7.96) "Women of Achaea, men of Achaea no longer" — Menelaus
    borrows Thersites' taunt verbatim (2.235): the loudmouth's insult
    has become respectable under shame's pressure. (Echo note; English
    repeats 2.235's rendering.)

[6] (7.113-114) "Even Achilles... shivered to meet this man." The
    absent hero as unit of measure again (cf. 2.768-770, 5.788-791) —
    here bent into hyperbole: nothing in the poem shows Achilles
    fearing Hector, and Book 22 will show the reverse. Agamemnon's
    flattery of Hector serves his need to stop his brother.

[7] (7.120-121) αἴσιμα παρειπών — "his urging was of things due" — the
    same narratorial formula that follows Agamemnon's atrocity-counsel
    at 6.62. Here the counsel is unimpeachable (saving Menelaus from a
    fatal mismatch), which weighs on the Book 6 dispute: the formula
    commends the persuasion's aptness, not its morality.

[8] (7.124-131) Nestor conjures Peleus at home — the old man who once
    joyfully catalogued the Argives' lineages, now imagined praying
    for death at news of their shame. First full sounding of the
    Peleus-motif: the father at home as the war's moral audience
    (Odysseus will weaponize the same figure to Achilles at 9.252-259).

[9] (7.132-156) The Ereuthalion story, promised at 4.319 and delivered
    here: Areithous the Club-man with his iron club (iron again the
    marked exception, cf. 4.123), killed by craft in a lane too narrow
    to swing in; the armor's passage from Ares' gift to Lycurgus to
    Ereuthalion; and young Nestor, "youngest-born of all," taking down
    the giant. This Lycurgus is an Arcadian mortal — not the Thracian
    god-fighter of 6.130-140 who shares the name.

[10] (7.161-169) The nine volunteers — the poem's explicit roster of
     the best available Achaeans with Achilles out: both Atreidae,
     Diomedes, both Ajaxes, Idomeneus, Meriones, Eurypylus, Thoas,
     Odysseus. The lot-prayer at 179-180 then ranks the top three in
     the army's own eyes: Ajax, Diomedes, Agamemnon.

[11] (7.175-189) The lot-procedure: each man scratches his mark
     (σῆμα) on his token; the herald carries the leaping lot around
     until its owner recognizes it. Like the "baneful signs" of 6.168,
     marks that identify without being read as language — the poem's
     world writes tokens, not texts.

[12] (7.195-199) Ajax's instructions — pray silently so the Trojans
     don't hear, "or aloud, for that matter, since we fear no man" —
     athetized by Zenodotus, Aristophanes, and Aristarchus all three
     (apparatus, Line 195-199). The swagger is pure Ajax; kept.

[13] (7.219-223) The tower shield: seven oxhides and an eighth face of
     bronze, made by Tychius of Hyle, "far the best of leather-cutters"
     — the craftsman-vignette again (cf. Phereclus, 5.59-64). Ajax's
     body-length shield is older military technology than the round
     shields elsewhere in the poem — a fossil the tradition preserved
     with the hero it belonged to.

[14] (7.226-232) "even after Achilles, breaker of men, the lion-heart"
     — Ajax concedes the ranking in the act of boasting; the poem's
     second-best hero is the one who says "second" out loud (cf.
     2.768-769).

[15] (7.237-241) Hector's credo: shield-work right and left, the charge,
     and "at close quarters, the dance of hostile Ares" — war as a
     craft with figures and steps (μέλπεσθαι, properly of dance and
     song). The self-description of a man who has made himself skilled
     at what he tells Andromache he has "learned" to do (6.444-445).

[16] (7.244-254) The spear-exchange replays the Paris-Menelaus duel
     verbatim: 250-254 = 3.355-360, and the English here repeats the
     Book 3 rendering word for word. The rematch of Book 3 with the
     war's real principals is scored on the same notes — except that
     this time both men are too good for the first exchange to decide
     anything.

[17] (7.255-257) The lions-and-boars simile — athetized by Zenodotus
     (apparatus, Line 255-257); nearly the same pair as 5.782-783.
     Kept, as the moment the formal duel becomes simply combat.

[18] (7.268-272) The stones: Hector's blow rings the bronze; Ajax's
     millstone rock breaks the shield inward and folds Hector's knees
     — "but Apollo straightened him at once." The god's whole
     intervention in five words; without it the duel (and the poem)
     ends here.

[19] (7.279-282) The heralds part them with staffs, and Idaeus — the
     Trojan herald of 3.248 — gives the evening its proverb: "It is
     good to yield to night as well." Night as the arbiter both
     armies obey; Hector repeats the line back with full agreement
     (293).

[20] (7.287-305) The gift-exchange: Hector's silver-studded sword for
     Ajax's crimson war-belt, "so that Achaeans and Trojans alike will
     say... they parted again joined together in friendship."
     Tradition made both gifts fatal: Ajax falls on Hector's sword
     (Sophocles' Ajax), and Hector's corpse is dragged by Ajax's belt
     — "the gifts of enemies are no gifts." The poem itself says
     nothing; the exchange is left shining. (Aristarchus athetized
     295, the "kinsmen and companions" line, within Hector's speech.)

[21] (7.321-322) The chine — the unbroken length of the back-cut — is
     the portion of honor, awarded to Ajax by Agamemnon: the feast as
     scoreboard (cf. Idomeneus's ever-full cup, 4.257-263).

[22] (7.327-343) Nestor's double proposal: burn the dead, and build
     the wall. Aristarchus athetized 334-335 — carrying the bones
     "home to the children" reflects classical Athenian practice (the
     state funeral), not Homeric cremation-and-mound, and the couplet
     has long been suspected as an Athenian interpolation (apparatus,
     Line 334-335). The wall itself is the poem's new stage-set: Books
     8-15 will be fought around it.

[23] (7.345-353) Antenor moves to give Helen back — the appeasement
     party in Troy made explicit (he hosted the pre-war embassy,
     3.205-224; later tradition spared his house at the sack for it).
     Aristarchus athetized 353. Paris's counter-offer — goods yes,
     "the woman I will not give back" — and Priam's meek relay of it
     complete the pattern: Troy's assembly knows the right answer and
     cannot act on it.

[24] (7.399-402) Diomedes' refusal: take neither the goods "nor Helen
     either — the cables of destruction are fastened on the Trojans
     already." The rope-image answers Menelaus's "ends of victory...
     held above" (101-102): the gods hold the rope-ends of outcomes.
     The young man silent under rebuke in Book 4 now answers embassies
     for the army, with the crowd shouting assent — the arc runs
     straight on to 9.32-49. (Echo note.)

[25] (7.421-432) The shared field at dawn: both sides washing the
     clotted blood from the dead they cannot recognize, "letting warm
     tears fall" — and Priam forbidding lament, as the Achaeans mourn
     unforbidden. The armies' identical griefs are given identical
     lines (428-429 = 431-432, Ilion for the ships); the translation
     keeps the symmetry exact.

[26] (7.433-441) "not yet dawn, but the night still half-light"
     (ἀμφιλύκη νύξ — only here in Homer): in the grey hour, the
     Achaeans raise one mound "for all alike" (ἄκριτον — undivided,
     no man singled) — the common grave of a common army, in contrast
     to every individual mound the poem imagines (cf. 86-91).

[27] (7.443-464) The gods audit the wall: Poseidon's professional
     jealousy (he and Apollo built Laomedon's wall — the last time
     Troy's ruler cheated them, 21.441-457), the missing hecatombs,
     and Zeus's remedy: erase it when the war ends, sea and sand over
     everything. Zenodotus, Aristophanes, and Aristarchus athetized
     the whole exchange (apparatus, Line 443-464); Aristotle admired
     it as the poet "effacing" his own invention — the poem arranges
     in advance why no trace of its greatest earthwork survives on
     the real coast (the demolition comes at 12.13-33).

[28] (7.467-475) The wine-fleet from Lemnos, sent by Euneus, son of
     Jason and Hypsipyle — the poem's one glance at the Argonaut
     generation, now middle-aged commerce. The market-list prices the
     camp's economy: bronze, iron, hides, live cattle — "and some with
     slaves" (ἀνδραπόδεσσι, the only occurrence of the classical word
     for chattel-slaves in Homer; all three Alexandrians athetized the
     line, partly for that anachronism). Rendered "slaves" under the
     project's policy: the traffic the poem's captives fear is here,
     briefly, a line-item.

[29] (7.478-482) The book closes on the uneasy feast: Zeus thundering
     all night "devising evil," green fear (χλωρὸν δέος — the fixed
     color of dread) on both armies, and no man drinking undedicated
     wine. After a day of institutions — duel, truce, wall, market —
     the last word is the god's weather, and it is against them.
