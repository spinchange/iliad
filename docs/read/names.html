Abantes Ἄβαντεςpeople

Say: uh-BAN-teez

The Euboean contingent of the Catalogue of Ships, holding Chalcis, Eretria, Histiaea, Cerinthus, Dios, Carystus and Styra, and led by Elephenor son of Chalcodon. The poem marks them twice over: they wear their hair long behind and shorn in front, and they are spearmen who close to break corslets rather than fight at range.

Kin: led by Elephenor, Chalcodon's son.

Refs: 2.536, 2.541, 2.542, 4.464. Books 2, 4.

See also: Achaeans.

Abydos Ἄβυδοςplace

Say: uh-BY-dos

A town on the Asian shore of the Hellespont, held with Sestus and Arisbe by Asius' contingent of Trojan allies. It is named twice more for the men who come from it: Democoon, Priam's bastard son, who kept the fast mares there, and the Phaenops whose shape Apollo borrows to shame Hector.

Refs: 2.836, 4.500, 17.584. Books 2, 4, 17.

See also: Asius, Arisbe, Hellespont.

Acamas Ἀκάμαςmortal

Say: AK-uh-mas

Two men of the name fight on the Trojan side, and the poem keeps them apart. The first is a son of Antenor, named with his brother Archelochus in the Catalogue and again among Hector's captains at 11.60; in Book 14 he stands over Archelochus' body and kills Promachus in vengeance, vaunting over him, and Meriones runs him down as he mounts his chariot at 16.342. The second is a Thracian leader, Eussorus' son, "the man who was best among the Thracians," whom Telamonian Ajax kills at 6.8; Ares had worn his likeness to rally the Trojans a book earlier (5.462).

Kin: the Trojan Acamas is Antenor's son and Archelochus' brother; the Thracian is Eussorus' son.

Kills: Promachus (14.476–477). Killed by: Meriones (16.342); the Thracian Acamas by Ajax (6.8).

Refs: 2.823, 2.844, 5.462, 6.8, 11.60, 12.100, 14.476, 14.478, 14.488,

16.342. Books 2, 5, 6, 11, 12, 14, 16.

See also: Antenor, Archelochus, Peiroos, Thracians, Meriones.

Achaea Ἀχαιΐςplace

Say: uh-KEE-uh

The Achaeans' homeland, named in the formula "horse-pasturing Argos and Achaea of the beautiful women" that closes the terms of the duel in Book 3, and turned against the army as a taunt in Menelaus' reproach — "Women of Achaea, men of Achaea no longer" (7.96).

Refs: 3.75, 3.258, 7.96, 11.770. Books 3, 7, 11.

See also: Achaeans, Argos.

Achaeans Ἀχαιοίpeople

Say: uh-KEE-unz

The poem's default name for the Greeks besieging Troy, used interchangeably with Argives and Danaans for the same host. After Zeus and the Trojans it is the commonest name in the Iliad: the army is the standing audience before which honor is won and lost, the assembly that must be persuaded, and the collective whose griefs the second line of the poem announces (1.2).

Epithets: "the long-haired Achaeans" (κάρη κομόωντες Ἀχαιοί, 2.11); "the strong-greaved Achaeans" (ἐϋκνήμιδες Ἀχαιοί, 1.17); "the bronze-shirted Achaeans" (χαλκοχίτωνες Ἀχαιοί, 1.371).

Also called: Argives; Danaans — metrically convenient synonyms for the same host, not distinct peoples. The Myrmidons and Hellenes are Achilles' own contingent and are not synonyms for the whole army.

Refs: 1.2, 1.17, 1.371, 2.11, 2.51 …and throughout.

Books 1–24.

See also: Argives, Danaans, Myrmidons, Hellenes, Achaea, Agamemnon.

Achilles Ἀχιλλεύςmortal

Say: uh-KIL-eez

The poem's subject, greatest of the Achaeans, whose wrath is its announced theme and its first word. Agamemnon's seizure of Briseis drives him from the fighting; his refusal of the embassy in Book 9 hardens the quarrel past mending; and Patroclus' death in his own armor brings him back not to win the war but to kill Hector. The Iliad ends not with that vengeance but with him giving the body up to Priam, having been argued out of his rage by an old man who kisses his hands.

Epithets: "swift-footed" (πόδας ὠκύς / ποδάρκης, 1.58); "brilliant" (δῖος, 1.7); "Zeus-sprung" (διογενής, 1.489).

Also called: Peleus' son; Aeacus' grandson; Thetis' son.

Kin: son of Peleus and the sea-nymph Thetis; grandson of Aeacus; father of Neoptolemus, reared on Scyros. Companion of Patroclus; foster-son of Phoenix.

Refs: 1.1, 1.58, 1.348, 9.199, 16.2, 18.22, 22.330, 24.596 …and throughout.

Books 1–2, 4–24.

See also: Patroclus, Hector, Agamemnon, Thetis, Peleus, Aeacus, Briseis, Priam, Phoenix.

Actor Ἄκτωρmortal

Say: AK-tor

A name borne by more than one man of the older generation, all of them fathers rather than fighters. The poem's Actors include Azeus' son, in whose house Astyoche bore Ares' twin sons Ascalaphus and Ialmenus; the father of Menoetius, and so Patroclus' grandfather (11.785, 16.14); the father of Echecles (16.189); and the father of the twins Cteatus and Eurytus, called both "Actor's line" and "the two sons of Actor," who crowd past Nestor's horses in his reminiscence of the Epeian games.

Kin: variously father of Menoetius, of Echecles, and of the Molione twins.

Refs: 2.513, 2.621, 11.750, 11.785, 13.186, 16.14, 16.189, 23.638.

Books 2, 11, 13, 16, 23.

See also: Menoetius, Patroclus, Amarynceus.

Adamas Ἀδάμαςmortal

Say: AD-uh-mas

Son of Asius, one of the Trojan captains who storm the wall in Book 12. In the fighting at the ships he stabs Antilochus' shield and slips back into the ranks "dodging doom," but Meriones catches him with a spear-cast in the groin and he dies writhing like a roped bull. Deiphobus names him among the dead Trojan leaders at 13.771.

Kin: son of Asius, Hyrtacus' son.

Killed by: Meriones (13.567–575).

Refs: 12.140, 13.561, 13.566, 13.760, 13.771. Books 12, 13.

See also: Asius, Meriones, Antilochus.

Admetus Ἄδμητοςmortal

Say: ad-MEE-tus

Father of Eumelus and husband of Alcestis, "brilliant among women, loveliest of the daughters of Pelias." He does not come to Troy: the name is a patronymic throughout, marking his son, who brings eleven ships from Pherae and drives the best horses in the Achaean host.

Kin: husband of Alcestis; father of Eumelus.

Refs: 2.713, 2.714, 23.289, 23.391, 23.532. Books 2, 23.

See also: Eumelus, Alcestis.

Adrastus Ἄδρηστοςmortal

Say: uh-DRAS-tus

King of Sicyon in the older generation — "Sicyon, where Adrastus first was king" — and owner of Arion, the swift horse "whose line was from the gods," against which Antilochus is told even a better driver could not have won. His daughters marry into the poem's Argive houses: Aegialeia is named as Diomedes' wife at 5.412, and Tydeus married a daughter of his after settling at Argos (14.121). The translation spells him Adrastus and the Trojan-side leader Adrestus, keeping two men distinct whom one spelling would merge; see `canon.md`.

Kin: father of Aegialeia; father-in-law of Tydeus.

Refs: 2.572, 5.412, 14.121, 23.347. Books 2, 5, 14, 23.

See also: Adrestus, Arion, Diomedes, Sicyon.

Adrestus Ἄδρηστοςmortal

Say: uh-DRES-tus

Three Trojan-side men bear this spelling. One leads the contingent from Adresteia and Apaesus with his brother Amphius, sons of Merops of Percote, who foresaw their deaths and could not keep them home. The second is the suppliant of Book 6, thrown from a bolting chariot, who clasps Menelaus' knees and offers ransom. Menelaus is on the point of granting it when Agamemnon runs up and shames him — "Soft-hearted Menelaus!" — so that Menelaus pushes the man back with his hand and Agamemnon does the killing (6.62–65). A third falls to Patroclus. The translation reserves Adrastus for the Sicyon king.

Kin: the Percotian Adrestus is Merops' son and Amphius' brother.

Killed by: Agamemnon (6.63–65); the third by Patroclus (16.694).

Refs: 2.830, 6.37, 6.45, 6.63, 16.694. Books 2, 6, 16.

See also: Adrastus, Amphius, Merops, Agamemnon, Menelaus.

Aeacus Αἰακόςmortal

Say: EE-uh-kus

Peleus' father and Achilles' grandfather, himself sprung from Zeus (21.189). He never appears: in all its occurrences the name is a patronymic, and the translation keeps the generations apart by a fixed rule — "Aeacus' son" is Peleus, "Aeacus' grandson" is Achilles, except inside the fixed epithet formulas, where "the swift-footed son of Aeacus" and "Aeacus' blameless son" mean Achilles. See `translation/CONVENTIONS.md`.

Kin: son of Zeus; father of Peleus; grandfather of Achilles.

Refs: 2.860, 2.874, 9.184, 16.15, 16.140, 16.854, 21.178, 21.189 …and

throughout. Books 2, 9–11, 16–18, 21, 23.

See also: Achilles, Peleus.

Aeneas Αἰνείαςmortal

Say: ee-NEE-us or uh-NEE-us

Leader of the Dardanians and, after Hector, Troy's best fighter. Son of Anchises and Aphrodite, he is twice snatched from death by gods who have plans for him — by his mother and Apollo when Diomedes crushes his hip with a boulder (5.311ff), and by Poseidon when Achilles has him beaten, in the scene that gives the poem its one glance past its own ending: Aeneas is destined to escape, "so that the race of Dardanus not perish seedless," and his line will rule the Trojans hereafter (20.302–308).

Also called: Anchises' son.

Kin: son of Anchises and Aphrodite; of the line of Dardanus through Assaracus.

Refs: 2.820, 5.166, 5.311, 5.445, 13.459, 16.620, 17.323, 20.302 …and

throughout. Books 2, 5–6, 8, 11–17, 20, 23.

See also: Anchises, Aphrodite, Dardanus, Dardanians, Achilles, Diomedes.

Aesepus Αἴσηποςmortal

Say: ee-SEE-pus

A Trojan twin, killed with his brother Pedasus by Euryalus in the general rout of Book 6. The poem gives the pair a full genealogy at the moment of their death: the water-nymph Abarbarea bore them to Bucolion, Laomedon's eldest son, got in secret while he was shepherding. The name is also a river of the Troad, one of those the gods turn against the Achaean wall (12.21).

Kin: son of Bucolion and the nymph Abarbarea; twin of Pedasus.

Killed by: Euryalus (6.20–28).

Refs: 2.825, 4.91, 6.21, 12.21. Books 2, 4, 6, 12.

See also: Pedasus, Bucolion, Euryalus, Laomedon.

Aethe Αἴθηother

Say: EE-thee

Agamemnon's mare, lent to Menelaus for the chariot race at Patroclus' funeral — a gift to Agamemnon from Echepolus son of Anchises, made so that he might stay home from the war. Nestor's son Antilochus is warned that she will "pour shame" on his horses, and her fury nearly carries Menelaus past him at the finish.

Kin: yoked with Menelaus' own horse Podargus.

Refs: 23.295, 23.408, 23.524. Book 23.

See also: Podargus, Menelaus, Antilochus, Echepolus.

Aetolians Αἰτωλοίpeople

Say: ee-TOH-lee-unz

The contingent from Pleuron, Olenus, Pylene, Chalcis and Calydon, led to Troy by Thoas son of Andraemon with forty ships. The Catalogue explains the odd succession: Oeneus' sons were dead and Meleager with them, so the whole rule fell to Thoas. Their older war against the Curetes over Calydon is the setting of Phoenix's Meleager story in Book 9.

Kin: led by Thoas, Andraemon's son.

Refs: 2.638, 2.643, 4.527, 9.529, 9.531, 13.218, 23.633. Books 2, 4–5, 9,

13, 15, 23.

See also: Thoas, Meleager, Oeneus, Calydon, Curetes.

Agamemnon Ἀγαμέμνωνmortal

Say: ag-uh-MEM-non

Commander of the whole Achaean expedition and Achilles' antagonist in the quarrel that starts the poem. His refusal to give Chryseis back brings the plague, and his taking of Briseis in compensation drives Achilles from the fighting; the disaster that follows is his to answer for, and in Book 19 he answers for it by blaming Blindness sent from Zeus. His authority rests on the sceptre whose descent Hephaestus to Zeus to Hermes to Pelops to Atreus to Thyestes to himself the poem traces at 2.101–108: he rules "many islands and all of Argos," and he is consistently a worse fighter than the men he commands.

Epithets: "lord of men" (ἄναξ ἀνδρῶν, 1.7); "wide-ruling" (εὐρὺ κρείων, 1.102); "shepherd of the people" (ποιμὴν λαῶν, of Agamemnon at 2.243) — the last is a general royal epithet, used of Atreus and others too.

Also called: Atreus' son — in the singular this means Agamemnon unless the scene is Menelaus'; see `canon.md`.

Kin: son of Atreus, grandson of Pelops; brother of Menelaus; husband of Clytemnestra.

Refs: 1.24, 1.130, 2.100, 2.107, 4.148, 9.114, 11.91, 19.86 …and throughout.

Books 1–11, 13–14, 16–19, 23–24.

See also: Achilles, Menelaus, Atreus, Briseis, Chryseis, Blindness.

Agenor Ἀγήνωρmortal

Say: uh-JEE-nor

Antenor's son, "a man blameless and strong," and the last Trojan to stand in Achilles' path before Hector. Apollo puts courage in him at 21.545 and he turns to face the charge, breaking off his own debate with the formula the poem gives its cornered men — "But why does my own heart argue these things with me?" (21.562, as at 11.407, 17.97, 22.122, 22.385). He casts, and Apollo snatches him away in mist, then wears his shape to decoy Achilles from the walls: the trick that lets the routed Trojans get inside the gates.

Kin: son of Antenor; father of Echeclus (20.474).

Refs: 4.467, 11.59, 12.93, 13.490, 15.340, 20.474, 21.545, 21.595, 21.600.

Books 4, 11–16, 20–21.

See also: Antenor, Achilles, Apollo, Hector.

Ajax Αἴαςmortal

Say: AY-jaks

Telamon's son, from Salamis with twelve ships: the biggest man in the army and its best fighter after Achilles, whom Helen picks out from the wall as "gigantic Ajax, wall of the Achaeans." He carries a tower-like shield of seven oxhides and an eighth layer of bronze, made by Tychius of Hyle. The lot chooses him to duel Hector in Book 7, and they part at nightfall exchanging gifts; he is one of the three sent to Achilles in Book 9, where his short blunt speech comes nearest to moving him; and in Book 15 he holds the last deck alone against the fire, giving ground plank by plank. Unqualified "Ajax" means him unless the line marks the other.

Epithets: "wall of the Achaeans" (ἕρκος Ἀχαιῶν, 3.229).

Also called: Telamonian Ajax; Telamon's son; the great Ajax.

Kin: son of Telamon; half-brother of Teucer.

Refs: 2.557, 3.229, 7.183, 7.219, 9.622, 15.727, 17.132 …and throughout.

Books 1–18, 23.

See also: Ajax son of Oïleus, Ajaxes, Telamon, Teucer, Hector, Achilles.

Ajax son of Oïleus Αἴας Ὀϊλῆος υἱόςmortal

Say: AY-jaks, son of oh-EYE-lyoos

Leader of the Locrians with forty ships: "the lesser Ajax, in no way so big as Telamonian Ajax / but far smaller: a little man, in a linen corslet, / but with the spear he surpassed all Hellenes and Achaeans" (2.528–530). He is the fastest Achaean in the chase — most of the kills in the Book 14 rout are his, "for there was no one like him for speed of foot" when men broke and ran — and the savage half of the pair: it is he who cuts off Imbrius' head in wrath for Amphimachus and sends it "rolling like a ball through the crowd" to fall at Hector's feet (13.202–205). In the footrace of the games Athena trips him at a dead run into the cattle-dung, and he stands spitting it out while the Argives laugh at him (23.774–784).

Also called: swift Ajax; the lesser Ajax; Oïleus' son. Unqualified "Ajax" is the Telamonian — see `canon.md`.

Kin: son of Oïleus; half-brother of Medon, Oïleus' bastard son (13.694–695).

Refs: 2.527, 13.203, 13.701, 14.520, 23.473, 23.754, 23.774. Books 2, 13–14,

23.

See also: Ajax, Ajaxes, Oïleus, Locrians, Medon.

Ajaxes Αἴαντεmortal

Say: AY-jak-seez

The dual — Telamonian Ajax and Ajax son of Oïleus fighting as a single unit, and the poem's standing image of a defensive line that holds. They are always found where the wall or the ships are about to break: marshaling the towers in Book 12, addressed first by Poseidon when he comes to rally the army in Book 13, carrying Imbrius' body off like two lions with a goat, and holding the line over Patroclus' corpse in Book 17. The Book 4 simile of a black storm-cloud coming on before the West Wind is given to them together.

Also called: the two Ajaxes.

Refs: 2.406, 4.273, 4.280, 12.265, 13.46, 13.197, 17.507, 17.732, 18.157.

Books 2, 4–8, 10, 12–13, 16–18.

See also: Ajax, Ajax son of Oïleus, Poseidon, Patroclus.

Alastor Ἀλάστωρmortal

Say: uh-LAS-tor

At least three men. One is a Pylian, named among Nestor's captains at 4.295 and twice paired with Mecisteus son of Echius as a stretcher-bearer, carrying the wounded off to the ships (8.333, 13.422). Another is a Trojan-side victim in Odysseus' rally at 5.677. The third is named only as a father: Tros, "Alastor's son," is the suppliant who reaches for Achilles' knees and is killed anyway at 20.463.

Refs: 4.295, 5.677, 8.333, 13.422, 20.463. Books 4–5, 8, 13, 20.

See also: Nestor, Odysseus, Tros.

Alcathous Ἀλκάθοοςmortal

Say: al-KATH-oh-us

A Trojan captain, Aesyetes' son, married to Anchises' eldest daughter Hippodameia. Idomeneus kills him in one of the poem's strangest deaths: Poseidon charms his eyes and shackles his limbs so that he stands unmoving "like a grave-pillar or a tree in high leaf" and cannot dodge, and his heart goes on beating hard enough to shake the spear standing in it. Deiphobus calls Aeneas to avenge the man who reared him.

Kin: son of Aesyetes; husband of Hippodameia, Anchises' eldest daughter; brother-in-law and foster-father to Aeneas.

Killed by: Idomeneus (13.427–444).

Refs: 12.93, 13.428, 13.465, 13.496. Books 12, 13.

See also: Idomeneus, Aeneas, Anchises, Poseidon.

Alcimedon Ἀλκιμέδωνmortal

Say: al-SIM-uh-don

Son of Laerces and grandson of Haemon, one of the five company-commanders Achilles sets over the Myrmidons when Patroclus goes out (16.197). In Book 17 he finds Automedon fighting alone from Achilles' chariot with Patroclus dead and Hector wearing the armor, tells him he has lost his wits, and takes the reins so that Automedon can fight on foot.

Kin: son of Laerces, grandson of Haemon.

Refs: 16.197, 17.467, 17.475, 17.481, 17.500, 17.501. Books 16, 17.

See also: Automedon, Achilles, Myrmidons, Patroclus.

Alpheius Ἀλφειόςplace

Say: al-FEE-us

The great river of the western Peloponnese, "which flows wide through the Pylians' land." It marks Nestor's country in his reminiscences — the cattle-raid that reached its sacred stream by midday, the ford named in the Catalogue — and as a river-god it heads a Pylian genealogy: Alpheius begot Ortilochus, who fathered Diocles, whose twin sons Aeneas kills at 5.541–560.

Refs: 2.592, 5.545, 11.712, 11.726, 11.728. Books 2, 5, 11.

See also: Nestor, Pylos, Elis.

Altes Ἄλτηςmortal

Say: AL-teez

Lord of the Lelegians, "holding steep Pedasus on the Satnioeis," and father of Laothoe, one of Priam's wives. He is named only through his grandsons: Lycaon recites the descent while begging Achilles for his life at 21.85, and Priam names him again mourning Lycaon and Polydorus at 22.51, recalling the dowry "the famous name" gave with his daughter.

Kin: father of Laothoe; grandfather of Lycaon and Polydorus.

Refs: 21.85, 21.86, 22.51. Books 21, 22.

See also: Laothoe, Lycaon, Polydorus, Priam, Pedasus.

Amarynceus Ἀμαρυγκεύςmortal

Say: am-uh-RIN-syoos

An Epeian lord of the older generation, father of Diores. Nestor's last long reminiscence is of the funeral games his sons held for him at Buprasium, where Nestor beat every man there at boxing, wrestling, running and the spear, and lost only the chariot race to the two sons of Actor — the memory he offers Achilles as he accepts a prize he no longer has to compete for.

Kin: father of Diores.

Refs: 2.622, 4.517, 23.630. Books 2, 4, 23.

See also: Diores, Nestor, Actor, Epeians, Buprasium.

Amazons Ἀμαζόνεςpeople

Say: AM-uh-zonz

A people of women who fight, named only in the back-story and only twice: Priam recalls campaigning against them in Phrygia in his youth, and Bellerophon is set to fight them as the third of the impossible tasks he survives.

Epithets: "the match of men" (ἀντιάνειραι, 3.189).

Refs: 3.189, 6.186. Books 3, 6.

See also: Priam, Bellerophon.

Amphidamas Ἀμφιδάμαςmortal

Say: am-FID-uh-mas

Two men. One is of Cythera, a link in the descent of the boar's-tusk helmet Odysseus wears on the night raid: Autolycus stole it, gave it to Amphidamas at Scandeia, who gave it to Molus, whose son Meriones lends it on. The other is of Opoeis, whose son Patroclus killed as a boy in a quarrel over dice — "a child's act; I did not mean it" — the killing that brought him to Peleus' house.

Kin: the Cytheran is a guest-friend of Molus; the Opoeisian is father of the boy Patroclus killed.

Refs: 10.268, 10.269, 23.87. Books 10, 23.

See also: Meriones, Molus, Patroclus, Opoeis.

Amphimachus Ἀμφίμαχοςmortal

Say: am-FIM-uh-kus

Two men, both leaders in the Catalogue. The Epeian is a son of Cteatus, of Actor's line, one of four captains from Elis; Hector spears him in the chest as he comes into the fighting at the ships and is reaching to strip his helmet when Ajax drives him back, and the lesser Ajax kills Imbrius in wrath for him. The other is a Carian, son of Nomion and brother of Nastes, one of the pair who "went to war decked in gold, like a girl."

Kin: the Epeian is Cteatus' son, of Actor's line; the Carian is Nomion's son and Nastes' brother.

Killed by: Hector (13.185–187).

Refs: 2.620, 2.870, 2.871, 13.185, 13.189, 13.195, 13.203. Books 2, 13.

See also: Nastes, Actor, Cteatus, Hector, Ajax.

Anchises Ἀγχίσηςmortal

Say: an-KY-seez

Aeneas' father by Aphrodite, who "conceived him to Anchises herding cattle" on the spurs of Ida. He does not fight: he belongs to the genealogy Aeneas recites to Achilles at 20.208–241, the Dardanian line running Dardanus — Erichthonius — Tros — Assaracus — Capys — Anchises — Aeneas, the branch that survives when Priam's does not. A different Anchises, of Sicyon, is the father of the Echepolus who bribed Agamemnon with a mare to be let off the war (23.296).

Kin: son of Capys; father of Aeneas by Aphrodite; father-in-law of Alcathous.

Refs: 2.819, 5.247, 5.313, 13.428, 17.491, 20.208, 20.240, 23.296.

Books 2, 5, 12–13, 17, 20, 23.

See also: Aeneas, Aphrodite, Dardanus, Capys, Alcathous, Echepolus.

Andromache Ἀνδρομάχηmortal

Say: an-DROM-uh-kee

Hector's wife, daughter of Eëtion of Thebe-under-Placus, whose father and seven brothers Achilles killed in the sack of that city — so that, as she tells Hector, he is now father and mother and brother to her as well as husband. She meets him at the Scaean Gates in Book 6 with their son in her arms and begs him to fight from the wall; he refuses, and the scene ends with the child frightened by his own father's helmet. She is at her loom, ordering a bath for him, when the wailing tells her he is dead (22.437–515), and she leads the lament over his body in the poem's last scene.

Epithets: "white-armed" (λευκώλενος, 6.371).

Kin: daughter of Eëtion; wife of Hector; mother of Astyanax.

Refs: 6.371, 6.377, 6.395, 6.405, 8.187, 17.208, 24.723. Books 6, 8, 17, 24.

See also: Hector, Astyanax, Eëtion, Thebe, Achilles.

Antenor Ἀντήνωρmortal

Say: an-TEE-nor

The wisest of the Trojan elders and the one who says plainly what the war is about. He had hosted Menelaus and Odysseus on the embassy that first asked for Helen back, and he identifies them for Priam from the wall; in Book 7 he tells the assembly to give Helen and her goods to the Atreidae, since "it is with sworn oaths made false that we fight," and Paris refuses. Many of his sons die in the poem — Archelochus, Acamas, Iphidamas, Coon, Polybus — and Agenor is the last to face Achilles.

Kin: husband of Theano, Athena's priestess in Troy; father of Archelochus, Acamas, Agenor, Iphidamas, Coon, Polybus, and others.

Hector Ἕκτωρmortal

Priam's eldest son, Troy's champion, and the man on whom the city rests — "for Hector alone was the saving of Ilion" (6.403). He is the poem's second protagonist: he duels Ajax to a standstill in Book 7, breaks the Achaean gate with a stone in Book 12, fires the ships in Book 15, and strips Achilles' armor from Patroclus' body, the act that brings Achilles back and destroys him. His domestic scene with Andromache and the infant Astyanax at the Scaean Gates (6.390–496) is set against his own certainty that Ilion will fall; he twice rejects Polydamas' counsel to withdraw, the second time fatally. In Book 22 he waits alone outside the wall, runs three times around the city, is tricked by Athena in Deiphobus' shape, and takes Achilles' spear through the gullet. Achilles drags his body for twelve days until Priam ransoms it, and the poem closes on his burial.

An island off Troy, always "rugged," and in the poem a marker of the sea-road rather than a place with people. Poseidon's undersea cave lies "midway between Tenedos and rugged Imbros"; Iris dives into the sea "midway between Samos and rugged Imbros" on her way to Thetis; Hera and Sleep pass over it and Lemnos on the way to Ida. Eëtion of Imbros is the guest-friend who ransomed Lycaon out of Lemnos, and Hecuba names it in her lament as one of the places across the sea Achilles sold her sons into.

Refs: 13.33, 14.281, 21.43, 24.78, 24.753. Books 13–14, 21, 24.

See also: Lemnos, Samos, Tenedos, Poseidon, Eëtion, Lycaon.

Iphiclus Ἴφικλοςmortal

Iphidamas Ἰφιδάμαςmortal

Say: if-ID-uh-mas

Antenor's son, "a good man and a big one," and the answer to the Muse-question of who first came against Agamemnon in his aristeia. Reared in Thrace by his mother's father Cisses, who tried to keep him there by giving him his daughter, he left the bridal chamber "chasing the report of the Achaeans" with twelve ships, beached them at Percote, and walked the rest of the way to Ilion. His thrust bends on Agamemnon's war-belt "like lead"; Agamemnon wrenches the spear from his grip and cuts his neck, and he "slept a sleep of bronze," far from the wife he had bought with a hundred cattle and a promise of a thousand goats and sheep. His brother Coon wounds Agamemnon in the forearm trying to drag the body clear and is beheaded over it.

Kin: son of Antenor and Theano; grandson of Cisses; brother of Coon.

Killed by: Agamemnon (11.238–241).

Refs: 11.221, 11.234, 11.257, 11.261. Book 11.

See also: Antenor, Coon, Agamemnon, Theano, Cisses, Percote.

Iris Ἶριςgod

Say: EYE-ris

The gods' runner, and the poem's most reliable instrument: she carries Zeus's word and no one else's business is ever her own. She rouses the Trojan assembly in Priam's likeness at 2.786, leads the wounded Aphrodite out of the fighting and up to Olympus (5.353–369), turns Hera and Athena back from the field with Zeus's threat (8.398–425), and brings Achilles unarmed to the ditch on Hera's secret errand (18.166–202). She has one line of her own judgment — telling Poseidon that the hearts of the great can turn (15.203) — and Poseidon calls it the mark of a messenger who knows what is fitting. In Book 23 she carries Achilles' prayer to the winds; in Book 24 she goes down through the sea to Thetis and then to Priam, and her speech to him repeats Zeus's word almost exactly.

Epithets: "wind-footed swift Iris" (ποδήνεμος ὠκέα Ἶρις, 2.786); "swift-footed Iris" (πόδας ὠκέα Ἶρις, 2.790); "of the golden wings" (χρυσόπτερος, 8.398); "storm-footed" (ἀελλόπος, 8.409).

Jason Ἰήσωνmortal

Laodice Λαοδίκηmortal

Laomedon Λαομέδωνmortal

Say: lay-OM-uh-don

Priam's father, dead before the poem, and its standing example of a man who cheats gods. Poseidon tells the story twice: he and Apollo served him a whole year at a stated wage — Poseidon building the city wall, Apollo herding cattle on Ida — and at the term he robbed them by force and threatened to bind them and sell them overseas and cut off their ears (21.441–457, cf. 7.452–453). He also refused Heracles the horses promised for a service, and Heracles sacked Ilion for it (5.640–651). His horses remain the standard of excellence: Nestor tells Antilochus no man will pass him, not driving Arion nor "the horses of Laomedon, best of the breed reared here" (23.348).

Kin: son of Ilus; father of Priam, Tithonus, Lampus, Clytius, Hicetaon and Bucolion.

Refs: 3.250, 5.269, 5.640, 5.649, 7.453, 20.236, 21.444, 21.452, 23.348.

Books 3, 5–7, 15, 20–21, 23.

See also: Priam, Poseidon, Apollo, Heracles, Ilus, Lampus, Tros.

Laothoe Λαοθόηmortal

Say: lay-OTH-oh-ee

One of Priam's wives, daughter of Altes lord of the Lelegians, and mother of Lycaon and Polydorus — the two sons Achilles kills within a book of each other. Lycaon names her as he supplicates: "to a short life my mother bore me — Laothoe, daughter of the old man Altes" (21.84–89). Priam, watching from the wall, counts the same two as missing and says that if they are dead it is grief to his heart and their mother's, "a queen among women" (22.46–53).

Kin: daughter of Altes; wife of Priam; mother of Lycaon and Polydorus.

Refs: 21.85, 22.48. Books 21–22.

See also: Altes, Priam, Lycaon, Polydorus, Achilles.

Lemnos Λῆμνοςplace

Say: LEM-nos

The island Hephaestus fell on when Zeus flung him from the threshold of heaven, where the Sintian men took him up (1.593); Philoctetes lies there abandoned with his snake-bite while his ships fight at Troy (2.722); and it is "the city of godlike Thoas" that Hera passes through on her way to find Sleep (14.230). It is also the poem's slave-market: Achilles ships Lycaon there and sells him, and the son of Jason pays the price (21.40–41) — a sale Lycaon and Achilles both throw back at each other in Book 21, and one Priam expects for his other sons in Book 24.

Leonteus Λεοντεύςmortal

Say: lee-ON-tyoos

One of the two Lapith captains, always paired with Polypoetes: they lead forty ships in the Catalogue and stand before the gate in Book 12 "like high-crowned oaks in the mountains that abide the wind and rain through all their days," refusing to be driven inside the wall. There he spears Hippomachus at the war-belt and then goes at the rest with the sword. In Book 23 he throws the lump of pig-iron second and loses to Polypoetes.

Epithets: "scion of Ares" (ὄζος Ἄρηος, 2.745).

Kin: son of Coronus, Caeneus' son.

Lesbos Λέσβοςplace

Say: LEZ-bos

One of Achilles' conquests before the poem opens: the seven women skilled in blameless work whom Agamemnon offers in the embassy are the ones Achilles himself chose "when he himself took well-built Lesbos" (9.129–130 = 9.271–272), and the woman sleeping beside him at the end of Book 9 is Diomede, Phorbas's daughter, brought from there. Achilles names it again in Book 24 as the seaward bound of Priam's old prosperity, "the seat of Macar."

Leto Λητώgod

Say: LEE-toh

Mother of Apollo and Artemis by Zeus, named in the poem's ninth line as the parent of the god who sends the plague. She takes the Trojan side: she heals Aeneas with Artemis in Apollo's shrine on Pergamos (5.447), and in the theomachy she is matched against Hermes — who declines to fight her, telling her to boast among the gods that she overcame him, and leaves her to gather up her daughter's scattered bow and arrows (21.497–504). Zeus names her in his catalogue of loves at 14.327, and it is for matching herself against Leto that Niobe's twelve children are killed (24.607). Achilles' horse Xanthus calls Apollo "the best of the gods, whom lovely-haired Leto bore" (19.413).

Epithets: "lovely-haired" (ἠΰκομος, 1.36); "fair-cheeked" (καλλιπάρῃος, 24.607).

Kin: by Zeus, mother of Apollo and Artemis.

Leïtus Λήϊτοςmortal

Locrians Λοκροίpeople

Say: LOK-ree-unz

The lesser Ajax's forty ships, from Cynus, Opoeis and the towns around the Boagrius, "who live across from holy Euboea." Book 13 makes them the poem's only distinct light infantry: they carry no bronze helmets, no shields and no ash spears, only bows and slings of twisted wool, so they will not stand in the close fight — they follow behind the armored line and shoot unseen, and it is their arrows that break the Trojan formations and rout them (13.712–722).

Lycaon Λυκάωνmortal

Say: ly-KAY-on

Two men. The chief one is Priam's son by Laothoe, whose supplication in Book 21 is the poem's most sustained refusal. Achilles had taken him once by night in his father's orchard cutting fig-shoots for chariot-rails, shipped him to Lemnos and sold him; ransomed by Eëtion of Imbros, he had been home eleven days when Achilles meets him again in the river. He grasps the knees with one hand and the spear with the other and pleads guest-right and the grain of Demeter; Achilles answers that Patroclus died and was a better man, and that death and strong fate stand over himself too, then cuts through his collarbone and slings him into the river to the fish. His corslet is what Paris arms in for the duel (3.333). The second Lycaon is Pandarus's father, of Zeleia, whose eleven new-built chariots stand under cloths at home while Pandarus fights on foot (5.193–197); "Lycaon's shining son" throughout Books 4 and 5 means Pandarus, not Priam's son.

Kin: Priam's son is son of Priam and Laothoe, brother of Polydorus; the other is father of Pandarus.

Killed by: Achilles (21.114–119).

Refs: 2.826, 3.333, 4.89, 5.95, 5.193, 20.81, 21.35, 21.71, 21.127, 22.46,

23.746. Books 2–5, 20–23.

See also: Pandarus, Priam, Laothoe, Polydorus, Achilles, Eëtion, Lemnos.

Lycia Λυκίαplace

Say: LISH-uh or LIS-ee-uh

The homeland of Sarpedon and Glaucus, the farthest of Troy's allies — "from far Lycia, from the eddying Xanthus" (2.877). Sarpedon names it as what he has left: his wife, his little son, and great wealth (5.480–481). It is also where Bellerophon was sent with the folded tablet, and where the king gave him his daughter and the best of the land (6.168–195); and Glaucus's account of the estate on the banks of Xanthus is the ground of Sarpedon's argument about why kings must fight in front (12.310–321). Sleep and Death carry Sarpedon's body back to "the rich country of wide Lycia" for burial (16.683).

Lycians Λύκιοιpeople

Say: LISH-unz

Troy's strongest allies, led by Sarpedon and Glaucus, and the only contingent the poem treats as a rival to the Trojans themselves. Hector's rallying-cry names them in their own clause — "Trojans and Lycians and Dardanians who fight at close quarters" (8.173) — and it is they who first break through the Achaean wall in Book 12. Sarpedon shames them into the assault ("Lycians, why do you slacken your rushing courage so?", 12.409) and again as he dies over his own body; when he is killed they panic and abandon it, and Glaucus rounds on Hector for it in Book 17, threatening that no Lycian will fight for Troy again.

Lycurgus Λυκοῦργοςmortal

Say: ly-KUR-gus

Two men, both from stories told inside the poem. Diomedes cites the first as the warning against fighting gods: Dryas' son, who chased the nurses of raving Dionysus down Nysa beating them with an ox-goad, until Dionysus dived under the sea and Thetis took him in terror; the gods were angered, Zeus struck him blind, and he did not live long, "hated by all the immortal gods" (6.130–140). The second is the Arcadian of Nestor's Book 7 reminiscence, who killed Areithous the Club-man by craft in a narrow lane where the iron club was useless, and in old age passed the stripped armor to his aide Ereuthalion.

Kin: the first is son of Dryas.

Kills: the Arcadian kills Areithous (7.142–145).

Lyrnessus Λυρνησσόςplace

The town Achilles sacked, taking Briseis out of it "with great toil" and killing Mynes and Epistrophus, the sons of Euenus (2.690–693). Meleager Μελέαγροςmortal

Say: mel-ee-AY-jur

Phoenix's exemplum in the embassy, and the poem's model for Achilles' own case. Oeneus' son, dead before the Catalogue is spoken (2.642), he killed the boar Artemis sent against Calydon and then, in anger at his mother Althaea's curses, withdrew from the fighting and lay beside his wife Cleopatra while the Curetes stormed the walls. Elders, priests, his father, his sisters and his mother all begged him and he refused the more; only when the towers were under bombardment and the city burning did his wife's plea move him, and he went out — and got none of the gifts he had been offered. Phoenix's point is the moral: come out while the gifts are still on the table.

Kin: son of Oeneus and Althaea; husband of Cleopatra.

Refs: 2.642, 9.543, 9.550, 9.553, 9.565, 9.590. Books 2, 9.

See also: Phoenix, Oeneus, Althaea, Cleopatra, Calydon, Curetes, Artemis.

Menelaus Μενέλαοςmortal

Say: men-uh-LAY-us

Agamemnon's brother and Helen's husband, the man the war is nominally for, and the one Achaean whose motive is personal. He volunteers unbidden for the muster (2.408), takes the duel with Paris in Book 3 and all but wins it — the spear through the shield, the sword shattering on the helmet-ridge, the chin-strap strangling Paris until Aphrodite breaks it and carries the man off — and is then shot by Pandarus in the broken truce, the arrow steered by Athena into the buckles of his war-belt (4.127–140). The poem's word for him is restraint verging on softness: Agamemnon overrules his mercy to the suppliant Adrestus and does the killing himself (6.51–65), the army fears for him when he offers to fight Hector (7.104–109). His full measure comes in Book 17, where he stands over Patroclus's body, kills Euphorbus for it, and, when at last he must leave it to the Ajaxes, goes off to find Antilochus so someone can carry the news to Achilles. In Book 23 he charges Antilochus with fouling him in the chariot-race and then, satisfied by an apology, gives the mare back.

Meriones Μηριόνηςmortal

Say: muh-RY-oh-neez

Idomeneus's aide and second in command of the Cretans, and one of the poem's most consistently competent fighters. Their exchange outside the huts in Book 13 — he has come back for a spear, having shattered his on Deiphobus's shield, and Idomeneus offers him twenty Trojan spears taken from the men he has killed — is the poem's fullest picture of two veterans talking shop. He wounds Deiphobus in the arm, shoots Harpalion through the buttock into the bladder as he retreats, kills the priest Laogonus in Book 16 and trades taunts with Aeneas over a missed cast. He is Odysseus's armorer for the night raid, lending the boar's-tusk helmet whose four-generation provenance the poem tracks (10.260–271). In Book 23 he drives the slowest horses and finishes fourth, then wins the archery: Teucer, who did not vow to Apollo, cuts only the cord; Meriones snatches the bow, vows the hecatomb, and shoots the dove through under the wing high under the clouds.

Myrmidons Μυρμιδόνεςpeople

Say: MUR-mih-donz

Achilles' own contingent, fifty ships from Phthia and Hellas; the Catalogue notes that the same men "were called Myrmidons, and Hellenes, and Achaeans" (2.684), which makes the last a regional name here and not the whole host. They sit out the war with their leader — Agamemnon's insult is "go home and be king over your Myrmidons" (1.180) — until Book 16, when Achilles arms them for Patroclus. Their two similes there are the poem's harshest: wolves that have torn a stag, their muzzles red, going down to lap black water; and roadside wasps that boys have been provoking, who come out all at once. They mourn Patroclus, drive their chariots three times around the body, and build Achilles' hut in the description that opens the last scene of Book 24.

Nestor Νέστωρmortal

Say: NES-tor

The aged king of Pylos, third-generation ruler who has outlived two generations of mortal men, and the counselor whose plans set the poem's machinery moving. He tries and fails to compose the quarrel in Book 1; he proposes the wall and ditch (7.325–343); he frames the embassy to Achilles and picks its members (9.162–172); he devises the night raid; and it is his suggestion at 11.794–803 — send Patroclus out in Achilles' armor — that Patroclus carries back and that kills him. His authority is verbal, not martial: he is rescued out of the fighting in Book 8, and at the funeral games Achilles gives him a prize outright because he will not compete, "the hard weight of age is on you now."

Odysseus Ὀδυσσεύςmortal

The Achaeans' tactician, and the man the poem sends whenever a thing must be managed rather than won by force. He turns the army back from the ships in Book 2 and beats Thersites out of the assembly; he escorts Chryseis home; he speaks first for the embassy in Book 9 and Nestor charges him "above all" to win Achilles over; he goes out with Diomedes on the night raid and brings back Rhesus' horses; and in Book 19 he holds Achilles to the army's need to eat before fighting. Paris Πάριςmortal

Priam's son, whose taking of Helen is the cause of the war and who is the least admired man in the poem. Patroclus Πάτροκλοςmortal

Say: puh-TROH-klus

Achilles' companion, and the hinge of the poem. Peleus Πηλεύςmortal

Say: PEE-lee-us

Achilles' father, king of the Myrmidons, and in the great majority of its 146 occurrences a patronymic: "Peleus' son" is Achilles, and the name's work in the poem is to keep saying whose son he is, beginning with the first line. Zephyrus Ζέφυροςgod

Say: ZEF-ih-rus

The west wind, named in Book 23 only, where Iris finds all the winds feasting indoors at "the house of gusting Zephyrus" and delivers Achilles' prayer: he and Boreas rise with an unearthly roar, drive the clouds before them, and fall on the pyre so that Patroclus burns all night. Elsewhere in the poem the same wind is called "the West Wind" without the name — as the sire of Xanthus and Balius by the harpy Podarge (16.150), and in Xanthus' own boast that the two of them could run with its breath (19.415).

Kin: father of Xanthus and Balius by Podarge (named there as "the West Wind").

Refs: 23.195, 23.200, 23.208. Book 23.

See also: Boreas, Iris, Achilles, Patroclus, Xanthus (horse), Podarge.

Zeus Ζεύςgod

Say: ZOOS

The presiding god of the poem and the agent of its plot. Its fifth line announces that through all the deaths "the will of Zeus moved toward fulfillment," and the mechanism is set in Book 1: Thetis clasps his knees, he bows his head — the pledge that cannot be taken back — and promises to give the Trojans power until the Achaeans honor Achilles. He enforces it by banning the gods from the field on pain of Tartarus, by his golden scales, and by a long plan-speech at 15.56–77 that lays out Books 16 through 22 in advance. He is not omnipotent in it so much as bound by it: he cannot save Sarpedon, his own dearest son, without unmaking the order the other gods live by, and instead rains drops of blood on the earth; he weighs Hector's fate and lets it sink; Hera deceives him into sleep and he wakes furious but does not undo it. In Book 24 he ends the poem by sending Thetis to Achilles and Iris to Priam, ransom for a body.

Epithets: "aegis-bearing" (αἰγίοχος, 1.202); "Zeus the cloud-gatherer" (νεφεληγερέτα, 1.511); "Zeus the counselor / of the counsels" (μητίετα, 1.175); "who thunders on high" (ὑψιβρεμέτης, 1.354); "who delights in thunder" (τερπικέραυνος, 1.419); "lord of lightning" (ἀστεροπητής, 1.580); "wide-seeing" (εὐρύοπα, 1.498); "high-throned" (ὑψίζυγος, 4.166); "father of men and gods" (πατὴρ ἀνδρῶν τε θεῶν τε, 1.544); "the dispenser of war" (ταμίης πολέμοιο, 4.84); "the loud-thunderer, Hera's lord" (ἐρίγδουπος πόσις Ἥρης, 7.411); "Zeus of All Voices" (Πανομφαῖος, 8.250); "Zeus of Guest-right" (Ζεὺς ξείνιος, 13.625).

Also called: "son of Cronos" (Κρονίδης / Κρονίων, 1.398) — the patronymic that resolves to Zeus throughout; "Cronos" alone is the Titan father.

Kin: son of Cronos and Rhea; brother of Poseidon, Hades and Hera, who is also his wife; the three brothers divide sky, sea and underworld by lot (15.187–193). Father of Athena, of Apollo and Artemis by Leto, of Ares and Hebe by Hera, of Aphrodite by Dione, of Hermes, of Heracles by Alcmene, and of Sarpedon.

Refs: 1.5, 1.503, 1.528, 8.5, 15.187, 16.431, 22.209, 24.103 …and throughout.
