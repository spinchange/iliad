Book XVII

The fight over the body of Patroclus: Hector puts on the armor of Achilles, the immortal horses weep, and Ajax prays not for rescue but for light.

Translator’s note on Book 17

Book 17 is the Iliad's longest tug-of-war: one dead man at the center, and the whole day's war reorganized around the question of who will hold him. The poem frames it in similes of guardianship and labor rather than glory — Menelaus over the body "as a mother- cow, lowing, stands over her first calf" (note 1); the corpse dragged this way and that "as men stretch a great bull's hide soaked in fat," the tanning-yard come to the battlefield (note 21); the rescue at last like mules hauling a ship-timber down a rocky path, the two Ajaxes behind it like a wooded ridge damming a flood (note 37). Against these stand the book's two heights of theology. Zeus watches Hector put on the stripped armor of Achilles and shakes his head: the gear "not taken by right," the power granted as ποινή — blood-price paid in glory to a man who will never come home from the battle, "nor will Andromache take from you the famous armor of Peleus' son" (notes [12], [13]). And Zeus watches the immortal horses stand like a grave-stone, manes in the dust, weeping for their charioteer, and asks the poem's deepest question of his own gift: why did we give you to a mortal, "you who are ageless and deathless? Was it so that you too might have sorrows among unhappy men? For there is nothing anywhere more pitiful than man, of all things that breathe and creep upon the earth" (notes [25], [26]).

The book's people are its second glory: Euphorbus dead with his Graces' hair in the blood and the olive-sapling simile that Pythagoras's biographers never stopped quoting (note 4); Glaucus proposing to trade corpse for corpse, Patroclus for Sarpedon — bodies as the war's currency (note 10); the anonymous soldiers' paired vows (note 23); Antilochus struck speechless by the news he must carry (note 35); and Ajax, in the god-sent mist, making the request Longinus judged the most sublime in literature — not life, but light: "and in the light — kill us, since that is now your pleasure" (note 33).

1Nor did it escape Atreus' son, Menelaus dear to Ares,

2that Patroclus had been broken by Trojans in the combat.

3He came through the front-fighters helmed in blazing bronze

4and stood over the body — as a mother-cow, lowing,

5stands over her first calf, who never knew birth before:1

6so tawny-haired Menelaus stood over Patroclus.

7He held his spear before him, and his shield, balanced every way,

8hot to kill whatever man might come against him.

9Nor was Panthous's son of the good ash spear careless

10of blameless Patroclus fallen: he came and stood

11close by, and spoke to Menelaus dear to Ares:

12"Son of Atreus — Zeus-nurtured Menelaus, marshal of the people —

13give way: leave the body; let the bloody spoils be.

14For no man of the Trojans or their famed allies

15hit Patroclus with the spear in the strong encounter before me:2

16so let me take my good fame among the Trojans,

17or I will strike you and take your honey-sweet life."

18Then, deeply angered, tawny-haired Menelaus answered him:

19"Father Zeus — how ugly a thing is arrogant boasting!

20Not the leopard's fury is so great, nor the lion's,

21nor the murderous wild boar's — whose heart in his chest

22is the biggest of all, glorying in his strength —

23as the pride of the sons of Panthous with their good ash spears.

24Yet the strength of Hyperenor, breaker of horses, had no joy

25of his youth, when he mocked me and stood against me

26and called me the sorriest fighter of the Danaans:

27I say his own feet never carried him home

28to delight his dear wife and his devoted parents.

29Just so, I promise you, I will unstring your strength, if you stand

30against me. Go back into the crowd — that is my counsel —

31and do not stand against me, before some evil

32takes you. When the thing is done, even the fool learns."

33So he spoke, and did not persuade him; and he answered:

34"Now, Zeus-nurtured Menelaus, now you will pay in full3

35for my brother, whom you killed — and boast of it in speeches —

36whose wife you widowed in the depth of her new bridal chamber,

37who brought his parents cursed mourning and grief.

38I could be some stay of weeping for those unhappy two

39if I brought back your head and your armor

40and laid them in the hands of Panthous and brilliant Phrontis.

41But not for long shall the labor go untried between us,

42and unfought — whether it end in courage or in flight."

43So speaking, he stabbed at the shield, balanced every way;

44but the bronze did not break through: the point bent back

45in the strong shield. And second, with the bronze,

46rose Atreus' son Menelaus, with a prayer to Zeus the father:

47as Euphorbus drew back, he stabbed the base of his throat,

48and leaned into the thrust, trusting his heavy hand;

49and clean through the soft neck the point ran out.

50He fell with a thud, and his armor clattered upon him.

51His hair was soaked with blood — hair like the Graces',4

52and the braids pinched tight in gold and silver.

53As a man rears a flourishing olive sapling

54in a lonely place, where water wells up in plenty —

55a fair shoot, and a thriving; and the breathing of every

56wind rocks it, and it breaks thick with white blossom;

57but suddenly a wind comes with a great squall

58and wrenches it out of its trench and lays it flat on the earth:

59such was Panthous's son of the good ash spear, Euphorbus,

60when Atreus' son Menelaus killed him, and set to stripping his armor.

61And as when some mountain-bred lion, sure of his strength,5

62snatches from a grazing herd the finest heifer:

63her neck he breaks first, seizing it in his strong teeth,

64and then he gulps down the blood and all the inward parts,

65rending her; and around him the dogs and the herdsmen

66raise clamor upon clamor — from a distance: they have no will

67to go against him, for green fear has hold of them:

68so the heart in the chest of not one of them dared

69to go against glorious Menelaus.

70And now Atreus' son would easily have carried off the famed armor

71of Panthous's son, had not Phoebus Apollo grudged it,

72and roused Hector against him, the equal of swift Ares,

73in the likeness of a man, Mentes, leader of the Cicones;

74and speaking winged words he addressed him:

75"Hector — you are running now, chasing what cannot be caught:6

76the horses of the war-minded son of Aeacus. They are hard

77for mortal men to break or to drive,

78for any man but Achilles, whom an immortal mother bore.

79And meanwhile Menelaus, warlike son of Atreus,

80standing over Patroclus, has killed the best of the Trojans —

81Panthous's son Euphorbus — and stopped his rushing courage."

82So speaking, the god went back into the toil of men;

83and terrible grief packed Hector's darkened heart.

84He peered along the ranks, and knew at once

85the one man stripping the famed armor, the other lying

86on the ground, the blood running from the open wound.

87He came through the front-fighters helmed in blazing bronze,

88screaming sharp, like the unquenchable flame

89of Hephaestus. And his sharp cry did not escape Atreus' son;

90troubled, he spoke to his own great-hearted spirit:

91"Ah me — if I abandon the fine armor,

92and Patroclus, who lies here for the sake of my honor,

93I fear any Danaan who sees it will call it my disgrace.

94But if, in shame, I fight Hector and the Trojans

95alone — I fear the many will surround me, one man:

96Hector of the flashing helmet is leading all of them here.

97But why does my own heart argue these things with me?

98When a man wills, against the god's face, to fight a man

99whom a god honors — a great woe rolls down on him quickly.

100So no Danaan who sees me give way before Hector

101will call it my disgrace: he fights out of heaven.

102But if I could only hear Ajax of the great war-cry —

103the two of us together might go back and remember our war-craft,

104even against the god's face, and haul the body clear

105for Achilles, Peleus' son. Of evils, that would be best."

106While he was turning this over in mind and heart,

107the Trojan battle-lines came on; and Hector led them.

108And he gave ground backward, and left the body,

109turning again and again — like a bearded lion7

110that dogs and men drive off from the fold

111with spears and shouting: the brave heart in his chest

112goes stiff with the cold, and unwilling he leaves the yard:

113so tawny-haired Menelaus went from Patroclus.

114He turned and stood, when he reached the band of his companions,

115peering everywhere for great Ajax, Telamon's son;

116and he saw him quickly, on the far left of the whole battle,

117heartening his companions and driving them to fight,

118for Phoebus Apollo had thrown unearthly panic among them.

119He set off at a run, and stood by him at once, and spoke:

120"Ajax — this way, old friend! Let us hurry for dead Patroclus,

121and see if we can bring the body at least to Achilles —

122naked: for Hector of the flashing helmet has the armor."

123So he spoke, and stirred the heart in war-minded Ajax,

124and he came through the front-fighters, and with him tawny-haired Menelaus.

125Hector, when he had stripped Patroclus of the famed armor,

126was dragging him, to shear the head from the shoulders with the sharp bronze8

127and haul the body off and give it to the dogs of Troy.

128But Ajax came up close, carrying his shield like a tower,

129and Hector drew back into the band of his companions

130and sprang into his chariot; and he gave the fine armor

131to Trojans to carry to the city, to be his own great glory.

132And Ajax covered Menoetius's son with his broad shield

133and stood — as a lion stands over his young,9

134a lion leading his cubs, when hunting men come on him

135in the timber: he glories in his strength,

136and draws the whole scowl of his brows down over his eyes:

137so Ajax bestrode the hero Patroclus.

138And on the other side Atreus' son, Menelaus dear to Ares,

139stood fast, nursing great grief in his chest.

140Then Glaucus, Hippolochus's son, leader of the Lycian men,

141glaring darkly at Hector, rebuked him with a hard word:

142"Hector — best of us all to look at: in battle you fall far short.10

143Fine fame indeed is yours — a runaway's.

144Consider now how you will save your town and citadel

145alone — you with the people born in Ilion;

146for no Lycian, at least, will go out to fight the Danaans

147for your city, since there was no thanks, it seems,

148for battling the enemy without respite, forever.

149How would you save a lesser man in the press of war,

150hard-hearted one, when Sarpedon — your guest-friend and companion —

151you abandoned to the Argives, their prey and their prize:

152a man who served you greatly, your city and yourself,

153while he lived; and now you have not dared to keep the dogs from him.

154So now, if any Lycian man will obey me,

155we go home — and let sheer destruction show over Troy.

156For if the Trojans now had daring fury in them,

157unshaking — the kind that comes into men who for their fatherland

158take on the toil and the grinding of war against their enemies —

159we would quickly haul Patroclus inside Ilion.

160And if that man came to the great city of lord Priam,

161dead — if we hauled him out of the fighting —

162the Argives would quickly ransom back the fine armor

163of Sarpedon, and we would bring the man himself into Ilion:

164for the aide of such a man lies killed — far the best

165of the Argives by the ships, he and his close-fighting aides.

166But you had not the courage to stand against great-hearted

167Ajax, eye to eye in the enemies' clamor,

168and fight him straight — since he is a better man than you."

169Glaring at him darkly, Hector of the flashing helmet answered:

170"Glaucus — why does a man like you talk so overweening?11

171Well now! And I always said you were first in judgment

172of all who live in Lycia of the deep soil;

173but now I scorn your wits outright — such a thing to say:

174you say I did not stand against gigantic Ajax.

175It is not battle I shudder at, nor the thunder of horses;

176but always the mind of Zeus who bears the aegis is stronger:

177he sends even the brave man running, and strips away victory

178easily — and then again himself drives a man to fight.

179Come stand beside me, old friend — watch my work:

180see whether all day long I prove the coward you call me,

181or whether I stop many a Danaan too, however hot

182his courage, from his defense of dead Patroclus."

183So speaking, he called to the Trojans with a great shout:

184"Trojans and Lycians and Dardanians who fight at close quarters —

185be men, my friends; remember your rushing courage,

186while I put on the armor of blameless Achilles,

187the fine armor I stripped from the strength of Patroclus when I killed him."

188So speaking, Hector of the flashing helmet went off

189out of the grim war; and running he overtook his companions,

190quickly, on swift feet, not yet far away —

191the men carrying toward the city the famed armor of Peleus' son.

192And standing apart from the battle of many tears, he changed his armor:12

193his own he gave to the war-loving Trojans to carry

194to holy Ilion; and he put on the immortal armor

195of Achilles, Peleus' son, which the heavenly gods

196had given his dear father; and the father, grown old, passed it

197to his son — but the son did not grow old in his father's armor.

198And when Zeus the cloud-gatherer saw him from afar

199arming himself in the gear of the godlike son of Peleus,

200he shook his head, and spoke to his own spirit:

201"Ah, poor man — death is nowhere in your thoughts,13

202and it stands close beside you. You are putting on the immortal

203armor of the best of men — a man the others shudder at.

204His companion you have killed, who was gentle and strong,

205and stripped the armor from his head and shoulders

206not by right. Yet for now I will hand you great power —

207as recompense for this: that never home out of the battle

208will you come, nor Andromache take from you the famous armor of Peleus' son."

209He spoke, and the son of Cronos bowed his head with his dark brows;

210and he fitted the armor to Hector's body, and Ares entered him,

211the terrible war-god, and his limbs were filled inside

212with courage and strength. He went out to the famed allies

213with a great shout, and showed himself before them all,

214blazing in the armor of the great-hearted son of Peleus.

215And going among them he stirred each man with his words —

216Mesthles and Glaucus and Medon and Thersilochus,

217Asteropaeus and Deisenor and Hippothous,

218Phorcys and Chromius and Ennomus the bird-reader;

219urging these on, he spoke winged words:

220"Hear me, you numberless tribes of allies who dwell around us:14

221it was not for a crowd's sake — not because I lacked numbers —

222that I gathered you here, each man from his own city,

223but so that you would guard, with a will, the Trojans' wives

224and little children from the war-loving Achaeans.

225With that in mind I wear down my own people

226for gifts and provisions, to feed the courage in each of you.

227So now — let every man turn straight ahead, and die

228or live: that is the trysting-talk of war.

229And whoever hauls Patroclus, dead though he is,

230in among the horse-taming Trojans, and Ajax yields to him —

231half the spoils I will share out to that man, and half I keep

232myself; and his glory shall be as great as mine."

233So he spoke; and they marched straight down on the Danaans, heavy,

234spears held high; and their hearts had great hope

235of hauling the body from under Ajax, Telamon's son —

236fools: over that body he was to rob many of life.15

237And then Ajax spoke to Menelaus, good at the war-cry:

238"Old friend — Zeus-nurtured Menelaus — I no longer count

239on the two of us, even ourselves, coming home from this war.

240It is not so much for the corpse of Patroclus I fear —

241he will glut the dogs and birds of Troy soon enough —

242as for my own head, that something may happen to it,

243and yours: for Hector, that cloud of war, has covered

244everything, and for us sheer destruction shows clear.

245Come — call the best of the Danaans, if any may hear."

246So he spoke, and Menelaus, good at the war-cry, did not disobey,

247and cried a piercing cry, to carry to the Danaans:

248"Friends — leaders and lords of the Argives —

249you who beside the two Atreus' sons, Agamemnon and Menelaus,

250drink at the public cost, and each give orders

251to your people — from Zeus come honor and glory.

252It is hard for me to pick out each one

253of the leaders — so great is the strife of war ablaze —

254so let each man come uncalled, and feel the outrage in his heart

255that Patroclus should be sport for the dogs of Troy."

256So he spoke; and the swift Ajax, Oïleus' son, heard him sharp,

257and came first to meet him, running through the combat,

258and after him Idomeneus, and Idomeneus's aide

259Meriones, the equal of Enyalius, slayer of men.

260But of the rest — who could speak their names out of his own mind,

261all who came up behind and woke the Achaean battle?15

262And the Trojans struck first, massed; Hector led them.

263As when at the mouth of a sky-fed river

264the great swell bellows against the current, and around it

265the jutting shores boom, as the salt sea belches beyond them:

266with such a roar came the Trojans on. But the Achaeans

267stood fast around Menoetius's son, one heart in all of them,

268fenced with their bronze-faced shields. And over their bright

269helmets the son of Cronos poured thick mist,16

270since never in the time before had he hated Menoetius's son,

271while he was alive and the aide of Aeacus' grandson;

272and he loathed that he become the prey of the dogs

273of Troy — and so he roused his companions to defend him.

274And at first the Trojans pushed back the bright-eyed Achaeans:

275they left the body and shrank away, and the high-hearted Trojans

276took not one of them with their spears, for all their striving,

277but set to hauling the corpse. Yet only a little while

278were the Achaeans to hold off from it: they were rallied fast

279by Ajax, who in build, and in what he wrought,

280surpassed all the Danaans after the blameless son of Peleus.

281He swept straight through the front-fighters, like a wild boar

282in his strength, that up in the mountains scatters hounds

283and strong young men with ease, wheeling through the glens:

284so the son of illustrious Telamon, shining Ajax,

285swept in and easily scattered the Trojan battle-lines

286that had taken their stand over Patroclus, minded above all

287to haul him to their own city and win the glory.

288Now Hippothous, the shining son of Pelasgian Lethus,17

289was dragging him by the foot through the strong encounter,

290a shield-strap bound around the ankle-tendons,

291doing a favor to Hector and the Trojans. But swiftly

292the evil came on him that no one could hold off, wish it as they might:

293the son of Telamon, springing at him through the crowd,

294struck close-in, through the helmet's bronze cheek-pieces;

295and the horsehair-crested helmet split around the spear-point,

296struck by the great spear and the heavy hand,

297and the brain came running out of the wound along the socket,

298bloody; and there and then his strength was unstrung, and from his hands

299he let the foot of great-hearted Patroclus drop

300to lie on the ground; and he fell face-down on the corpse beside it,

301far from deep-soiled Larisa; nor did he repay

302his dear parents the price of his rearing: his life was short,

303broken under the spear of great-hearted Ajax.

304And Hector in turn cast at Ajax with his shining spear;

305but he saw it coming and dodged the bronze spear —

306barely; and it struck Schedius, great-hearted Iphitus's son,

307far the best of the Phocians, whose home was in famed

308Panopeus, where he was king over many men:

309it struck him under the middle of the collarbone, and through it

310the bronze point drove, and came out at the base of the shoulder.

311He fell with a thud, and his armor clattered upon him.

312And Ajax struck war-minded Phorcys, Phaenops's son,

313full in the belly as he bestrode Hippothous,

314and burst the plate of his corslet, and the bronze

315spilled his bowels through; and falling in the dust he clutched the earth in his palm.

316And the front-fighters gave ground, and glorious Hector;

317and the Argives shouted loud, and dragged away the corpses,

318Phorcys and Hippothous, and loosed the armor from their shoulders.

319And then once more the Trojans, before the Achaeans dear to Ares,

320would have climbed into Ilion, broken by their own weakness,

321and the Argives would have won glory even beyond the portion of Zeus18

322by their own power and strength — had not Apollo himself

323stirred up Aeneas, in the likeness of the herald

324Periphas, Epytus's son, who at his old father's side

325had grown grey in his heraldry, his heart a well of kindly counsel;

326in his likeness Apollo, son of Zeus, spoke to him:

327"Aeneas — how could you and yours hold steep Ilion

328even against a god? — as I have seen other men do,

329trusting their power and strength, their manhood

330and their own numbers, though their people were too few.

331But Zeus wills the victory to us, far rather

332than to the Danaans — and you flinch unspeakably, and will not fight."

333So he spoke; and Aeneas knew Apollo who strikes from afar,

334looking him in the face, and called aloud to Hector:

335"Hector — and you other leaders of Trojans and allies —

336this now is disgrace: to climb back into Ilion

337before the Achaeans dear to Ares, broken by our own weakness.

338No — for a god has just now stood beside me and said

339Zeus, highest deviser of battle, is still our helper.

340So let us go straight for the Danaans, and not let them

341bring dead Patroclus to their ships at their ease."

342So he spoke, and sprang far out beyond the front-fighters, and stood;

343and they wheeled about, and stood to face the Achaeans.

344Then Aeneas wounded Leiocritus with his spear,

345the son of Arisbas, brave companion of Lycomedes;

346and as he fell, Lycomedes dear to Ares pitied him,

347and came and stood very close, and cast with his shining spear,

348and struck Apisaon, Hippasus's son, shepherd of the people,

349in the liver below the midriff, and unstrung his knees on the instant —

350the man who had come out of deep-soiled Paeonia,

351and was their best in battle after Asteropaeus.

352And as he fell, warlike Asteropaeus pitied him,

353and he too drove forward, willing to fight the Danaans;

354but there was no way any longer: fenced with shields on every side

355they stood around Patroclus, spears held out before them.

356For Ajax ranged among them all with strict command:19

357no man to give back a step from the body,

358no man to fight out in front of the other Achaeans,

359but to stand close over him, and fight hand to hand.

360So gigantic Ajax commanded; and the earth grew soaked

361with purple blood, and they fell in heaps together,

362dead men of the Trojans and their overmastering allies

363and Danaans too — for not without blood did these defend —

364but far fewer of them perished: they remembered always

365to ward sheer ruin from one another in the crowd.

366So they fought on, like fire; nor would you have said20

367the sun was safe in the sky, or the moon:

368for they were held in mist, all the best men in the battle

369who stood around the dead son of Menoetius.

370But the other Trojans and strong-greaved Achaeans

371fought at their ease under clear air: the piercing light

372of the sun lay spread, and no cloud showed over all

373the earth or the mountains; and they fought in pauses,

374keeping well apart, dodging each other's

375missiles of groaning. But those in the middle suffered

376in the mist and the war, worn down by the pitiless bronze —

377all who were the best. And two men had not yet heard —

378two famous men, Thrasymedes and Antilochus —

379that blameless Patroclus was dead: they thought him

380living still, fighting Trojans in the front of the crowd;

381and watching against the death and rout of their companions

382they fought apart, since Nestor had so ordered them,

383when he drove them to the war from the black ships.

384But for the others, all day long the great struggle of grinding

385strife stood taut: with the weariness and the sweat of it,

386without respite, the knees and shins, the feet beneath,

387the hands and eyes of every man were spattered

388in the fight around the brave aide of the swift-footed son of Aeacus.

389As when a man gives his people the hide of a great bull21

390to stretch, soaked through with fat:

391they take it and stand in a circle and stretch it

392outward; and quickly the moisture goes, and the fat sinks in

393with so many pulling, and the whole hide is stretched taut through:

394so this way and that, in a little space, both sides hauled

395the corpse; and their hearts had great hope —

396the Trojans, of dragging him to Ilion; the Achaeans,

397to the hollow ships. And around him the brawl rose

398savage: not Ares driver of armies, not Athena

399could have looked at it and scorned it, however great their anger:

400such was the evil toil of men and horses Zeus

401stretched taut over Patroclus on that day.

402And brilliant Achilles knew nothing at all, yet, of Patroclus dead:22

403for the battle was fought far from the swift ships,

404under the wall of the Trojans. So never in his heart

405did he think him dead — but living, pressed hard on the gates,

406and coming home again; since he never thought at all

407that Patroclus would sack the city without him — nor with him:

408for often he had learned as much from his mother, hearing her apart,

409when she brought him word of the purpose of great Zeus.

410But now his mother told him nothing of the evil that had happened —

411so great — that his far-dearest companion had perished.

412And they around the corpse, holding their sharpened spears,

413pressed close without respite, and killed each other;

414and thus would one of the bronze-shirted Achaeans say:

415"Friends — no glory for us in going back23

416to the hollow ships. Sooner let the black earth gape

417for all of us here. That would be better by far, at once,

418than to leave this man to the horse-taming Trojans

419to haul to their own city, and win the glory."

420And thus in turn would one of the great-hearted Trojans say:

421"Friends — even if it is fate that we be broken beside this man,

422all of us alike, let no one yet give way from the war."

423So a man would speak, and stir the fury of each.

424So they fought on; and the iron din went up

425through the barren air to the brazen sky.24

426But the horses of Aeacus' grandson, apart from the battle,25

427were weeping, ever since they first had learned their charioteer

428was fallen in the dust at the hands of man-slaughtering Hector.

429Automedon, Diores' stout son, tried them —

430lashing them again and again with the quick whip's stroke,

431again and again with honeyed words, and again with curses:

432but the pair would not go back to the ships and the broad Hellespont,

433nor into the battle after the Achaeans.

434As a grave-stone stands unmoving, set on the barrow

435of a dead man or a woman,

436so they stood, unstirring, with the beautiful chariot,

437drooping their heads to the earth; and hot tears

438ran from their eyelids to the ground as they mourned

439in longing for their charioteer; and their rich manes were fouled,

440streaming down from the yoke-pad on both sides of the yoke.

441And seeing the two of them mourning, the son of Cronos pitied them,

442and he shook his head, and spoke to his own spirit:

443"Ah, poor pair — why did we give you to king Peleus,26

444a mortal — you who are ageless and deathless?

445Was it so that you too might have sorrows among unhappy men?

446For there is nothing anywhere more pitiful than man,

447of all things that breathe and creep upon the earth.

448But not on you, at least, and your intricate chariot

449shall Hector, Priam's son, ride. I will not allow it.

450Is it not enough that he has the armor, and vaunts in it — just so?

451Into your knees and into your hearts I will throw fury,

452so you may carry Automedon safe out of the war

453to the hollow ships. For I will still hand the enemy glory,

454to kill, until they reach the well-benched ships,

455and the sun goes down and the sacred darkness comes on."

456So speaking, he breathed brave fury into the horses;

457and the two of them, shaking the dust from their manes to the ground,

458swiftly carried the quick chariot among Trojans and Achaeans.

459And behind them Automedon fought, grieving for his companion,27

460swooping with the horses like a vulture on geese:

461easily he would run back out of the Trojans' clamor,

462and easily charge in again, pressing through the thick crowd.

463But he took no men when he swept in pursuit:

464it was not possible, alone in the sacred chariot,

465to go at them with the spear and hold the swift horses.

466At last a companion saw him with his own eyes —

467Alcimedon, the son of Laerces, Haemon's son;

468he stood behind the chariot and spoke to Automedon:

469"Automedon — which of the gods put this profitless plan

470in your chest, and took your good wits from you?

471— fighting the Trojans alone, in the front of the crowd,

472while your companion lies killed, and Hector himself

473glories with the armor of Aeacus' grandson on his own shoulders."

474And Automedon, Diores' son, answered him:

475"Alcimedon — what other Achaean is your equal

476at holding the fury of immortal horses in hand —

477except Patroclus, the equal of the gods in counsel,

478while he lived? Now death and fate have overtaken him.

479So: you take over the whip and the shining reins,

480and I will step down from the horses, and fight."

481So he spoke; and Alcimedon, springing onto the rescue-chariot,

482quickly caught up the whip and reins in his hands,

483and Automedon leapt down. And glorious Hector saw it,

484and at once he spoke to Aeneas, who was near:

485"Aeneas — counselor of the bronze-shirted Trojans —

486I see the two horses of the swift-footed son of Aeacus

487come out into the battle with feeble charioteers.

488I could hope to take them, if you in your heart

489are willing; since if the two of us charged, they would not

490dare stand against us face to face, and fight the war-god."

491So he spoke, and the brave son of Anchises did not disobey.

492The two went straight on, their shoulders wrapped in oxhides

493dry and stiff, with heavy bronze hammered over them;

494and with them went Chromius, and godlike Aretus,

495the two together; and their hearts had great hope

496of killing the men, and driving off the strong-necked horses —

497fools: not without blood were they to come away

498again from Automedon. He, with a prayer to Zeus the father,

499was filled with courage and strength in his darkened heart;

500and at once he spoke to Alcimedon, his trusted companion:

501"Alcimedon — hold the horses not far off from me,

502but breathing right on my back. For I do not think

503Hector, Priam's son, will be checked in his fury

504before he has mounted behind the fair-maned horses of Achilles —

505with the two of us killed — and routed the ranks of the Argive

506men; or else been taken himself among the first.

507So speaking he called the two Ajaxes and Menelaus:

508"You two Ajaxes, leaders of the Argives, and Menelaus —

509leave the corpse to the best men you have, to stand

510and bestride it and hold off the enemy ranks,

511and from the two of us — living men — beat off the pitiless day.

512For here they bear down hard through the war of tears,

513Hector and Aeneas, the best men of the Trojans.

514But these things lie on the knees of the gods:

515I too will let fly — and all the rest will be Zeus's care."

516He spoke, and balanced his long-shadowed spear and hurled it,

517and struck the shield of Aretus, balanced every way;

518and the shield did not hold the spear: the bronze drove clean through,

519and through the war-belt it tore into the pit of his belly.

520As when a strong-grown man with a sharp axe,27

521striking behind the horns of an ox of the field,

522shears the tendon clean through, and the ox springs forward and drops:

523so Aretus sprang forward and fell on his back; and the spear,

524quivering razor-sharp in his bowels, unstrung his limbs.

525And Hector cast at Automedon with his shining spear;

526but he saw it coming and dodged the bronze spear,

527ducking forward; and behind him the long spear

528stuck fast in the ground, and the butt-end stood quivering;

529and there at last massive Ares stilled its fury.

530And now they would have gone at each other with swords, close in,

531had not the two Ajaxes parted them for all their heat,

532coming through the crowd at their companion's call.

533In dread of them the others drew back again —

534Hector and Aeneas and godlike Chromius —

535and left Aretus lying there, torn through the heart.

536And Automedon, the equal of swift Ares,

537stripped the armor, and spoke his triumph over him:

538"Now I have eased my heart a little of its grief

539for Menoetius's dead son — though the man I killed was worse."

540So speaking, he took up the bloody spoils and set them

541in the chariot, and mounted, feet and hands

542all bloody, like a lion that has fed on a bull.

543And back over Patroclus the strong encounter was stretched again,

544grinding, of many tears; and Athena woke the struggle,

545come down from heaven: for wide-seeing Zeus had sent her

546to drive on the Danaans — for now his mind was turned.

547As Zeus stretches a shimmering rainbow out of heaven28

548for mortals to see, a portent either of war

549or of a chill storm — the kind that stops men's work

550on the face of the earth, and troubles the flocks:

551so she, wrapping herself in a shimmering cloud,

552sank into the Achaean host, and woke each man.

553First she spoke to Atreus' son, urging him on —

554strong Menelaus, for he was nearest her —

555likened to Phoenix in body and tireless voice:

556"Yours will be the hanging head, Menelaus, yours the disgrace,

557if under the wall of the Trojans the running dogs

558drag the trusted companion of illustrious Achilles.

559Hold on with all your strength, and drive on the whole army."

560Then answered her Menelaus, good at the war-cry:

561"Phoenix — old father, elder born long ago — if only Athena

562would give me strength, and hold off the rush of missiles!

563Then I would be willing indeed to stand and defend

564Patroclus: for his death has touched me to the heart.

565But Hector has the terrible fury of fire, and will not stop

566cutting men down with the bronze; for Zeus hands him the glory."

567So he spoke; and the goddess grey-eyed Athena was glad

568that he had prayed to her first of all the gods.

569She put strength into his shoulders and his knees,

570and set in his chest the boldness of the fly,29

571which — beaten off again and again from a man's skin —

572persists in biting; and human blood is sweet to it:

573with boldness like that she filled his darkened heart,

574and he strode over Patroclus and cast with his shining spear.

575Now there was among the Trojans one Podes, Eëtion's son,30

576a rich man and a brave one; and Hector honored him

577above the people, for he was his dear companion at the feast.

578Him tawny-haired Menelaus struck on the war-belt

579as he sprang away in flight, and drove the bronze clean through:

580he fell with a thud; and Atreus' son Menelaus

581dragged the corpse away from the Trojans into the band of his companions.

582But Apollo came and stood by Hector, and stirred him,

583in the likeness of Phaenops, Asius's son, who of all

584his guest-friends was dearest to him, with his house at Abydos;

585in his likeness Apollo the worker-from-afar spoke to him:

586"Hector — what other Achaean will fear you now?

587Look how you flinched from Menelaus, who before this

588was a soft spearman — and now he is gone, alone, lifting

589the corpse away from under the Trojans: he has killed your trusted

590companion, brave among the front-fighters — Podes, Eëtion's son."

591So he spoke; and the black cloud of grief wrapped Hector round,

592and he came through the front-fighters helmed in blazing bronze.

593And then the son of Cronos took up the tasseled aegis,31

594glittering, and hid Ida under clouds,

595and lightened and thundered hugely, and shook it,

596and gave victory to the Trojans, and routed the Achaeans.

597First to lead the rout was Peneleos the Boeotian:32

598turned forever toward the enemy, he was hit in the shoulder,

599a grazing hit on the crest of it; but the spear-point of Polydamas

600scored clean to the bone — for he had come close when he threw.

601Then Hector wounded Leïtus, close in, on the wrist —

602the son of great-hearted Alectryon — and stopped his fighting;

603he shrank back, glancing round, for his heart no longer hoped

604to hold a spear steady in hand and fight the Trojans.

605And as Hector chased Leïtus, Idomeneus

606struck him on the corslet, on the chest beside the nipple;

607but the long spear snapped at the socket, and the Trojans

608shouted. And Hector cast at Idomeneus, Deucalion's son,

609standing in his chariot; and missed him by a little,

610but struck the aide and charioteer of Meriones,

611Coeranus, who had followed him from well-founded Lyctus.

612(Idomeneus had come on foot at first, leaving the double-ended

613ships, and would have handed the Trojans a great triumph

614had not Coeranus driven up fast with the swift horses:

615to him he came as light, and beat off the pitiless day —

616and lost his own life under man-slaughtering Hector.)

617Him Hector struck below the jaw, under the ear, and the spear-butt

618dashed his teeth out at the roots, and cut the tongue through the middle:

619he crashed from the chariot, and poured the reins to the ground.

620And Meriones stooped and gathered them up off the plain

621in his own hands, and called to Idomeneus:

622"Lay on the whip now, till you come to the swift ships!

623You see it yourself: the power is no longer with the Achaeans."

624So he spoke; and Idomeneus whipped the fair-maned horses

625back to the hollow ships; for fear had fallen on his heart.

626Nor did great-hearted Ajax fail to see it, and Menelaus —

627that Zeus was giving the Trojans the tide-turning victory;

628and great Telamonian Ajax began to speak among them:

629"Shame! By now even a man who was a great fool33

630could see that father Zeus himself is helping the Trojans.

631Every missile of theirs strikes home, whoever lets fly,

632coward or brave man: Zeus guides them all alike;

633and ours drop to the ground, every one, spent for nothing.

634Come then — let us ourselves think out the best plan,

635both how to haul the corpse away, and how we ourselves

636may come home to be a joy to our dear companions,

637who look this way, grieving, and say that no longer

638can the fury of man-slaughtering Hector and his invincible

639hands be held — that he will fall on the black ships.

640And would there were some companion to carry word quickly

641to Peleus' son; for I do not think he has even heard

642the wretched news — that his dear companion has perished.

643But nowhere can I see such a man among the Achaeans:

644they are held in the mist, themselves and their horses alike.

645Father Zeus — deliver the sons of the Achaeans out of the mist;

646make clear sky; grant our eyes to see;

647and in the light — kill us, since that is now your pleasure."

648So he spoke; and the father pitied him as he wept,34

649and at once he scattered the mist and thrust the fog away,

650and the sun blazed out, and the whole battle came clear.

651And then Ajax spoke to Menelaus, good at the war-cry:

652"Look about you now, Zeus-nurtured Menelaus — see if you can

653sight Antilochus alive, great-hearted Nestor's son,

654and send him running to war-minded Achilles

655with word that his far-dearest companion has perished."

656So he spoke, and Menelaus, good at the war-cry, did not disobey,

657and set out — like a lion from a farmstead35

658that has grown weary at last of provoking the dogs and the men,

659who will not let him take the fat of the herd,

660watching all night long; and he, craving the meat,

661charges in but gets nowhere, for javelins thick and fast

662fly out to meet him from daring hands,

663and burning brands besides, and he flinches at those for all his fury,

664and at dawn he goes off apart, sullen at heart:

665so from Patroclus went Menelaus, good at the war-cry,

666much against his will; for he feared the Achaeans

667in the grim rout would leave him, prey for the enemy;

668and long he charged Meriones and the two Ajaxes:

669"You two Ajaxes, leaders of the Argives, and Meriones —

670now let each man remember the gentleness

671of poor Patroclus: to every one of us he knew how

672to be kind, while he lived. Now death and fate have overtaken him."

673So speaking, tawny-haired Menelaus went off,

674glancing on every side, like an eagle — the bird

675men say has the sharpest sight of all things under heaven:

676high as he rides, the swift-footed hare does not escape him

677crouched under a leafy bush, but down upon it

678he swoops, and seizes it fast, and takes its life:

679so then, Zeus-nurtured Menelaus, your shining eyes

680went ranging everywhere across the crowd of your companions,

681hunting Nestor's son, if you might see him still alive.

682And he saw him quickly, on the far left of the whole battle,

683heartening his companions and driving them to fight;

684and standing close, tawny-haired Menelaus spoke:

685"Antilochus — come here, Zeus-nurtured; hear

686the wretched news — the thing that should never have happened.

687Already, I think, you see it with your own eyes

688and know it: a god is rolling calamity on the Danaans,

689and victory is the Trojans'. And killed is the best of the Achaeans —

690Patroclus; and a great longing is built for the Danaans.

691Run to the ships of the Achaeans, quick, and tell

692Achilles — he may yet bring the body safe to his ship,

693naked: for Hector of the flashing helmet has the armor."

694So he spoke; and Antilochus heard the word with horror.35

695For a long time speechlessness held him: his eyes

696filled with tears, and his strong voice was checked.

697Yet not even so did he neglect Menelaus's charge:

698he set off at a run, and gave his armor to his blameless companion

699Laodocus, who wheeled the whole-hoofed horses close beside him.

700And his feet carried him weeping out of the battle,

701bearing the evil word to Achilles, Peleus' son.

702And your heart, Zeus-nurtured Menelaus, had no will36

703to defend the worn companions there where Antilochus

704had left them, and great longing was built for the Pylians;

705but he sent them brilliant Thrasymedes,

706and himself went back again to bestride the hero Patroclus;

707and running he stood beside the two Ajaxes, and spoke at once:

708"That man I have sent off to the swift ships,

709to go to Achilles of the swift feet. But I do not think

710Achilles will come now, however great his anger at brilliant Hector:

711there is no way he could fight the Trojans naked.

712So let us ourselves think out the best plan,

713both how to haul the corpse away, and how we ourselves

714may escape death and doom out of the Trojans' clamor."

715Then great Telamonian Ajax answered him:

716"All you have said is right and true, far-famed Menelaus.

717You and Meriones — stoop down, quickly, get under him,

718lift the corpse and carry him out of the toil. And behind you

719we two will fight the Trojans and brilliant Hector,

720one heart in us as we share one name, we two who before now

721have stood the sharp war-god side by side, together."

722So he spoke; and they took the corpse up off the ground in their arms,

723high and mightily; and behind them the Trojan army

724screamed, when they saw the Achaeans lifting the body,

725and charged like hounds that spring on a wounded boar37

726ahead of the young hunters:

727for a while they race in, furious to tear him —

728but when he wheels among them, sure of his strength,

729back they shrink, and scatter in terror this way and that:

730so the Trojans for a while kept up the chase in a mass,

731stabbing with swords and double-curved spears;

732but whenever the two Ajaxes turned about and stood

733against them, their color changed, and not one dared

734dart forward and fight for the corpse.

735So the two of them, straining, carried the body out of the battle

736toward the hollow ships; and the war stretched taut behind them,

737savage as fire that leaps on a city of men,

738rising suddenly, and blazes, and the houses crumble

739in the great glare, and the wind's strength roars across it:

740so the relentless din of horses and of spearmen

741came on behind them as they went.

742And they — like mules who have thrown their great strength into the work,

743dragging down from the mountain along a rugged track

744a roof-beam or a huge ship-timber, and inside them the heart

745is worn with weariness and sweat together as they strain:

746so the two of them, straining, carried the body. And behind them

747the two Ajaxes held the enemy — as a wooded ridge

748holds back water, a ridge lying clean across a plain,

749which checks even the punishing currents of powerful rivers,

750and at once turns the flood of them all to wander

751loose across the lowland; and all their strength cannot break it:

752so the two Ajaxes, behind, kept fending off the battle

753of the Trojans. But they followed hard, and two above all,

754Aeneas, Anchises' son, and glorious Hector.

755And as a cloud of starlings goes, or of jackdaws,38

756shrieking ruin, when they see coming at them

757a falcon, that carries death to small birds:

758so before Aeneas and Hector the young Achaean men

759went shrieking ruin, and forgot their war-craft.

760And much fine armor fell around and about the ditch

761as the Danaans fled; and there was no pause in the war.

Notes

1 lines 1–6 — Menelaus over the body "as a mother-cow, lowing, stands over her first calf, who never knew birth before" (πρωτοτόκος κινυρή — the lowing of a first-time mother). The poem opens its longest battle with its most maternal image: the corpse-defense that will consume the book is presented first not as war but as instinct — and given to Menelaus, the poem's specialist in loyal, unglamorous care. ↩

2 lines 9–32 — Euphorbus claims the spoils by right of first blood (his dart at 16.806-815); Menelaus's answer settles the Hyperenor account opened at 14.516 — with details the earlier narrative never gave (the mockery, "sorriest fighter of the Danaans"): the poem's ledgers, as at 17.24-28, remember more than they recorded. His closing proverb — "When the thing is done, even the fool learns" (ῥεχθὲν δέ τε νήπιος ἔγνω) — became Greek proverbial stock permanently. ↩

3 lines 33–42 — Euphorbus's revenge-speech is a family portrait: the brother's widow "in the depth of her new bridal chamber," the parents' "cursed mourning," and the imagined homecoming — your head and armor "in the hands of Panthous and brilliant Phrontis." He is the third Panthoid the war has engaged (Polydamas, Hyperenor); by naming his own parents he writes the address on his own death-notice. ↩

4 lines 43–60 — The kill and the double image. First the hair: "soaked with blood — hair like the Graces', and the braids pinched tight in gold and silver" — the poem's dandy-warrior, beautiful even in the detail of his hair-clasps. Then the olive sapling: reared by hand in a lonely place by welling water, rocked by every breathing of wind, white with blossom — torn out by one squall and laid flat. The most tender death-simile in the Iliad, spent on a minor Trojan — and made immortal twice over: Porphyry's Life of Pythagoras quotes the passage because Pythagoras claimed to have been Euphorbus (16.[43]); the sage's remembered death arrived pre-fitted with the loveliest lines in Homer. ↩

5 lines 61–69 — The lion takes the finest heifer: lines 63-64 repeat 11.175-176 verbatim (our English kept) — the neck broken first, the blood and inward parts gulped down — while dogs and herdsmen clamor "from a distance," held by green fear. Menelaus, so often the poem's second-rank fighter, is for once the beast nobody dares approach. ↩

6 lines 70–105 — Apollo-as-Mentes turns Hector back with the horses-taunt that reuses Dolon's dream (10.402-404, our English kept): the unattainable team as the measure of Trojan overreach, third time offered, never possessed. Menelaus's soliloquy that follows is the poem's clearest statement of the shame-calculus (fixed frame lines kept: "Troubled, he spoke..."; "But why does my own heart argue..."): abandoning the body is disgrace, fighting a god-backed man is doom — "When a man wills, against the god's face, to fight a man whom a god honors, a great woe rolls down on him quickly" — and the resolution is neither: withdraw, find Ajax, return. Prudence with a plan is not cowardice; the poem lets the calculation stand unmocked. ↩

7 lines 109–113 — The reluctant lion — driven from the fold, "the brave heart in his chest goes stiff with the cold" — the first of the book's two borrowings from Ajax's great retreat- simile family of Book 11 (see note [35] for the second): the poem now clothes Menelaus in the images it minted for its strongest defender. ↩

8 lines 125–127 — Hector's intention, stated flat: to shear the head from the shoulders and give the trunk "to the dogs of Troy." The corpse-outrage theme crosses a line no one has yet crossed in act — and it is Hector, the poem's most humane warrior, who first conceives it. Books 22-24 will weigh everything that follows against these three lines. ↩

9 lines 132–139 — Ajax over the corpse as a lion over his cubs, the whole scowl of his brows drawn down over his eyes — guardianship again (note [1]), now paternal; and Menelaus beside him "nursing great grief." The two images of the book's opening — mother-cow and lion-father — bracket the dead man like parents. ↩

10 lines 140–168 — Glaucus's rebuke — the harshest any ally gives Hector — climaxes in the book's most startling proposal: haul Patroclus into Ilion and trade him — "the Argives would quickly ransom back the fine armor of Sarpedon, and we would bring the man himself into Ilion." Corpse for corpse, gear for gear: the dead as exchangeable stores of value. The poem's whole final movement (Hector's body ransomed for weighed gold) is glimpsed here first as an ally's grievance; and the grievance itself — "there was never any thanks" — is the mercenary's ledger Hector must answer with his own accounting at 220-232. ↩

11 lines 169–182 — Hector's answer reuses the theology of 16.688-692 (our English kept): "always the mind of Zeus... is stronger: he sends even the brave man running... and then again himself drives a man to fight." True doctrine — and, as ever with Hector, deployed exactly where it excuses him. The challenge ("stand beside me, old friend — watch my work") converts the quarrel into witness. ↩

12 lines 183–197 — The changing of the armor, staged "apart from the battle of many tears" like a rite: his own gear sent into Ilion, the immortal gear put on — and the poem recites its provenance as elegy: gods to Peleus; Peleus, "grown old," to his son; "but the son did not grow old in his father's armor." The line every reader remembers: the armor's history is a history of interrupted inheritance, and Hector is now inside it. ↩

13 lines 198–214 — Zeus's head-shake over Hector is the poem's theology of recompense at its most precise: the armor taken "not by right" (οὐ κατὰ κόσμον); the grant of "great power" made explicitly τῶν ποινήν — as blood-price, compensation paid in advance for the death already decreed: "never home out of the battle will you come, for Andromache to take from you the famous armor of Peleus' son." Andromache, who begged him to fight from the wall (6.431), enters the sentence as the person who will not perform the homecoming ritual. Then the uncanny grace-note: the armor fits — Zeus himself "fitted the armor to Hector's body, and Ares entered him." The gift, the doom, and the ecstasy are one act. ↩

14 lines 215–232 — Hector's speech to the allies is the epic's frankest look at coalition warfare: "it was not for a crowd's sake I gathered you... I wear down my own people for gifts and provisions, to feed the courage in each of you" — the war economy Glaucus invoked (147-148) conceded in full. Then the third and strangest use of the intimacy-word: die or live — "that is the trysting-talk of war" (πολέμου ὀαριστύς; 13.291, 14.216). And the bounty: half-shares with Hector for whoever drags Patroclus in — glory made divisible and priced. ↩

15 lines 233–261, 260–261 — The advance is stamped "fools" (over that body Ajax "was to rob many of life"); Ajax's grim realism ("It is not so much for the corpse I fear... as for my own head"); Menelaus's call to the leaders "who drink at the public cost" — rank invoked through its perquisites; and then the narrator's own confession, athetized by Zenodotus: "But of the rest — who could speak their names out of his own mind...?" The inverted Muse-invocation: where 2.484 called on the goddesses because memory fails, here the singer simply shows the failure. The ancients heard a lapse; it reads as the book's honesty about crowds. ↩

16 lines 262–273 — Zeus pours mist over the body's defenders with the poem's most carefully argued motive: "never in the time before had he hated Menoetius's son, while he was alive... and he loathed that he become the prey of the dogs of Troy." The god who engineered the death (16.644-655) now spends the day protecting the corpse: the two attitudes are not reconciled — they are both true, and the poem leaves them side by side. ↩

17 lines 274–318 — The first full swing of the tug: Hippothous drags the body by a shield-strap bound round the ankle- tendons — the technical, butcher's detail — and dies across it, "far from deep-soiled Larisa; nor did he repay his dear parents the price of his rearing" (θρέπτρα — child-care as a debt the dead leave unpaid; cf. 4.477-478). Schedius the Phocian falls to Hector — the death our register logged when his namesake died at 15.515: the poem keeps both Schedii and spends them in different books. The exchange ends with the Argives dragging away not Patroclus but the Trojan dead: every body on this field is now cargo. ↩

18 lines 319–334 — The counterfactual — Troy broken "even beyond the portion of Zeus" (ὑπὲρ Διὸς αἶσαν) by mortal strength alone — is the strongest such clause in the poem: fate has slack in it, and only Apollo's intervention takes it up. The god borrows the shape of Periphas the herald, "grown grey in his heraldry" beside old Epytus — the poem's tenderness for professions again — and Aeneas knows him at once ("looking him in the face"): the recognition granted to the pious Aeneas and almost no one else. ↩

19 lines 356–365 — Ajax's field-order is the tactical heart of the book: no retreat from the body, no heroics out in front, "stand close over him, and fight hand to hand." The result the poet audits precisely: the ground purple, dead on both sides — "but far fewer of them perished: they remembered always to ward sheer ruin from one another in the crowd." The phalanx-discipline of Books 13 and 16 has become doctrine, stated and costed: cohesion is casualty-arithmetic. ↩

20 lines 366–383 — The doubled sky: over Patroclus, mist and night — "you would not have said the sun was safe, or the moon"; everywhere else "the piercing light of the sun lay spread, and no cloud showed over all the earth or the mountains," where men fight "in pauses, keeping well apart." A private midnight for the best, sunshine for the rest — the poem's most surreal battlefield image, ending on the two who fight in the light and do not know (Thrasymedes and Antilochus): light as ignorance, darkness as the place where the truth is. ↩

21 lines 384–401 — The tanning simile: the great bull's hide, soaked in fat, stretched in a circle by many hands "until the moisture goes and the fat sinks in... and the whole hide is stretched taut through" — the day's whole war compressed into a work-yard: the corpse pulled "this way and that, in a little space," by professionals, patiently. Even Ares and Athena "could not have scorned" the toil — the gods invoked as connoisseurs of labor. Nothing in the poem is grimmer than the craftsmanship of this comparison. ↩

22 lines 401–411 — And Achilles does not know. The passage Zenodotus cut (404-425 in his edition): Thetis "often" told him the purpose of Zeus, "but now his mother told him nothing of the evil that had happened." Inside it, the strangest clause in the poem's plotting: Achilles never hoped Patroclus would sack the city "without him — nor with him": he has carried his mother's prophecies far enough to know Troy is not theirs to take together. The ignorance is thus doubly cruel: he knows the large doom and not the small one that is running toward him on Antilochus's feet. ↩

23 lines 412–425 — The paired anonymous vows (τις-speeches): the Achaean — "Sooner let the black earth gape for all of us here"; the Trojan — "even if it is fate that we be broken beside this man, all of us alike, let no one yet give way." The two armies, nameless, say the same sentence from opposite sides. ↩

24 lines 424–425 — "the iron din went up through the barren air to the brazen sky" — iron, bronze, and the barren middle air (ἀτρύγετος, the sea's own epithet lent to the ether): the war as metallurgy filling the whole space between earth and heaven. ↩

25 lines 426–440 — The weeping horses. Immortal Xanthus and Balius stand apart from the battle "as a grave-stone stands unmoving, set on the barrow of a dead man or a woman" — creatures of wind become their charioteer's own monument — heads to the earth, "hot tears" running, "their rich manes fouled, streaming down from the yoke-pad." Automedon tries whip, honey, and curses; grief does not answer to any of the three. It is the poem's only animal mourning, and among its greatest inventions: sorrow without speech, loyalty without hope, in bodies that cannot die. ↩

26 lines 441–455 — Zeus's pity — the sentence at the poem's center of gravity: "why did we give you to king Peleus, a mortal — you who are ageless and deathless? Was it so that you too might have sorrows among unhappy men? For there is nothing anywhere more pitiful than man, of all things that breathe and creep upon the earth." The god who runs the war states its final metaphysics: mortality itself, not any battle, is the pity of it; the immortal horses' one flaw is contact with us. Then the practical rider: Hector shall not have them ("Is it not enough that he has the armor...?"), and the sunset clause of the day repeated word for word from 11.193-194 — the doom still running on schedule beneath the compassion. ↩

27 lines 456–542 — The Automedon sequence: the charioteer fights "like a vulture among geese," is talked into sense by Alcimedon (the Myrmidon fifth-captain of 16.197), and the two-man crew re-forms — driver breathing on his back — against Hector, Aeneas, Chromius, and Aretus. Aretus takes the spear "as when a strong-grown man with a sharp axe, striking behind the horns of an ox of the field, shears the tendon clean through, and the ox springs forward and drops" — the sacrificial slaughter-stroke on the battlefield. And Automedon's vaunt is the book's grief-economy in one line: "Now I have eased my heart a little of its grief... though the man I killed was worse." Vengeance as approximate payment; the ledger never balances, and he says so. ↩

28 lines 543–552 — Athena comes down wrapped in "a shimmering cloud" like the rainbow Zeus stretches "for mortals to see, a portent either of war or of a chill storm that stops men's work on the face of the earth, and troubles the flocks." The rainbow as omen (11.27-28 made it armor- decoration) — beauty that means interruption: war for men, weather for the sheep, the two disasters given equal standing in the farmer's sky. ↩

29 lines 553–573 — Athena-as-Phoenix, and the fly. She fills Menelaus "with the boldness of the fly, which — beaten off again and again from a man's skin — persists in biting; and human blood is sweet to it." The smallest courage-simile in epic and the most audacious: persistence stripped of all dignity and honored anyway — appetite as valor. Plutarch and Galen both quote it; no other poet would have dared attach it to a king at the moment a goddess strengthens him. ↩

30 lines 575–591 — Podes, Eëtion's son — "his dear companion at the feast" (εἰλαπιναστής): Hector's table-intimate, rich and brave, killed in flight by the man Apollo then calls "before this a soft spearman" (μαλθακὸς αἰχμητής). The grief-cloud that wraps Hector is personal — the war has finally reached his own mess-table. The name Eëtion tolls again (Andromache's father, 6.396; Pedasus's city, 16.153): everything Hector loves seems to descend from men of that name. ↩

31 lines 593–596 — Zeus ends the argument by hand: the tasseled aegis taken up, Ida hidden in cloud, lightning, thunder, the shake — "and gave victory to the Trojans, and routed the Achaeans." The instrument Apollo borrowed at 15.229 is back with its owner; four verbs, and the day tilts. ↩

32 lines 597–625 — The rout ledger: Peneleos and Leïtus wounded (the two Boeotian catalogue-leaders of 2.494 subtracted in two lines); Idomeneus's spear snapping on Hector's corslet; and Coeranus of Lyctus, who dies taking the cast meant for his king — "to him he came as light, and beat off the pitiless day — and lost his own life": the poem's formula of salvation (φάος, light) priced at one charioteer. Meriones stooping to gather the fallen reins off the plain while the world collapses is the kind of detail this poet never omits: someone must always pick up the reins. ↩

33 lines 626–647 — Ajax's prayer. First the observation ("even a man who was a great fool could see that father Zeus himself is helping the Trojans — every missile of theirs strikes home... and ours drop to the ground, every one"), then the double need (save the body; send word to Achilles), and then the request that Longinus (On the Sublime 9.10) set above nearly everything in literature: "Father Zeus — deliver the sons of the Achaeans out of the mist; make clear sky; grant our eyes to see; and in the light — kill us, since that is now your pleasure" (ἐν δὲ φάει καὶ ὄλεσσον). He does not pray to live; he prays to see. The demand is worthy of the fighter the poem has built across seventeen books: no complaint, no bargain — visibility, and then whatever Zeus likes. ↩

34 lines 648–650 — "And the father pitied him as he wept" — the tears are Ajax's (the strongest man in the army weeping inside his prayer, a detail easy to miss and impossible to forget), and Zeus's answer is instant and exact: mist scattered, fog thrust away, "the sun blazed out, and the whole battle came clear." He grants the seeing. The killing he keeps on its schedule. ↩

35 lines 651–701 — The message chain. Menelaus leaves the body "much against his will," in the reluctant-lion simile repeated from 11.550-555 (our English kept — the simile that carried Ajax's retreat now carries Menelaus's errand); his charge to the defenders is the dead man's true eulogy: "let each man remember the gentleness of poor Patroclus: to every one of us he knew how to be kind, while he lived." Then the eagle-eye search, and the delivery to Antilochus — who answers with the poem's most complete silence: "For a long time speechlessness held him: his eyes filled with tears, and his strong voice was checked." He hands his armor to Laodocus and runs weeping — the fastest of the young Achaeans carrying the heaviest sentence in the poem, unarmed, "bearing the evil word." Book 18 opens on his arrival. ↩

36 lines 702–721 — Menelaus returns — the apostrophe turned on him twice in fifty lines (679, 702): the book's faithful laborer earns the second person too. Ajax's plan divides the last work: Menelaus and Meriones to carry, the two Ajaxes — "one heart in us as we share one name" — to hold the rear: the pun on Αἴαντε as a fighting unit, two men conjugated as a dual. ↩

37 lines 722–753 — The carry: the body hoisted "high and mightily"; the boar wheeling on the hounds; the fire engulfing a city ("the houses crumble in the great glare"); and then the pair of similes that turn rescue into labor and landscape — the mules dragging beam or ship-timber "down from the mountain along a rugged track... the heart worn with weariness and sweat"; the Ajaxes as "a wooded ridge... lying clean across a plain," damming the flood of powerful rivers and turning it loose over the lowland. Men as terrain: the defensive fighter's apotheosis is to become geography. ↩

38 lines 755–761 — The end: starlings and jackdaws "shrieking ruin" before the falcon — the young Achaeans in flight before Aeneas and Hector — "and much fine armor fell around and about the ditch as the Danaans fled; and there was no pause in the war." The book that fought all day for one man's body closes with armor strewn like litter and the fighting unpaused: no cadence, no rest — because the poem's next sound is Antilochus's voice in Achilles' hearing, and nothing in the war will ever be the same after it. ↩
