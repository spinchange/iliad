General Introduction

the poem, the art of repetition, the Greek text, the English line, and this edition

I. The Poem

The Iliad is not the story of the Trojan War. The war is ten years long; the poem covers fifty-one days of it, and of those days all but a handful pass in a line or two of summary. There is no Judgment of Paris in it (one glancing mention, in its last book, which ancient critics wanted to cut), no abduction of Helen, no sacrifice of Iphigenia, no wooden horse, no fall of the city. The poem assumes you know all that the way you know the weather. What it narrates is announced in its first word — mēnis, wrath: the wrath of Achilles, from the afternoon it is kindled by one man's arithmetic of honor to the night, some seven weeks later, when it has burned all the way through him and gone out, and he gives a dead body back to an old man and agrees that they should both eat dinner.

Between those two points the poem's architecture is exact. A king confiscates the prize of his best fighter, and the fighter withdraws — not merely from battle but from the whole system of honor-for-service that the prize embodied; his own goddess mother carries his grievance to Zeus, and heaven agrees to make the army lose until it learns what he was worth. That is Book 1. The debt comes due through the poem's long middle: the army is beaten back onto its ships, the king offers restitution and is refused — Achilles, given the chance to end his wrath on magnificent terms, discovers instead that he can imagine leaving the war altogether — and the fire reaches the first ship. Then the poem turns its screw: Achilles will not fight, but he lends his armor and his dearest companion, and Patroclus dies in his place, wearing his bronze, at the hands of Hector. From that point the wrath changes object. What was aimed at Agamemnon is aimed at Hector; what was a strike becomes a hunt; and the man who would not fight for honor now fights past every limit — against rivers, against decency, against the plain fact of his own oncoming death, which he knows in detail and accepts out loud. He kills Hector, defiles the body, and cannot stop; his grief will not close. It closes only in Book 24, when the gods intervene not with force but with a visit: Priam, Hector's father, crosses the plain at night, kneels to the man who killed his son, and kisses his hands. The two of them weep — each for his own dead — and then, because the poem is wiser than any of its readers, they look at each other and marvel, and eat, and sleep. The Iliad ends with a burial and a shared meal among enemies. The city will still fall; everyone in the poem knows it; the poem does not need to show it.

The subject underneath the subject is mortality. Achilles is the one figure in the poem who gets to choose — a long obscure life or a short one with unwithering fame — and the poem's cruelty and its honesty is that it lets him make the choice twice: once in the abstract, in Book 9, when he threatens to sail home and calls the choice itself into question ("The portion is equal for the man who hangs back and the man who fights hard"); and once for real, in Book 18, over Patroclus's body, with nothing abstract left anywhere. Around that single free choice the poem arranges hundreds of unfree ones — minor men, each given a name and a father and a home and a widow at the moment of death, each "broken" in the poem's flat carpenter's verb — and one great counter-figure: Hector, who has no choice at all, who fights because a city and a wife and an infant son stand behind him, and who is the only person in the poem we watch being loved. The Iliad's sympathy does not follow its politics. It is a Greek poem in which the most humane scenes belong to Troy — Helen on the wall, Hector's helmet frightening his baby, Andromache running for the ramparts "like a madwoman" — and its final line of praise is for the man its hero killed.

Above the men sit the gods, and the poem uses them with a double-entry bookkeeping that no later theology has ever forgiven it for. The gods are the audience of the war and its meddlers; they feel real grief — Zeus rains blood for Sarpedon, and says of mortals, in the poem's shortest theology, "they matter to me, even as they perish" — but they cannot die, and so nothing for them is ever finally at stake. The poem knows exactly what that means: when gods fight gods, in Book 21, it is comedy, boxed ears and a laughing father; when men fight men it is everything. Mortality is not the men's handicap. It is their seriousness. The gods watch the war the way we read the poem — with passionate partisanship and perfect safety — and the discomfort of that resemblance is intended.

II. The Art Of Repetition

The Iliad comes out of a tradition of oral composition in performance, and its style is built from repeated elements: fixed epithets ("swift-footed Achilles," "Hector of the flashing helmet"), whole recurring lines ("Then in answer spoke to him..."), repeated blocks (a message delivered in the same words it was given), and type-scenes (arming, sacrifice, supplication) whose skeletons recur with controlled variation. For a nineteenth- or twentieth-century aesthetic trained to prize variety, this was an embarrassment to be smoothed away; translators varied the epithets and paraphrased the repeated speeches so the poem would read like a novel. This translation takes the opposite view, and it is the single decision from which most of its other decisions follow: the repetition is the art. A formula, once fixed, recurs in these pages verbatim. When the Greek repeats a whole line, the English repeats its own line, word for word; when a messenger relays a speech, the relay repeats the original exactly as far as the Greek does, shifting person only where the Greek shifts person.

The reason is not fidelity for its own sake. Repetition is how this poem thinks. When five different men — Odysseus, Menelaus, Agenor, Hector, Achilles — stand alone at their five separate crossroads and ask the same line, "But why does my own heart argue these things with me?", the line is the point: the poem is showing you that the solitary man debating flight is a single recurring human situation, and the fifth occurrence carries the weight of the other four. When Achilles taunts a Trojan with "Unhappy are the fathers whose sons face my fury!" he is repeating, word for word, what Diomedes said in Book 6 — a line the audience has heard before in a scene that ended, unforgettably, in guest-friendship instead of killing; this time no one exchanges armor. When the narrator says of Patroclus's arming what he will say of Achilles' arming three books later, the identity of the lines is a fuse burning between the two scenes. Vary the wording and all of this disappears — the echoes, the ironies, the sense that the poem's world runs on recurrences the characters cannot see. Keep it, and English readers can hear, for once, the machine turning.

The bookkeeping this requires is heavy, and it is done in the open: every fixed rendering is logged in a register (translation/CONVENTIONS.md) that binds all later books; the first English wording of a repeated line is binding on every recurrence; conformance is checked mechanically, and the whole translation was audited after completion, book by book against the Greek, precisely to hunt down places where two occurrences of one Greek line had drifted apart in English. Where the register deliberately merges two Greek terms under one English word, or splits one Greek word by context, the merge is documented. The same register conventions are shared, where the poems share formulas, with a companion Odyssey built on the same principles.

III. The Greek Text

The translation follows the Oxford text of Monro and Allen, in the public-domain digitization that prints the poem at 15,693 lines, and it follows it line for line: English line 200 of a book renders Greek line 200, so that any line here can be laid beside its original — or against any commentary keyed to the standard numeration — without arithmetic. Six lines of that numeration (the block 9.458-461, and the single lines 11.543 and 14.269) are absent from the transmitted source text this project prints; they are not silently patched. The gaps are marked where they fall, and the missing lines — where antiquity preserves them, as Plutarch preserves the four suppressed lines of Phoenix's confession — are translated in the notes.

Ancient scholarship is part of this poem's transmission, and the edition keeps it visible. Lines athetized by the Alexandrian critics — marked as spurious by Zenodotus, Aristophanes, or Aristarchus — are translated wherever the printed text prints them, and the athetesis is recorded in a note, never acted on: so the divine singer stays in the dance on the Shield, the Pelian ash stays in the arming of Book 19, and the Judgment of Paris stays in Book 24 over Aristarchus's objection. Where the printed text itself makes an editorial choice against the manuscript majority, the note says so — most famously at 1.350, where this text's "boundless sea" displaces the vulgate's "wine-dark." And where a rejected ancient variant is too interesting to lose, the notes keep it: Zenodotus's text in which the corpses of the opening lines become a "feast" for the birds; his Book 19, which could not bear a seated Agamemnon and made him stand; the reading known to Strabo at 20.307 in which the rule of Aeneas's line is over "all," not over the Trojans — the variant Rome preferred, for obvious reasons. The apparatus of an ancient poem is not scaffolding to be struck; it is the record of twenty-five centuries of readers arguing about the same lines, and this translation was made in their company.

IV. The English Line

The verse is plain, dignified, modern English free verse — no meter, no rhyme — held to a loose five-to-six-beat line that runs to a median of thirteen syllables, with better than nine lines in ten falling between nine and sixteen. That is not an imitation of the hexameter; English cannot sustain one for 15,000 lines without stilts. It is a discipline with a different purpose: since the translation is line-for-line, the English line must do neither more nor less work than the Greek line it faces. It cannot pad, because the next Greek line is already waiting; it cannot sprawl, because it has to keep step for twenty-four books. What the line-for-line rule buys is worth its cost: the poem's pacing survives — the single-line death sentences land as single lines, the enjambments break where Homer broke them, the long speeches are exactly as long as Homer made them — and every sentence of the English is checkable against its original by anyone with a parallel text and a dictionary.

The diction aims at the register the Greek actually has: formal but not antique, plain but not flat. Homer's language was never anyone's daily speech — it is a poetic amalgam full of frozen archaisms — but it is rapid, direct, and unembarrassed, and the worst betrayal of it is fustian. A few English choices carry particular weight and are worth declaring at the door. The theme word mēnis is "wrath" throughout, never "rage" or "anger" — a grudge with divine standing, not a mood. Timē is always "honor"; geras always "prize," the concrete awarded object in which honor is embodied — the quarrel of Book 1 is unintelligible unless those two words stay rigid. Atē is "blindness" (capitalized when she walks as a goddess): the god-sent delusion Agamemnon pleads in his apology, "the eldest daughter of Zeus, who blinds all men." The enslaved of the poem are called slaves. Where the Greek says dmōs or dmōē this translation does not say "servant" or "maid," because the difference between a servant and a slave is the difference Briseis and Andromache spend the poem on the wrong side of, and a translation that softens the word has taken a side. And the small change of the formulas is kept literal where literalness still works: words are winged; dawn is rosy-fingered and saffron-robed; men glare darkly before they speak; the day of death is "the pitiless day."

Proper names use the familiar Latinized forms — Achilles, Ajax, Hecuba, Clytemnestra — on the principle that this poem belongs to readers, not to systems of transliteration; the register keeps the inventory, down to the diaeresis in Eëtion.

V. This Edition

Each of the twenty-four books is presented the same way: a one-sentence epigraph; a translator's commentary — an essay on what the book is and does, where its difficulties lie, and what this translation did about them; the verse itself, numbered to the Greek; and endnotes, marked in the text by bracketed numbers that restart with each book. The notes carry four kinds of freight: textual matters (atheteses, variants, disputed lines); formulas, flagged at their first occurrence in the whole poem and then trusted to the reader's ear; realia — who this minor king is, where that river runs, why a boar's-tusk helmet matters; and the occasional confession, where the English had to choose one horn of an ambiguity and the note preserves the other. A project file of disputed lines and a full formula register sit beside the translation for anyone who wants the workshop as well as the work. After completion, every book was re-read entire against the Greek, line against line, and the handful of places where the translation had drifted from its own rules were repaired; the record of that audit, too, is preserved.

A reader who wants a map before setting out can hold the poem's twenty-four books in one hand. Book 1: the quarrel, the plague, and Thetis's bargain with Zeus. Book 2: the testing of the army and the great catalogue of ships. Books 3-7: the war seen whole, as if for the first time — the duel of Paris and Menelaus, Helen naming the kings from the wall, the truce broken, Diomedes' rampage among gods and men, and, at the center, Hector in Troy with Hecuba, Helen, and Andromache — the poem's one visit inside the doomed city at peace. Book 8: Zeus bans the gods from the field and tilts the day to Troy. Book 9: the embassy — Agamemnon's gifts, and Achilles' refusal, the greatest speech in the poem. Book 10: the night raid. Books 11-15: the long third day of battle — the captains wounded one by one, the wall breached, Hera seducing Zeus on Ida, the tide turned twice, until Hector's hand is on the first ship. Book 16: Patroclus, in Achilles' armor, saves the ships, kills Sarpedon, and dies under the walls. Book 17: the fight over his body. Book 18: the news; Achilles' choice; Thetis; the making of the Shield, on which Hephaestus forges the whole living world the poem otherwise excludes. Book 19: the wrath against Agamemnon renounced; Blindness anatomized; the horse Xanthus prophesies. Books 20-21: Achilles returned — against Aeneas, against everyone, against the river itself, with the gods loosed into the battle and finally laughing in each other's faces. Book 22: Hector alone before the gates; the chase; the death; Andromache at the loom. Book 23: the funeral of Patroclus and the games — the poem already practicing peace. Book 24: the gods' verdict, Priam's night journey, the ransom of Hector, and the twelve days of truce in which the poem, having begun with two men quarreling over what a life is worth, ends with two enemies agreeing on what a death is owed.

Read it aloud where you can. The poem was made for the ear, and its repetitions — the epithets that toll like bells, the lines that return in new mouths, the pitiless day arriving on schedule — were composed to be heard coming. This translation has tried to keep every one of them audible.
