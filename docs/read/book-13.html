Book XIII

Poseidon's defiance: Zeus looks away, the sea-god slips into the battle in disguise, and the line holds — shield on shield, man on man.

Translator’s note on Book 13

Book 13 begins with a look and ends with a noise. Zeus, satisfied, turns his shining eyes away from Troy toward the far pastoral north — the mare-milkers and the Abioi, "most righteous of men" (note 1) — certain no god will dare interfere; Poseidon, watching from the peak of Samothrace, comes down in four strides, and the whole book runs on his stolen presence: a god working the Achaean line in borrowed shapes and voices, "in secret, always, up and down the army" — because Zeus is elder-born and knows more (note 15). The last line closes the frame: the clamor of both armies "reached the ether and the radiance of Zeus" — the battle he refused to watch arrives as sound (note 37).

Its centerpiece is the poem's one aristeia of a middle-aged man: Idomeneus of Crete, "half-grey," met coming back from a comrade's sickbed, talked into glory by a god, and given the book's most famous set-piece before he throws a spear — the anatomy of courage and cowardice in ambush, where the coward's color runs and his teeth chatter and the brave man's color never changes (note 12). His kills are the book's plot: Othryoneus, who bid for Cassandra with a promise instead of gifts (note 16); Asius, paying the death-notice filed in Book 12; and Alcathous, whom Poseidon freezes where he stands — the god holding the victim for the blow, the most terrible divine intervention in the poem (note 19).

Around the aristeia the book builds the day's resistance out of lists and low things: the locked phalanx where "shield pressed on shield, helmet on helmet, man on man" (note 6); the defense roster with its Ionians (nowhere else in Homer) and its exiled homicide Medon; the Locrians who fight shoeless of honor, with bows and wool slings, unseen from behind (note 31); beans jumping from a winnowing-shovel; oxen sweating at one plow; a roped bull; a worm. Where Book 12 measured war by peace, Book 13 arms peace and sends it back in. Two wounds are planted for later books: Ascalaphus lies dead and his father Ares, penned on Olympus by Zeus's command, does not know it (note 23); and Harpalion's father walks weeping behind the bier — Pylaemenes, whom the poem killed in Book 5: the most famous inconsistency in Homer, which ancient critics solved with an athetesis and this translation keeps in the open (note 27).

1Now Zeus, when he had brought the Trojans and Hector to the ships,

2left them beside them, to bear the toil and the misery

3without respite; and he himself turned his shining eyes

4away, gazing far off at the land of the horse-herding Thracians,

5and the close-fighting Mysians, and the proud Mare-milkers

6who drink the milk, and the Abioi, most righteous of men.1

7To Troy he no longer turned his shining eyes at all,

8for he did not expect in his heart that any immortal

9would come to stand by either Trojans or Danaans.

10But the lord earth-shaker kept no blind man's watch:

11he sat marveling at the war and the fighting,

12high on the topmost peak of wooded Samos,

13Thracian Samos; for from there all Ida showed clear,

14and Priam's city showed clear, and the ships of the Achaeans.

15There he sat, come up out of the sea, and he pitied the Achaeans

16broken under the Trojans; and against Zeus his anger was strong.

17At once he came down from the rugged mountain,

18striding fast on his feet; and the tall hills trembled, and the timber,

19under the immortal feet of Poseidon as he went.2

20Three strides he made, and with the fourth he reached his goal,

21Aegae, where his famous house in the depths of the water,

22golden, glittering, is built for him, imperishable forever.

23There he came, and yoked beneath his chariot the bronze-hoofed horses,

24swift-flying, with manes streaming gold,

25and clothed himself in gold, and took the whip,

26gold, well-wrought, and mounted the chariot,

27and drove out over the waves. And the sea-beasts gamboled beneath him,

28coming from every hollow, and did not fail to know their lord;

29and the sea in its joy stood apart; and the horses flew on

30lightly, and the bronze axle beneath was not even wetted;3

31and the bounding horses carried him toward the ships of the Achaeans.

32There is a broad cave in the depths of the deep water,

33midway between Tenedos and rugged Imbros:

34there Poseidon the earth-shaker stayed his horses,

35loosing them from the chariot, and threw down ambrosial fodder

36for them to eat; and around their feet he cast golden hobbles,

37unbreakable, not to be undone, so they would stay there unmoved

38for their lord's return; and he went to the army of the Achaeans.

39And the Trojans, massed like flame or like a stormwind,

40followed Hector, Priam's son, in insatiable fury,

41roaring, crying as one; and they hoped to take the ships

42of the Achaeans, and kill all their best men beside them.

43But Poseidon, holder of the earth, the earth-shaker,

44come up out of the deep water, was rousing the Argives,

45likened to Calchas in body and tireless voice.

46First he spoke to the two Ajaxes, eager as they already were:

47"You two Ajaxes — you will save the army of the Achaeans

48if you remember your courage, and no shiver of panic.

49Elsewhere I do not fear the invincible hands

50of the Trojans who have climbed our great wall in a mass:

51the strong-greaved Achaeans will hold all of them.

52But here I fear most terribly that we suffer something —

53here where that rabid one leads them, like a flame,

54Hector, who claims to be a son of mighty Zeus.

55May some god put it into the hearts of you two

56to stand hard yourselves, and to command the others:

57then you might beat him back from the swift-faring ships

58for all his fury, even if the Olympian himself is driving him."

59He spoke, and the holder of the earth, the earth-shaker,

60striking both of them with his staff, filled them with strong fury,

61and made their limbs light, their feet and their hands above.

62And he himself rose to fly like a swift-winged hawk

63that lifts from a sheer towering rock-face

64and swoops to chase some other bird across the plain:

65so Poseidon the earth-shaker darted away from them.

66Of the two, swift Ajax, Oïleus' son, knew him first,

67and at once he spoke to Ajax, Telamon's son:

68"Ajax — since some one of the gods who hold Olympus,

69likened to the seer, tells us to fight beside the ships —

70and that is not Calchas, the reader of gods' bird-signs:

71the trace of his feet and legs from behind, as he went off,

72I knew it easily — the gods are easy to know —4

73now the heart in my own chest, for its part,

74is set far more on warring and fighting,

75and my feet beneath are hungry for it, and my hands above."

76Answering him spoke Telamonian Ajax:

77"So with me: my invincible hands about the spear

78are hungry, and my fury has risen, and beneath me both

79my feet are driving; and I long — even alone —

80to fight Hector, Priam's son, in his insatiable fury."

81So the two of them spoke to one another,

82in the joy of battle-craving the god had cast into their hearts;

83and meanwhile the holder of the earth roused the Achaeans behind them,

84who were cooling their hearts back by the swift ships.

85Their limbs were unstrung with grievous weariness,

86and grief was rising in their hearts as they watched

87the Trojans, who had climbed the great wall in a mass:

88looking at them, they let the tears spill under their brows,

89for they did not think they would escape the evil. But the earth-shaker,

90passing easily among them, stirred the strong battle-lines.

91To Teucer first he came, and Leïtus, commanding,

92and the hero Peneleos, and Thoas, and Deïpyrus,

93and Meriones and Antilochus, masters of the war-shout;

94urging these on, he spoke winged words:

95"Shame, Argives — young men! It was you I trusted:

96fight, and you will save our ships.

97But if you slacken from the misery of this war,

98now dawns the day we are broken under the Trojans.

99Shame! — a great marvel is this I see with my eyes,

100a terrible thing I never once said would come to pass:

101the Trojans advancing on our ships — the Trojans, who before

102were like skittering deer, which through the timber,

103feed for jackals and leopards and wolves,

104wander witless and weaponless, with no fight in them:

105so before now the Trojans would not stand against

106the fury and the hands of the Achaeans — not for a moment.

107Now, far from their city, they are fighting at the hollow ships,

108through a leader's failing and the slackness of the army,

109who quarrel with him, and will not defend

110the swift-faring ships, but are killed among them.

111Yet even if the truth is that the man wholly to blame

112is the hero, Atreus' son, wide-ruling Agamemnon,

113because he dishonored the swift-footed son of Peleus,

114we, for our part, have no leave to slacken from the war.

115No: let us heal it, quickly. The minds of good men can be healed.5

116It is no longer honorable, this slackening of your rushing courage,

117you who are all of you the best men in the army. I would not

118quarrel with any man who hung back from the fighting

119because he was worthless; but with you my heart is angry to the core.

120Soft men! Soon you will make some greater evil yet

121by this hanging back. Let each of you put in his heart

122shame, and the fear of blame: a great struggle has risen.

123Hector is fighting at the ships now, good at the war-cry,

124in his power, and he has broken the gates and the long bar."

125So the holder of the earth, commanding, roused the Achaeans.

126And around the two Ajaxes the battle-lines took their stand,

127strong lines: Ares himself could not have come and faulted them,

128nor Athena, driver of armies. For the chosen best

129stood and awaited the Trojans and brilliant Hector,

130locking spear on spear, shield on layered shield:

131buckler pressed on buckler, helmet on helmet, man on man,

132and the horsehair crests on the bright ridges touched

133as they nodded, so close they stood to one another;6

134and the spears, shaken in daring hands,

135crossed over one another; and their minds were set straight ahead, hot to fight.

136The Trojans struck first, massed; Hector led them,

137straining straight on, like a boulder off a cliff-face

138that a river swollen with winter shoves down the ledge,

139breaking with prodigious rain the shameless rock's moorings;

140bounding high it flies, and the timber crashes

141under it; and it runs on unswerving, steady, until it reaches

142the level plain — and there it rolls no more, for all its rush:7

143so Hector for a while threatened to sweep clean to the sea,

144clean through the huts and ships of the Achaeans,

145killing; but when he ran against those close-set battle-lines,

146he stopped, jammed hard against them. And the sons of the Achaeans facing him,

147stabbing with swords and double-curved spears,

148thrust him away from them; and he shuddered and gave ground.

149He cried a piercing cry, to carry to the Trojans:

150"Trojans and Lycians and Dardanians who fight at close quarters —

151stand by me! Not for long will the Achaeans hold me,

152not though they have built themselves into a tower,

153no — I think they will give way under my spear, if truly

154the best of gods drove me on, the loud-thunderer, Hera's lord."

155So speaking he stirred the fury and the heart of every man.

156And Deiphobus strode among them thinking great things,

157Priam's son, holding before him his shield, balanced every way,

158stepping light on his feet, advancing under the shield's cover.

159And Meriones aimed at him with his shining spear

160and threw, and did not miss, striking the shield, balanced every way,

161the bull's-hide shield — but drove nowhere through it: well before that

162the long spear snapped at the socket. And Deiphobus

163held the bull's-hide shield away from him, and his heart feared

164the spear of war-minded Meriones; but that hero

165drew back into the band of his companions, angry to the bone

166both ways — for the lost victory, and the spear he had shattered —

167and set off along the huts and ships of the Achaeans

168to fetch a long spear that was left him in his hut.

169The others fought on, and the quenchless roar rose.

170Teucer, Telamon's son, was first to kill his man,

171the spearman Imbrius, son of Mentor rich in horses.

172He lived in Pedaeum, before the sons of the Achaeans came,

173and had for wife a bastard daughter of Priam, Medesicaste;

174but when the double-ended ships of the Danaans came,

175he came back to Ilion, and stood out among the Trojans,

176and lived with Priam, who honored him like his own children.

177Him the son of Telamon stabbed under the ear with the long spear,

178and wrenched the spear out; and he fell like an ash tree

179that on the crest of a mountain seen clear from every side

180is cut down by the bronze, and brings its soft leaves to the ground:

181so he fell, and his armor, intricate with bronze, clashed about him.

182And Teucer rushed in, hot to strip the armor off him;

183but as he rushed, Hector cast at him with his shining spear.

184He saw it coming and dodged the bronze spear —

185barely; and Hector struck Amphimachus, son of Cteatus

186of Actor's line, with the spear in the chest as he came into the battle:

187he fell with a thud, and his armor clattered upon him.

188And Hector rushed in to tear the helmet, close-fitted

189at the temples, from the head of great-hearted Amphimachus;

190but as he rushed, Ajax lunged at him with the shining spear —

191yet nowhere reached the flesh, for all of him was sheathed

192in terrible bronze. He stabbed the boss of the shield instead

193and shoved him back with great strength; and Hector gave ground

194behind the two corpses, and the Achaeans dragged them clear.

195Amphimachus, then, Stichius and brilliant Menestheus,

196captains of the Athenians, carried back among the Achaean host;

197and Imbrius the two Ajaxes, hot with rushing courage,

198as two lions snatch a goat from under the guard

199of sharp-toothed dogs and carry it through the thick brush,

200holding it high above the ground in their jaws:

201so the two Ajaxes, helmed for war, holding him high,

202stripped his armor; and the head from the soft neck

203Oïleus' son cut off, in wrath for Amphimachus,

204and swung and sent it rolling like a ball through the crowd;

205and it fell in the dust in front of Hector's feet.8

206And then it was that Poseidon was angered to the heart,

207his grandson fallen in the dread combat,9

208and he set off along the huts and ships of the Achaeans

209to rouse the Danaans; and he was building sorrows for the Trojans.

210And Idomeneus, famed with the spear, met him

211coming from a companion who had just come out of the fighting,

212cut behind the knee by the sharp bronze.

213His comrades carried him in; and Idomeneus, giving the healers

214their orders, was going to his hut, for he still longed

215to face the war. And the lord earth-shaker spoke to him,

216likening his voice to Thoas, Andraemon's son,

217who in all Pleuron and steep Calydon

218ruled the Aetolians, honored like a god by the people:

219"Idomeneus, counselor of the Cretans, where have they gone,

220the threats the sons of the Achaeans threatened against the Trojans?"

221And Idomeneus, leader of the Cretans, answered him:

222"Thoas, no man is at fault now — not so far

223as I can tell; for all of us know how to fight.

224No one is gripped by bloodless fear, no one yields

225to shirking and ducks out of this evil war. No — this, it seems,

226must be the pleasure of the overmastering son of Cronos:

227that the Achaeans perish here, nameless, far from Argos.

228But Thoas — you were always staunch before,

229and you rouse any other man you see slackening:

230so do not leave off now. Call to every man."

231Then answered him Poseidon the earth-shaker:

232"Idomeneus, may the man never come home

233from Troy — may he be sport for dogs on this ground —

234who on this day chooses to slacken from the fighting.

235Come then, take up your weapons and come out with me. We must

236press this together, and see if the two of us can be of use.

237Even sorry men, combined, come to some courage —10

238and the two of us would know how to fight even the best."

239So speaking, the god went back into the toil of men;

240and Idomeneus, when he reached his well-built hut,

241put his fine armor about his body, caught up two spears,

242and went his way like the lightning-bolt the son of Cronos

243takes in his hand and brandishes from glittering Olympus,

244showing a sign to mortals, and its flashes blaze far:

245so the bronze blazed on his chest as he ran.

246And Meriones, his brave aide, met him

247while he was still near the hut — he was going to fetch

248a bronze spear — and the strength of Idomeneus spoke to him:

249"Meriones, Molus's son, swift-footed, dearest of my companions,11

250why have you come, leaving the war and the combat?

251Are you hit somewhere — is an arrowhead wearing you down?

252Or do you come after me with some message? I myself

253have no wish to sit in the huts, but to fight."

254And level-headed Meriones answered him:

255"Idomeneus, counselor of the bronze-shirted Cretans,

256I come to fetch a spear, if one is left

257in your huts; the one I carried before, we shattered it —

258I struck the shield of Deiphobus in his arrogance."

259And Idomeneus, leader of the Cretans, answered him:

260"Spears — if you want them you will find one, and twenty,

261standing in my hut against the bright entrance-wall:

262Trojan spears, which I strip from the men I kill. I hold12

263it is not my way to fight standing far from enemy men;

264and so I have spears, and bossed shields,

265and helmets, and corslets shining bright."

266And level-headed Meriones answered him:

267"I too, by my hut and my black ship, have plenty

268of Trojan spoils — but they are not close by for the taking.

269For I say I am no more forgetful of courage than you:

270I take my stand among the first in the battle where men win glory

271whenever the strife of war has risen.

272Some other of the bronze-shirted Achaeans might miss

273my fighting; but you — you are the one who knows it, I think."

274And Idomeneus, leader of the Cretans, answered him:

275"I know your worth, and what kind of man you are. Why recite it?

276If now beside the ships all our best were being counted

277for an ambush — where the worth of men shows clearest,

278where the coward and the brave man stand revealed:

279the coward's color changes to one thing and another,

280and the spirit in his chest cannot hold him steady in his seat,

281but he shifts his crouch, sits back from foot to foot,

282and the heart in his ribs hammers hugely

283as he imagines the death-spirits, and his teeth chatter aloud;

284but the brave man's color never changes, and no fear in him

285is too great, once he has settled into the ambush of men,

286and he prays to be mixed at once in the misery of battle —

287there, no man could fault your fury or your hands.

288For even if you were hit in the work, or struck close in,

289the missile would not fall on the nape of your neck, or your back:

290it would meet your chest or your belly

291as you pressed forward into the front-fighters' trysting-place.13

292But come, no more standing here talking of this

293like children — someone might scoff beyond all measure.

294Go to the hut, and choose yourself a massive spear."

295So he spoke; and Meriones, the equal of swift Ares,

296quickly took up a bronze spear from the hut

297and went after Idomeneus, his whole mind on war.

298As Ares, bane of mortals, goes out to war,

299and with him follows Panic, his dear son, strong and fearless,

300who sends even the patient-hearted warrior running;

301the two of them arm out of Thrace against the Ephyri

302or the great-hearted Phlegyes — and they do not hear14

303both sides' prayers, but hand the glory to one:

304such were Meriones and Idomeneus, leaders of men,

305as they went into the war, helmed in blazing bronze.

306And Meriones spoke to him first with his word:

307"Son of Deucalion, where are you eager to enter the crowd?

308On the right of the whole army, or through the middle,

309or on the left? For nowhere else, I think, do the long-haired

310Achaeans so fail of hands in the fighting."

311And Idomeneus, leader of the Cretans, answered him:

312"In the center ships there are others to hold the defense,

313the two Ajaxes, and Teucer, who is best of the Achaeans

314in bowmanship, and good too in the standing fight:

315they will drive him to sickness of it, however hot for battle —

316Hector, Priam's son — however strong he is.

317It will be steep going for him, blaze as he may to fight:

318against their conquering fury and invincible hands

319he will not fire the ships — unless the son of Cronos in person

320hurls a burning brand onto the swift ships.

321To no man would he yield, great Telamonian Ajax —

322to no man who is mortal and eats the grain of Demeter,

323who can be broken by bronze or by great hurled stones.

324Not even to Achilles, breaker of men, would he give way —

325in the standing fight; in speed of foot there is no contesting him.

326So take us leftward of the army, and quickly: we will see

327whether we give the triumph to some man, or he to us."

328So he spoke; and Meriones, the equal of swift Ares,

329led on, until they came to the army where he had bidden.

330And when they saw Idomeneus, strong as a flame,

331him and his aide, in their intricate war-gear,

332they called to one another through the crowd and made for him,

333and their struggle joined, one mass, by the sterns of the ships.

334As when squalls come racing under shrill winds

335on a day when the dust lies thickest on the roads,

336and all together they raise up a great cloud of dust:

337so their battle came together in one mass, and they were hot

338in their hearts to slaughter each other in the crowd with the sharp bronze.

339And the man-destroying battle bristled with the long

340flesh-cutting spears they gripped; and eyes were blinded

341by the flare of bronze off glittering helmets

342and fresh-burnished corslets and shining shields

343as they came on in one mass. It would be a hard-hearted man

344who could rejoice at the sight of that toil, and not be grieved.15

345And the two strong sons of Cronos, divided in purpose,

346were building grim sorrows for the fighting men.

347Zeus willed the victory for the Trojans and Hector,

348glorifying swift-footed Achilles; yet he had no wish

349for the Achaean army to perish wholly before Ilion —

350he was glorifying Thetis, and her strong-hearted son.

351But Poseidon had come among the Argives and was stirring them,

352slipped up in secret from the grey salt water; for it galled him

353to see them broken by Trojans, and against Zeus his anger was strong.

354Both, to be sure, were of one stock and one parentage,

355but Zeus was elder-born, and he knew more.

356For that reason Poseidon shunned defending them openly,

357and in secret, always, roused them down the army, likened to a man.

358So the two of them knotted the cord of strong strife

359and leveling war, and drew it taut over both sides —

360a cord unbreakable, not to be untied; and it unstrung the knees of many.16

361Then — though his hair was half-grey — Idomeneus,

362calling on the Danaans, leapt on the Trojans and drove panic among them.

363For he killed Othryoneus, in the town from Cabesus —

364lately come, chasing the report of the war,

365and he had asked for the loveliest of Priam's daughters,

366Cassandra, without bride-gifts — promising a great work instead:

367to drive the sons of the Achaeans out of Troy against their will.

368And old Priam had promised her, had bowed his head:

369give her he would; and Othryoneus fought trusting the promises.

370Idomeneus aimed at him with his shining spear

371and threw, and hit him in mid-stride; the corslet of bronze

372he wore did not hold: the spear fixed in his belly's middle.

373He fell with a thud; and Idomeneus exulted, calling:

374"Othryoneus — I congratulate you beyond all living men

375if you are really going to accomplish all you promised

376Priam, Dardanus' heir! He promised you his daughter.

377Look — we too could promise you as much, and keep it:

378we would give you the loveliest of the daughters of Atreus' son,

379bringing her out of Argos for you to marry, if you will join us

380and sack the well-peopled city of Ilion.

381Come along: let us settle the marriage at the seafaring ships —

382you will find we are not hard men over bride-terms."17

383So speaking the hero Idomeneus dragged him by the foot

384through the strong encounter. But Asius came to avenge him,

385on foot before his horses; the aide who drove them

386kept them breathing at his shoulders always. And his heart was hot

387to hit Idomeneus; but the other was quicker, and struck him with the spear

388in the throat under the chin, and drove the bronze clean through.

389He fell as an oak falls, or a white poplar,

390or a towering pine, that carpenters up in the mountains

391cut down with fresh-whetted axes to be a ship's timber:18

392so he lay stretched out before his horses and chariot,

393bellowing, clawing at the blood-soaked dust.

394And the charioteer was struck out of the wits he had:

395he did not dare wheel the horses round and slip

396out of the enemy's hands; and Antilochus, staunch in the fight,

397caught him with the spear mid-body; the corslet of bronze

398he wore did not hold: the spear fixed in his belly's middle,

399and he fell gasping out of the well-built chariot.

400And the horses Antilochus, great-hearted Nestor's son,

401drove off from the Trojans to the strong-greaved Achaeans.

402Then Deiphobus came up very close to Idomeneus,

403grieving for Asius, and cast with his shining spear.

404But Idomeneus saw it coming and dodged the bronze spear:

405he hid himself behind his shield, balanced every way,

406the shield he carried, built of oxhides and gleaming bronze

407rounded on the wheel, fitted with two cross-rods:

408under it he gathered himself whole, and the bronze spear flew over

409and the shield rang dry and harsh as the spear grazed

410across it. Yet Deiphobus's heavy hand had not thrown in vain:

411he hit Hypsenor, Hippasus's son, shepherd of the people,

412in the liver below the midriff, and unstrung his knees on the instant.

413And Deiphobus vaunted terribly over him, crying far:

414"Not unavenged, after all, lies Asius! No — I say

415that even going down to Hades, the strong closer of the gate,

416he will be glad at heart: I have given him an escort for the road."19

417So he spoke, and grief came over the Argives at his vaunting,

418and most of all he stirred the heart of war-minded Antilochus;

419yet grieved as he was, he did not forget his companion:

420running, he stood over him and covered him with the shield,

421and two loyal companions stooped beneath him,

422Mecisteus, Echius' son, and brilliant Alastor,

423and carried him, groaning heavily, to the hollow ships.

424And Idomeneus did not slacken his great fury: he strained

425always either to wrap some Trojan in blinding night

426or to fall himself with a crash, warding ruin from the Achaeans.

427Then the dear son of Zeus-nurtured Aesyetes,

428the hero Alcathous — he was Anchises' son-in-law,

429married to the eldest of his daughters, Hippodameia,

430whom her father and her lady mother loved with all their hearts

431at home; for she outshone all the girls of her age

432in beauty and in handiwork and in wit, and for that

433the best man in the breadth of Troy had married her —

434him, then, Poseidon broke under Idomeneus's hand:

435he charmed his shining eyes and shackled his glorious limbs,20

436so that he could neither run back nor swerve aside,

437but stood unmoving, like a grave-pillar or a tree

438in high leaf, while the hero Idomeneus stabbed him with the spear

439mid-chest, and burst the bronze tunic around it,

440the tunic that till then had kept ruin from his flesh:

441it screamed dry and harsh as it tore around the spear.

442He fell with a thud, the spear stuck fast in his heart,

443and the heart, still beating, shook even the butt-end

444of the spear, quivering — until at last massive Ares stilled its fury.

445And Idomeneus vaunted terribly over him, crying far:

446"Deiphobus! Shall we call it a fair reckoning now —

447three men killed for one? — since it was you who boasted.

448Strange man: come stand against me yourself, face to face,

449and learn what son of Zeus's line has come here:

450Zeus first begot Minos, warden of Crete;

451and Minos got a son, blameless Deucalion;

452and Deucalion fathered me, lord over many men

453in wide Crete; and now the ships have carried me here,

454an evil to you, and your father, and the rest of the Trojans."21

455So he spoke; and Deiphobus was torn two ways —

456whether to fall back and take as companion some one

457of the great-hearted Trojans, or to try it alone.

458And as he weighed it, this seemed to him the better course:

459to go for Aeneas. He found him standing rearmost

460of the crowd; for he was forever furious at brilliant Priam,

461because, brave among men as he was, Priam gave him no honor.22

462Standing close, he spoke winged words to him:

463"Aeneas, counselor of the Trojans, now the great need is on you

464to stand for your sister's husband — if kinship's grief can reach you.

465Come, let us fight for Alcathous, who long ago,

466as your sister's husband, reared you in his house when you were little;

467and Idomeneus, famed with the spear, has stripped him."

468So he spoke, and stirred the heart in Aeneas's chest,

469and he went after Idomeneus, his whole mind on war.

470But no panic took hold of Idomeneus, as though he were some pampered boy:

471he stood his ground, like a boar in the mountains sure of his strength,

472who abides the great oncoming rabble of men

473in a lonely place, and the ridge of his back bristles up,

474and his eyes shine with fire, and he whets his tusks,

475hot to beat off the dogs and the men:

476so Idomeneus, famed with the spear, stood fast and did not yield

477to Aeneas coming swift to the rescue. But he called his companions,

478looking to Ascalaphus and Aphareus and Deïpyrus

479and Meriones and Antilochus, masters of the war-shout;

480urging these on, he spoke winged words:

481"Here, friends — I am alone: defend me! I dread him terribly,

482swift-footed Aeneas, who is coming at me —

483who is very strong in battle at killing men,

484and has the flower of youth besides, which is the greatest power.

485If we were of one age, with the heart I carry now,

486quickly would he carry off great power — or I would."

487So he spoke; and all of them, one spirit in their chests,

488took their stand close by him, shields leaned against their shoulders.

489And Aeneas on the other side called to his companions,

490looking to Deiphobus and Paris and brilliant Agenor,

491fellow-leaders of the Trojans with him; and after them

492the troops followed, as flocks follow the bell-ram

493down from the pasture to drink — and the shepherd's heart is glad:23

494so the heart in Aeneas's chest rejoiced

495when he saw the mass of troops following his own lead.

496And around Alcathous they went at each other, close in,

497with their long pikes; and the bronze about their chests

498rang terribly as they aimed at one another

499through the crowd. And two men of war beyond the others,

500Aeneas and Idomeneus, the equals of Ares,

501strained to hew each other's flesh with the pitiless bronze.

502Aeneas first cast at Idomeneus;

503but he saw it coming and dodged the bronze spear,

504and the point of Aeneas's spear went quivering

505into the earth, hurled in vain from his massive hand.

506But Idomeneus struck Oenomaus mid-belly,

507and burst the plate of his corslet, and the bronze

508spilled his bowels through; and falling in the dust he clutched the earth in his palm.

509And Idomeneus wrenched the long-shadowed spear out

510of the corpse, but could not strip the rest of the fine armor

511from his shoulders: the missiles pressed him too hard.

512For the joints of his feet no longer held firm in the charge —

513no spring left, either to follow his own cast or to dodge another's.

514So in the standing fight he still fended off the pitiless day,

515but in retreat his feet could no longer carry him quick out of the battle.

516And as he was stepping slowly back, Deiphobus cast at him

517with his shining spear — for the grudge he held him was relentless, always.

518But this time too he missed; and the spear hit Ascalaphus,

519the son of Enyalius; through the shoulder the massive spear

520drove; and falling in the dust he clutched the earth in his palm.

521But loud-roaring massive Ares knew nothing of it yet —

522that his son had fallen in the strong encounter —

523for he sat on the peak of Olympus, under the golden clouds,

524penned there by the designs of Zeus, where the other

525immortal gods sat too, barred from the war.24

526And around Ascalaphus they went at each other close in:

527Deiphobus tore the shining helmet from Ascalaphus,

528but Meriones, the equal of swift Ares, leaping in,

529stabbed his upper arm with the spear, and out of his hand

530the socketed helmet fell ringing to the ground.

531And Meriones, springing in again like a vulture,

532wrenched the massive spear out of his upper arm

533and drew back into the band of his companions. And Polites,

534Deiphobus's own brother, throwing his arms around his waist,

535led him out of the harsh-sounding war, until they reached

536his swift horses, which stood behind the battle and the fighting

537with their charioteer and the intricate chariot;

538these carried him toward the city, groaning heavily,

539worn; and the blood ran down from the fresh wound on his arm.

540The others fought on, and the quenchless roar rose.

541There Aeneas sprang on Aphareus, Caletor's son,

542and struck his throat — it was turned toward him — with the sharp spear;

543his head dropped to one side, and shield and helmet

544crumpled down on him, and death that breaks the spirit poured round him.

545And Antilochus, watching Thoon as he turned around,

546leapt in and stabbed him, and severed the whole vein

547that runs up the length of the back to reach the neck:

548the whole of it he severed, and the man dropped on his back in the dust,

549stretching both hands out toward his dear companions.

550And Antilochus sprang on him and set to stripping the armor

551from his shoulders, watching all round; for the Trojans, ringing him,

552stabbed at his broad glittering shield from this side and that, yet could not

553get through to graze the soft flesh behind it with the pitiless bronze —

554the flesh of Antilochus; for Poseidon the earth-shaker

555guarded Nestor's son on every side, even amid the thick missiles.

556For never was he out of reach of enemies; he wheeled

557among them; and his spear was never held still, but always

558shaken, quivering; and his aim was set in his mind

559either to cast at someone, or to charge in close.

560But as he aimed through the crowd he did not go unseen

561by Adamas, Asius's son, who leapt in close and stabbed

562the middle of his shield with the sharp bronze; but the blue-maned

563Poseidon made the point strengthless, grudging him that life.25

564And half of it stuck there, like a fire-hardened stake,

565in the shield of Antilochus, and half lay on the ground;

566and Adamas drew back into the band of his companions, dodging doom.

567But Meriones, following as he went, threw the spear

568and hit him between the genitals and the navel — there

569where the war-god comes most grievous to wretched mortals.

570There the spear stuck fast; and doubled over the shaft

571he writhed, as a bull writhes that herdsmen in the mountains

572have bound with willow-ropes and drag along unwilling:

573so, stabbed, he writhed a little while — not long —

574until the hero Meriones came close and wrenched

575the spear out of his flesh; and darkness covered his eyes.

576And Helenus struck Deïpyrus close in, on the temple,

577with a great Thracian sword, and sheared the helmet away;

578it was knocked spinning to the ground, and some Achaean

579picked it up as it rolled between the fighters' feet;

580and gloomy night came down and covered his eyes.

581Then grief seized Atreus' son, Menelaus good at the war-cry,

582and he strode out threatening against the hero, lord Helenus,

583shaking his sharp spear; and Helenus drew back the grip of his bow.

584So the two came on together, the one hot to cast

585the sharp-pointed spear, the other the arrow off the string.

586Priam's son struck him first, with the arrow, on the chest,

587on the corslet's plate — and the bitter arrow sprang off.

588As off a broad winnowing-shovel, on a great threshing-floor,

589the dark-skinned beans or the chickpeas jump

590under the shrill wind and the winnower's swing,

591so from the corslet of glorious Menelaus

592the bitter arrow glanced far off, turned hard away.26

593But Atreus' son, Menelaus good at the war-cry, struck

594the hand in which he held the polished bow: and into the bow

595the bronze spear drove, clean through the hand.

596He drew back into the band of his companions, dodging doom,

597his hand hanging slack at his side, dragging the ash spear.

598Great-hearted Agenor drew it out of the hand,

599and bound the hand itself in a sling of twisted

600sheep's wool, which his aide carried for the shepherd of the people.27

601Then Peisander came straight at glorious Menelaus —

602but an evil fate was leading him toward death's end:

603toward you, Menelaus, to be broken in the dread combat.

604And when they were close, advancing on one another,

605Atreus' son missed his cast; the spear was turned aside;

606and Peisander stabbed at the shield of glorious Menelaus

607but could not drive the bronze on through:

608the broad shield held it, and the spear snapped

609at the socket. Yet he rejoiced in his heart, and hoped for victory.

610But Atreus' son, drawing his silver-studded sword,

611sprang at Peisander; and he, from under his shield, pulled out

612a fine axe of good bronze on a haft of olive-wood,

613long and well-polished; and in the same instant they reached each other.

614Peisander chopped at the ridge of the horsehair-crested helmet

615at its very peak, under the plume; Menelaus caught him coming on

616at the forehead, above the base of the nose: the bones cracked loud,

617and his eyes, all blood, dropped in the dust by his feet,

618and he doubled over and fell. And setting a heel on his chest

619Menelaus stripped his armor, and spoke in triumph over him:

620"This is how you will leave the ships of the swift-colted Danaans —

621you Trojans past all pride, never sated with the terrible war-shout —

622nor short of any other outrage and disgrace,

623like the outrage you did to me, you foul dogs, with never a fear

624in your hearts for the harsh wrath of loud-thundering Zeus

625of Guest-right, who one day will destroy your steep city.

626You who went off — wantonly — carrying away my wedded wife

627and much of my wealth, when it was she who had made you welcome;

628and now you rage to hurl annihilating fire

629on the seafaring ships, and to kill the Achaean heroes.

630But you will be stopped, somewhere, war-hungry as you are.

631O father Zeus — they say you stand above all others

632in wisdom, men and gods alike; yet all this comes from you:

633what favor you are showing to men of violence —

634these Trojans, whose fury is forever reckless, who cannot

635get their fill of the din of leveling war!

636Of all things there is satiety — of sleep, of love,

637of sweet song and of blameless dancing;

638a man would sooner pray to take his fill of these

639than of war. But the Trojans are never sated with battle."28

640So speaking, blameless Menelaus stripped the bloody armor

641from the body and gave it to his companions,

642and went back himself again and mixed with the front-fighters.

643There the son of king Pylaemenes leapt out at him,

644Harpalion, who had followed his dear father to fight

645at Troy, and never came home again to his fatherland.

646He stabbed the middle of Atreus' son's shield with his spear,

647close in, but could not drive the bronze on through,

648and drew back into the band of his companions, dodging doom,

649glancing on every side, lest flesh of his meet bronze.

650But Meriones, as he retreated, shot a bronze-tipped arrow

651and hit him in the right buttock; and the arrow

652passed under the bone, straight on, into the bladder.

653Sinking down on the spot, in the arms of his dear companions,

654breathing out his life, he lay stretched on the ground

655like a worm; and the dark blood ran out and soaked the earth.29

656Around him the great-hearted Paphlagonians busied themselves:

657they lifted him into a chariot and drove him toward holy Ilion,

658grieving; and among them went his father, shedding tears;

659and there was no blood-price for his son who was dead.

660Now at his killing the heart of Paris was angered exceedingly,

661for he had been his guest-friend among the many Paphlagonians;

662and angered for him, he let fly a bronze-tipped arrow.

663There was one Euchenor, the son of the seer Polyidus,

664a rich man and a brave one, whose house was in Corinth,

665who had boarded his ship knowing well his deadly fate:

666for the good old man Polyidus had told him, often,

667that he would waste to death of a grievous sickness at home,

668or go down under Trojan hands among the ships of the Achaeans;

669and so he shunned two things at once: the heavy fine

670of the Achaeans, and the loathsome sickness, that his heart might feel no pain.30

671Him Paris struck below the jaw, under the ear; and quickly the spirit

672went out of his limbs, and the hateful darkness took him.

673So they fought on, like blazing fire;

674and Hector, dear to Zeus, had not heard, and did not know at all

675that on the left of the ships his people were being cut down

676by the Argives — and soon the triumph would have belonged

677to the Achaeans: such was the holder of the earth, the earth-shaker,

678driving the Argives on, and defending them besides with his strength.

679But Hector held where first he had leapt the gates and the wall,

680breaking the close-set battle-lines of Danaan shield-men,

681there where the ships of Ajax and Protesilaus

682were drawn up on the beach of the grey sea, and above them

683the wall was built at its lowest — there where beyond all places

684the men themselves and their horses were fiercest in the fight.

685There the Boeotians, and the Ionians with their trailing tunics,31

686the Locrians, and the Phthians, and the shining Epeians

687held him off the ships as he charged hard, but had not strength

688to thrust him back away from them — brilliant Hector like a flame —

689the picked men of the Athenians among them; and their leader

690was Menestheus, Peteos' son, and with him followed

691Pheidas and Stichius and brave Bias; and the Epeians' leaders

692were Meges, Phyleus' son, and Amphion and Dracius,

693and before the Phthians, Medon, and Podarces steadfast in battle.

694Now the one — Medon — was a bastard son of godlike Oïleus

695and brother of Ajax; but he lived in Phylace,

696far from his own fatherland, having killed a man,

697a kinsman of his stepmother Eriopis, wife of Oïleus;32

698and the other was a son of Iphiclus, Phylacus's line.

699These, armed in the front of the great-hearted Phthians,

700fought in defense of the ships beside the Boeotians;

701and Ajax, the swift son of Oïleus, would no longer

702stand apart from Ajax, Telamon's son — not by a step:

703but as in fallow land two wine-dark oxen strain

704at the jointed plow with one and the same heart, and around

705the roots of their horns the sweat wells up in streams,

706and only the polished yoke holds the two apart

707as they drive down the furrow, and the plow cuts to the field's edge:

708so the two of them took their stand, shoulder to shoulder.33

709But the son of Telamon had troops with him, many and brave,

710companions who took his shield from him

711whenever weariness and sweat came over his knees;

712while no Locrians followed the great-hearted son of Oïleus:

713their hearts had no stomach for the standing fight,

714for they had no bronze helmets, thick with horsehair,

715no round shields and no ash spears:

716it was trusting in their bows, and in the twisted wool

717of sheep, that they had followed him to Ilion; and with these,

718shooting thick and fast, they broke the Trojan battle-lines.

719So then: the ones in front, in their intricate war-gear,

720fought the Trojans and Hector of the bronze helmet,

721while the others shot from behind, unseen; and the Trojans

722lost all memory of the joy of battle: the arrows drove them into rout.34

723And then, in misery, back from the ships and the huts

724the Trojans would have retreated to windy Ilion,

725had not Polydamas come and stood by daring Hector:

726"Hector, there is no dealing with you: no man's persuasion moves you.

727Because a god gave you the works of war beyond others,

728you insist on knowing counsel, too, better than others;

729but you cannot possibly take all things to yourself at once.

730To one man the god has given the works of war,

731to another the dance, to another the lyre and song,

732and in another's chest wide-seeing Zeus sets a mind

733of worth, and many men get profit of it,

734and it saves many, and he who has it knows it best of all.35

735But I will say it as it seems to me to be best.

736Everywhere around you the garland of war is blazing;

737and the great-hearted Trojans, now they have crossed the wall,

738are some of them standing back with their arms, and some fighting,

739fewer against more, scattered along the ships.

740Fall back, then, and summon all our best men here;

741and then we could weigh the whole counsel together —

742whether to fall on the many-benched ships, if the god

743is willing to hand us the power, or whether after all

744to come away from the ships unhurt. For I, for one, fear

745the Achaeans may pay us back yesterday's debt in full:

746a man waits by the ships insatiate of war,

747and I think he will hold back from the fighting no longer."

748So spoke Polydamas, and the prudent word pleased Hector,

749and at once he leapt with his weapons from the chariot to the ground,

750and speaking winged words he addressed him:

751"Polydamas — you hold all our best men here,

752I will go over there and take my share of the fighting,

753and come back quickly, when I have given them their orders."

754He spoke, and set off — like a snow-shining mountain —36

755screaming, and flew through the Trojans and the allies.

756And they all surged toward Panthous's son, gracious Polydamas,

757when they heard the voice of Hector.

758But he went ranging through the front-fighters, searching

759for Deiphobus, and the strength of lord Helenus,

760and Adamas, Asius's son, and Asius, Hyrtacus's son —

761if he might find them anywhere. He found them

762not unhurt, not unruined: some by the sterns

763of the Achaean ships lay dead at Argive hands,

764and some were inside the wall with wounds from far or near.

765But one he found quickly, on the left of the tearful battle:

766brilliant Alexandros, the husband of lovely-haired Helen,

767heartening his companions and urging them to fight.

768Standing close, he raked him with shaming words:

769"Paris — evil Paris — beauty and nothing more, woman-mad, seducer:37

770where is Deiphobus, and the strength of lord Helenus,

771and Adamas, Asius's son, and Asius, Hyrtacus's son?

772And where is Othryoneus? Now all of steep Ilion

773is ruined, top to bottom. Now your own sheer destruction is sure."

774Then godlike Alexandros answered him:

775"Hector — since your heart is set on blaming the blameless,

776some other day, perhaps, I may hang back from the fighting;

777but not now: my mother did not bear me wholly a coward.

778From the moment you woke the battle of our companions at the ships,

779from then we have stood our ground here, engaging the Danaans

780without respite. The companions you ask after are dead —

781except Deiphobus and the strength of lord Helenus:

782those two have gone off, each wounded in the hand

783by long spears; the son of Cronos kept death from them.

784Now lead on, wherever your heart and spirit command,

785and we will follow you, and gladly; nor do I think

786you will find us short of courage, so far as the strength is in us.

787Beyond his strength no man can fight, however hot he burns."

788So speaking the hero turned his brother's mind.

789They set off for where the battle and struggle burned thickest,

790around Cebriones, and blameless Polydamas,

791and Phalces, and Orthaeus, and godlike Polyphetes,

792and Palmys, and Ascanius and Morys, sons of Hippotion,

793who had come in relief out of deep-soiled Ascania

794the dawn before; and now Zeus drove them to fight.

795And they came on like a squall of punishing winds

796that under the thunder of father Zeus drives at the plain

797and with a prodigious roar meets the sea, and the countless

798seething waves of the loud-roaring water

799arch up, white-crested, some in front, and others behind them:

800so the Trojans, closed up, some in front, and others behind them,

801glittering in bronze, came on behind their leaders.

802And Hector led them, the equal of Ares bane of mortals,

803Priam's son; before him he held his shield, balanced every way,

804thick with hides, with heavy bronze hammered over it,

805and about his temples the shining helmet shook.

806Everywhere he probed the battle-lines, advancing,

807trying whether they might give before him as he came under shield-cover;

808but he could not confound the heart in the Achaeans' chests.

809And Ajax, striding long, was first to challenge him:

810"Possessed one — come closer! Why try to frighten Argives

811with mere noise? We are not unschooled in battle;

812it is the evil whip of Zeus that has broken us Achaeans.

813I suppose your heart hopes to strip the ships clean —

814well, we too have hands, quick hands, to hold them.

815Long before that, mark me, your own well-peopled city

816will be taken under our hands, and sacked.

817And for you yourself, I say the hour is near when, running,

818you will pray to father Zeus and the other immortals

819to make your fair-maned horses swifter than falcons

820as they carry you to the city, raising the dust of the plain."

821And as he spoke, a bird flew by on the right hand,

822an eagle, flying high; and the Achaean army shouted at it,

823heartened by the sign. But glorious Hector answered:

824"Ajax, you blundering ox of a braggart — what a thing to say!

825I wish I were as surely, all my days, the son

826of aegis-bearing Zeus, and the lady Hera had borne me,

827and I were honored as Athena and Apollo are honored,

828as surely as this day now brings evil to the Argives —

829all of them; and you will be killed among them, if you dare

830stand against my long spear, which will tear open

831your lily-soft skin; and you will glut the dogs and birds of Troy38

832with your fat and your flesh, fallen beside the ships of the Achaeans."

833So speaking he led the way, and they followed him

834with a prodigious roar, and the army screamed behind him.

835And the Argives on the other side raised their cry, and did not forget

836their courage, but stood fast against the best of the Trojans coming on;

837and the clamor of both reached the ether and the radiance of Zeus.39

1 lines 1–9 — Zeus, sure of his arrangements, turns his shining eyes away from the war — north, to the horse-herding Thracians, the close-fighting Mysians, and then clean off the map of the war: the Hippemolgoi, "Mare-milkers who drink the milk," and the Abioi, "most righteous of men." The lines are the most famous ethnographic glance in Homer — steppe pastoralists (later identified with Scythians; Ammianus Marcellinus was still quoting the verse in the fourth century AD) whose remoteness from the war is precisely their righteousness. Zeus rests his eyes on the people who own least and quarrel least — and his inattention is the hinge of the plot: no god, he thinks, will dare (7-9). One will. ↩

2 lines 17–22 — Poseidon's descent: "the tall hills trembled, and the timber, under the immortal feet of Poseidon as he went. Three strides he made, and with the fourth he reached his goal." Longinus quotes the passage in On the Sublime (9.8) as an instance of the divine measured by cosmic stride. Aegae, his golden house under the water, was paired with Helice at 8.203; Samothrace ("Thracian Samos"), the north-Aegean peak, really does command a sight-line over Imbros to the Troad — the poet's geography of watching is checkable, and correct. ↩

3 lines 23–31 — The ride: gold horses, gold whip, gold armor — and then the great verse of the animal world's homage: "the sea-beasts gamboled beneath him, coming from every hollow, and did not fail to know their lord; and the sea in its joy stood apart." The wave makes way; the axle stays dry. After eleven books of the sea as grey backdrop for grief, one god's commute turns it joyful — the Iliad's single moment of happy nature. ↩

4 lines 66–75 — The lesser Ajax detects the god from the way he moves: "the trace of his feet and legs from behind, as he went off, I knew it easily — the gods are easy to know" (ἀρίγνωτοι δὲ θεοί περ). Divine disguise deceives the face-to-face view only; gait betrays godhead. The other symptom is inward: both Ajaxes' hands and feet go "hungry" for battle — possession described from inside the possessed. ↩

5 lines 95–124 — Poseidon's rebuke, pivoting on the poem's political nerve: even if Agamemnon is "wholly to blame... because he dishonored the swift-footed son of Peleus, we, for our part, have no leave to slacken" — and then the great clause: "let us heal it, quickly. The minds of good men can be healed" (ἀκεσταί τοι φρένες ἐσθλῶν, 115). The god concedes the grievance and dissolves its authority in nine words — the whole ethics of the quarrel-plot, and the sentence Achilles will not hear until Book 19. ↩

6 lines 126–135 — The locked phalanx: "buckler pressed on buckler, helmet on helmet, man on man, and the horsehair crests on the bright ridges touched as they nodded." The lines (131-133) recur at 16.215-217 when the Myrmidons form up — reuse this English there. Whatever the date of hoplite tactics, this is the sensation of close order: equipment touching equipment before any enemy is met. ↩

7 lines 136–146 — Hector as the boulder: torn loose by a river in flood, bounding, the woods crashing beneath it — "until it reaches the level plain, and there it rolls no more, for all its rush." The simile carries its own prophecy: the stone's momentum is the cliff's gift, not the stone's, and the plain is waiting. Hector jams against the close-set lines within five lines of it. ↩

8 lines 170–205 — Imbrius, the son-in-law from Pedaeum who came back to Troy when the war began and was honored by Priam "like his own children" — another of the poem's in-law obituaries (cf. Iphidamas, Othryoneus, Alcathous: Troy's defense is a web of marriages). He falls like an ash on a mountain crest cut down by bronze. Then the book's most brutal image: the lesser Ajax cuts the head from the corpse "in wrath for Amphimachus" and bowls it through the crowd "like a ball," to stop in the dust at Hector's feet. Wrath answered with sport: the small Ajax's savagery is a fixed trait (cf. 23.473-498, and his end in the Odyssey), and the head at Hector's feet is a delivery with an address on it. ↩

9 lines 206–209 — Amphimachus was Cteatus's son — a grandson of the Moliones pair whom Poseidon fathered and once rescued from Nestor (11.750-752). So the god's rage here is family grief: the Iliad's gods enter its casualty-lists two generations removed, and Poseidon's next intervention (note [25]) is likewise a grandfather's grudge. ↩

10 lines 232–238 — Poseidon-as-Thoas: on the day's deserter, "may he be sport for dogs"; then the recruiting proverb — "Even sorry men, combined, come to some courage — and the two of us would know how to fight even the best." The arithmetic of the wall (12.412) again: courage as a sum, spoken by a god enlisting one tired middle-aged king. ↩

11 line 249 — "Meriones, Molus's son": Molus received the boar's- tusk helmet in its pedigree (10.269-270) — the same household's war-gear economy continues here, an entire scene about where spears come from. Idomeneus and Meriones bicker with perfect courtesy — the older man teasing, the younger standing on his record — before the god-stirred battle lets either spend it. ↩

12 lines 260–294 — The armory speech: twenty Trojan spears against the bright wall of the hut, "which I strip from the men I kill," because Idomeneus does "not fight standing far from enemy men." Then the great set-piece (275-286): the ambush as the assay of worth — the coward's color running, his crouch shifting from foot to foot, the heart hammering on the ribs, the teeth chattering aloud; the brave man's color "never changes," and his one prayer is to be mixed in the fighting at once. It is the poem's fullest psychology of fear, set not in the charge but in the waiting — and given to the oldest front-fighter in the army. ↩

13 line 291 — "the front-fighters' trysting-place" — ὀαριστύς, the word for lovers' intimate talk (cf. 22.127-128, where Hector uses it of a boy and girl whispering), applied to the range where chests meet spears. The battlefield as terrible intimacy: one of the boldest single words in the Iliad. ↩

14 lines 298–305 — Ares and Panic (Phobos, his son, the capitalized personification of 4.440) arming out of Thrace against the Ephyri or Phlegyes — gods who "do not hear both sides' prayers, but hand the glory to one." War-god theology in miniature; and an uneasy simile to hang on Idomeneus and Meriones, since Ares at this hour is penned on Olympus, deaf to everything — not even hearing of his own son's death (note [24]). ↩

15 lines 339–344 — The battle "bristled" (ἔφριξεν) with flesh- cutting spears — the field itself gets gooseflesh; eyes are blinded by bronze glare. Then the narrator's aside: "It would be a hard-hearted man who could rejoice at the sight of that toil, and not be grieved." As at 12.176 and 4.539-542, the poem turns to check the audience's pleasure — the singer measuring the distance between glory sung and toil seen. ↩

16 lines 345–360 — The theological knot of the middle Iliad: Zeus wills Trojan victory (to glorify Thetis and her son), yet not Achaean annihilation; Poseidon works the other side "in secret... likened to a man" — because "both were of one stock and one parentage, but Zeus was elder-born, and he knew more." Primogeniture as cosmology (restated by force in 15.165-167, and contested at 15.185-199). And so the two sons of Cronos "knotted the cord of strong strife and leveling war, and drew it taut over both sides — a cord unbreakable, not to be untied; and it unstrung the knees of many": the tug-of-war image of battle (cf. 7.102, 11.336) tied to its grimmest clause — the knot the gods cannot break is loosed instead in men's knees. Aristarchus athetized 350 (the Thetis line) as needless; the printed text keeps it. ↩

17 lines 363–382 — Othryoneus, war-bridegroom: he bid for Cassandra — her first appearance in the poem, "loveliest of Priam's daughters," with no word of prophecy, which is post-Homeric — "without bride-gifts," offering instead the war itself as bride-price. Idomeneus kills him mid-stride and conducts the poem's cruelest wedding-negotiation over the body: we'll give you Agamemnon's loveliest daughter "if you will join us and sack the well-peopled city of Ilion. Come along: let us settle the marriage at the seafaring ships — you will find we are not hard men over bride-terms." The marriage-economics of death (Iphidamas, 11.241-245) turned entirely to mockery: the dead man dragged by the foot toward his "wedding." ↩

18 lines 389–393 — Asius falls "as an oak falls, or a white poplar, or a towering pine, that carpenters up in the mountains cut down with fresh-whetted axes to be a ship's timber" — paying the death-notice filed at 12.116-117, killed by the very man it named. The simile recurs verbatim at 16.482-484 for Sarpedon — reuse this English there. Man as felled timber "to be a ship's timber": the war's dead as material for the very machines that brought the war. ↩

19 lines 402–416 — Deiphobus's vaunt for Hypsenor: Asius shall not lie unavenged — "even going down to Hades, the strong closer of the gate, he will be glad at heart: I have given him an escort for the road." The dead man as travel-arrangement; the poem's bookkeeping of vengeance (ποινή) spoken as courtesy. (In fact Hypsenor's death-wound is described and mourned — but the poet never says he died; ancient critics worried the point.) ↩

20 lines 424–444 — Alcathous: the poem's most terrible intervention. Poseidon "charmed his shining eyes and shackled his glorious limbs" — the god holds the man still, "like a grave-pillar or a tree in high leaf," while Idomeneus spears him through the chest. The bridegroom-obituary again (Hippodameia, who outshone her age-mates "in beauty and in handiwork and in wit"); the grave-stele simile stands the man's own monument up in advance; and the heart, still beating, shakes the butt-end of the spear — the most clinically horrifying detail in the poem. A god fighting for the Achaeans does this: the poem does not flinch, and does not comment. ↩

21 lines 446–454 — Idomeneus's ledger ("three for one") and pedigree: Zeus - Minos "warden of Crete" - Deucalion - Idomeneus. The Cretan king's genealogy is the shortest distance in the poem between Zeus and a living fighter — three generations — and he ends it flatly: the ships have carried me here, "an evil to you, and your father, and the rest of the Trojans." ↩

22 lines 459–461 — Aeneas found sulking, "rearmost of the crowd, for he was forever furious at brilliant Priam, because, brave among men as he was, Priam gave him no honor." The Achilles-pattern in Trojan miniature: worth unhonored by the king, service withheld. The grievance (never elsewhere explained) belongs to the junior branch of the royal house — cf. 20.178-183, where Achilles taunts him with hoping to inherit Priam's rank, and 20.302-308, where Poseidon prophesies that his line, not Priam's, will rule the Trojans hereafter. ↩

23 lines 489–495 — Aeneas's men follow "as flocks follow the bell-ram down from the pasture to drink — and the shepherd's heart is glad": war-following rendered as the tamest scene the poem knows, with the commander's joy set inside the shepherd's. The gentlest simile in a savage book. ↩

24 lines 518–525 — Deiphobus's cast kills Ascalaphus, "son of Enyalius" (Ares' son by the Catalogue, 2.512) — "but loud-roaring massive Ares knew nothing of it yet... he sat on the peak of Olympus, under the golden clouds, penned there by the designs of Zeus." A father's ignorance held in suspension across two books: at 15.110-142 Ares learns, arms for revenge against Zeus's edict, and must be forced down by Athena. The quiet lines here are the fuse. ↩

25 lines 554–575 — Poseidon shields Antilochus (Nestor's son — the god's affection for the house of Neleus again) and makes Adamas's spear-point "strengthless... grudging him that life." Adamas dies under Meriones' throw "between the genitals and the navel — there where the war-god comes most grievous to wretched mortals" — the poem's plainest statement about wounds and pain — writhing "as a bull writhes that herdsmen in the mountains have bound with willow-ropes and drag along unwilling." The pastoral world keeps arriving as the measure of agony. ↩

26 lines 586–592 — Helenus's arrow springs off Menelaus's corslet "as off a broad winnowing-shovel, on a great threshing-floor, the dark-skinned beans or the chickpeas jump under the shrill wind and the winnower's swing." Harvest again — the missile as chaff-work; the deadly and the domestic in one bounce. The duel itself is asymmetric: spear against bow, and the spear pins the bow-hand to the bow. ↩

27 lines 598–600 — The wounded hand is bound "in a sling of twisted sheep's wool" — the σφενδόνη, the Iliad's only named sling, here as bandage; at 716 the same twisted wool arms the Locrians as a weapon. One humble implement, both uses, thirty lines apart: the poem's quartermaster memory at work. ↩

28 lines 620–639 — Menelaus over Peisander (killed in the poem's one axe-against-sword exchange, his eyes dropped bloody in the dust): the wronged husband's theology. The Trojans are indicted under "the harsh wrath of loud-thundering Zeus of Guest-right" (Ζηνὸς ξεινίου... μῆνιν — the poem's theme-word "wrath" given to Zeus as protector of hospitality, the war's whole legal basis in one phrase); then the strange, wonderful complaint: "Of all things there is satiety — of sleep, of love, of sweet song and of blameless dancing; a man would sooner pray to take his fill of these than of war. But the Trojans are never sated with battle." The catalogue of things men can have enough of is the poem's shyest confession of what peace is for. Ancient critics athetized here (637); readers have loved the passage in exact proportion. ↩

29 lines 643–659 — Harpalion dies "like a worm... stretched on the ground" — the poem's smallest death-simile, for a boy who followed his father to war. And then the famous impossibility: "among them went his father, shedding tears" — his father is Pylaemenes, killed by Menelaus at 5.576-579 in our own text. Ancient critics athetized 658-659 to heal the contradiction; moderns treat it as the clearest seam of oral composition — the poet reaching for the pathos-formula (a father behind a bier) without an audit of Book 5. The translation keeps the printed text and the wound. The closing line — "and there was no blood-price for his son who was dead" — prices the loss in the currency the poem uses everywhere (cf. 9.632-636, 18.498). ↩

30 lines 663–672 — Euchenor of Corinth, the seer's son, who sailed "knowing well his deadly fate": his father had told him he would die of sickness at home or under Trojan hands at the ships. He chose the spear — "and so he shunned two things at once: the heavy fine of the Achaeans, and the loathsome sickness." The precise double of Achilles' two fates (9.410- 416) at commoner's scale — and with the poem's driest touch, the war-death is also a tax dodge: the θωή, the fine levied on draft-evaders, listed in one breath with mortal disease. He is killed by Paris, absent-mindedly, in someone else's revenge. ↩

31 lines 685–700 — The defense roster: Boeotians, Locrians, Phthians, Epeians, picked Athenians — and "the Ionians with their trailing tunics" (Ἰάονες ἑλκεχίτωνες), the name's only occurrence in Homer, applied to Athenian-led troops: a single audible seam between the poem's world and the poet's own Ionian one. ↩

32 lines 694–697 — Medon, bastard son of Oïleus, exiled from his fatherland "having killed a man, a kinsman of his stepmother Eriopis": the homicide-exile is epic's standard hard-luck biography (cf. Patroclus, 23.85-88). These four lines recur verbatim at 15.333-336, where Medon dies — the poem files a man's whole story twice, once at his post and once at his death. Reuse this English there. ↩

33 lines 701–708 — The two Ajaxes as "two wine-dark oxen" (βόε οἴνοπε — the sea's own color-word given to plow-beasts) straining at one plow, sweat welling at the roots of their horns, the polished yoke alone holding them apart: the poem's great image of joint labor — heroic partnership rendered as agriculture, down the furrow to the field's edge. ↩

34 lines 712–722 — The Locrian way of war: no helmets, no shields, no ash spears — bows and wool slings, shooting "from behind, unseen," while the armored line fights in front. Combined arms, and the breaking of the Trojan rush credited to the missile troops the heroic code barely deigns to see (cf. 11.385-390): the poem knows exactly what wins battles, and says so here without editorializing. ↩

35 lines 726–747 — Polydamas's third counsel — accepted, like the first: the famous division of gifts. "To one man the god has given the works of war, to another the dance, to another the lyre and song, and in another's chest wide-seeing Zeus sets a mind of worth... But you cannot possibly take all things to yourself at once." (Line 731 — dance and song — was omitted by Aristarchus; Zenodotus kept it, Lucian quoted it, and the printed text prints it.) The tactical advice — regroup, count heads, decide — carries a shadow-clause: "a man waits by the ships insatiate of war," the book's only reference to Achilles as a standing threat, from the man who is always right. ↩

36 line 754 — Hector sets off "like a snow-shining mountain" — ὄρεϊ νιφόεντι ἐοικώς: a man like a mountain, and a moving one; ancient and modern readers have puzzled at it (a mass of glittering bronze? a peak walking?). It is the largest thing Hector is ever compared to, on the day his rush crests. ↩

37 line 769 — "Paris — evil Paris — beauty and nothing more, woman-mad, seducer": Hector's rebuke repeats 3.39 word for word, our English kept. There it opened the poem's first duel, here its last day of Trojan victory — and Paris's answer this time is measured, brave, and just ("your heart is set on blaming the blameless" — the very phrase Patroclus used of Achilles, 11.654), ending on the proverb "Beyond his strength no man can fight, however hot he burns." The poem allows Paris his best moment exactly where Hector repeats his worst line. ↩

38 lines 809–832 — The exchange before the charge: Ajax's taunt promises Hector a prayer for horses "swifter than falcons," and the eagle confirms it on the right; Hector's reply is the strangest oath-wish in the poem — "I wish I were as surely, all my days, the son of aegis-bearing Zeus... as this day is bringing evil on the Argives": certainty of today traded against an impossible divinity, from the man who cannot know his warrant expires at sundown. His threat gives Ajax "lily- soft skin" (χρόα λειριόεντα) — the flower-word for the flesh of the enemy's biggest man; and "you blundering ox of a braggart" (βουγάϊε) is one of the poem's rare pure insults, coarse as a barnyard. ↩

39 line 837 — The last line: "and the clamor of both reached the ether and the radiance of Zeus." The book that began with Zeus turning his shining eyes away ends with the battle arriving at him as sound — αἰθέρα καὶ Διὸς αὐγάς, the noise climbing into the very brightness he looked away with. The ring closes: he will look back down at 15.4, and the correction will be immediate and total. ↩
