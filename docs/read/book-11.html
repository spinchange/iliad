Book XI

The long day begins: Agamemnon's morning of glory, the captains carried off wounded one by one, and Nestor plants the plan that will kill Patroclus.

Translator’s note on Book 11

Book 11 opens the longest day in poetry: the sun that rises from Tithonus's bed in line 1 will not set until 18.239-242, seven and a half books from now, and Zeus dictates the day's whole shape in advance in his message to Hector (185-194): let Agamemnon rage; when he is wounded and mounts his chariot, Hector's hour begins, and runs until he reaches the ships and the sun goes down. What follows is a day of subtraction. Agamemnon's aristeia — the poem's first sight of the commander actually pre-eminent in battle — burns hot and short, and then the captains are removed one by one: Agamemnon by Coon's spear, Diomedes and Eurypylus and Machaon by Paris's arrows, Odysseus by Socus's thrust; by the book's end Ajax alone is left in the field, backing away like a beast, like a donkey — and the despised bow (note 29) has done most of the day's work.

The book's second half changes register entirely, and is the hinge of the whole epic. Achilles, watching the rout from his stern for pleasure, sees Nestor carry a wounded man past and sends Patroclus to ask the name — and the poet stamps the errand at once: "he came out, the equal of Ares — and that was the beginning of his evil" (604). In Nestor's hut the plan is invented that Patroclus will repeat at the opening of Book 16: let Achilles send you out in his own armor (794-803, largely athetized in antiquity — see notes [42]-[43]). Nestor's enormous Pylian reminiscence (656-762) earns its length as paradigm — the young Nestor fought although his father forbade him and hid his horses; Achilles, able, withholds — and closes on the saddest line the old man speaks: "Such was I among men — if ever I was." Patroclus, running back, is detained by the wounded Eurypylus and stays to cut out the arrow: the book ends with the gentlest man in the poem healing one wound while his own death is being prepared, and its last clause repeats, word for word, the drying of Agamemnon's wound at 267 — the day's first casualty and its last, rhymed.

Our printed Greek genuinely lacks line 543 — the line Aristotle read, "for Zeus would be indignant with him, whenever he fought a better man" — and the translation reproduces the gap (numbering jumps 542 to 544), with the line translated in note 35. Register notes: the Muse-invocation reuses 2.484; 13-14 reuse 2.453-454 (athetized here as a wrongful repeat); 5-9 reuse the camp geometry of 8.222-226; Peleus's "always to be the best" repeats 6.208, our wording kept; and the cup of Nestor (632-637) meets the oldest joke in Greek epigraphy (note 38).

72The battle held their heads level; and they raged

76they sat in their own halls, where for each of them

81he sat away from the others, exulting in his glory,

82looking out at the city of the Trojans and the ships of the Achaeans,

83at the flash of bronze, at the killers and the killed.

84So long as it was morning, and the sacred day still growing,11

85so long the missiles of both sides struck home, and the people fell.

100their chests gleaming bare, when he had stripped their tunics,

102two sons of Priam, bastard and true-born both,

121from those two; they themselves were fleeing before the Argives.

122Next Peisander and Hippolochus, staunch in the fight,

123sons of war-minded Antimachus — the man who, above all,

124taking Alexandros's gold, his glittering gifts,

125opposed giving Helen back to tawny-haired Menelaus —

126his two sons the lord Agamemnon caught,

127both in one chariot, trying to hold their swift horses,

128for the shining reins had slipped out of their hands

129and the pair had panicked. Like a lion the son of Atreus

131"Take us alive, son of Atreus — and take a worthy ransom.

132Many treasures lie stored in the house of Antimachus,

133bronze, and gold, and iron worked with much labor:

134out of these our father would gladden you with ransom past counting

135if he learned we were alive by the ships of the Achaeans."

136So the two of them, weeping, addressed the king

137with honeyed words; but the voice they heard was unhoneyed:13

138"If you two are truly sons of war-minded Antimachus,

139who once in the Trojan assembly urged that Menelaus,

140come as an envoy with godlike Odysseus,

141be killed on the spot and not released back to the Achaeans —

142now you will pay for your father's foul outrage."

143He spoke, and thrust Peisander from the chariot to the ground,

144striking him with the spear in the chest; he crashed on his back in the dust.

145Hippolochus leapt away; and him he killed on the ground,

146shearing his hands off with the sword, and striking off his neck,

147and sent him rolling like a stone through the crowd.

148He left them; and where the thickest battle-lines were driven,

149there he charged in, and with him other strong-greaved Achaeans.

150Foot-soldiers killed foot-soldiers, forced into flight,

151and horsemen horsemen — from under them rose the dust

152out of the plain, stirred by the thundering feet of horses —

153dealing death with the bronze. And the lord Agamemnon,

154killing always, followed, calling on the Argives.

155As when annihilating fire falls on unthinned timber,

156and the whirling wind carries it everywhere, and the thickets

157fall root and all under the fire's assault,

158so under Atreus' son Agamemnon fell the heads

159of Trojans in flight; and many strong-necked horses

160rattled empty chariots down the causeways of the battlefield,

161missing their blameless charioteers — who lay on the earth,

162far dearer now to the vultures than to their wives.14

163But Hector — Zeus drew him out of the missiles and the dust,

164out of the man-slaughter, out of the blood and the tumult,

165while Atreus' son followed on, hot, calling to the Danaans.

166Past the tomb of ancient Ilus, Dardanus' descendant,15

167across the middle of the plain, past the fig tree they streamed,

168straining for the city; and always Atreus' son followed

169screaming, his invincible hands spattered with gore.

170But when they came to the Scaean Gates and the oak tree,

171there at last they halted, and stood waiting for one another.

172Others were still in full flight across the plain, like cattle

173a lion has set running, coming on them in the milking-dark of night —16

174all of them; but to one of them sheer destruction appears:

175her neck he breaks first, seizing it in his strong teeth,

176and then he gulps down the blood and all the inward parts;

177so Atreus' son, the lord Agamemnon, pressed them,

178killing always the hindmost man; and they fled before him.

179Many fell from their chariots on their faces, or on their backs,

180under Atreus' son's hands, for he raged with the spear before all.17

181But when he was on the point of coming up under the city

182and the steep wall, then the father of men and gods

183came down from heaven and took his seat on the peaks

184of Ida of the many springs; and he held the lightning in his hands.

185And he sent Iris of the golden wings to carry his word:

186"Up, go, swift Iris; tell this word to Hector.

187So long as he sees Agamemnon, shepherd of the people,

188raging among the front-fighters, cutting down the ranks of men,

189so long let him give ground, and command the rest of the army

190to fight the enemy through the strong encounter.

191But when, struck by a spear or hit by an arrow,

192Agamemnon leaps to his horses, then I will hand him the power

193to kill, until he reaches the well-benched ships

194and the sun goes down and the sacred darkness comes on."18

195So he spoke, and wind-footed swift Iris did not disobey,

196and went down from the hills of Ida to holy Ilion.

197She found the son of war-minded Priam, brilliant Hector,

198standing among his horses and his bolted chariot;

199and standing close, swift-footed Iris spoke to him:

200"Hector, son of Priam, the equal of Zeus in counsel,

201Zeus the father has sent me out to tell you this.

202So long as you see Agamemnon, shepherd of the people,

203raging among the front-fighters, cutting down the ranks of men,

204so long yield from the fighting, and command the rest of the army

205to fight the enemy through the strong encounter.

206But when, struck by a spear or hit by an arrow,

207Agamemnon leaps to his horses, then Zeus will hand you the power

208to kill, until you reach the well-benched ships

209and the sun goes down and the sacred darkness comes on."

210So speaking, swift-footed Iris went her way,

211and Hector with his weapons leapt from the chariot to the ground,

212and shaking his sharp spears went everywhere through the army,

213stirring them to fight; and he woke the dread din of battle.

214They wheeled about and stood to face the Achaeans,

215and the Argives on their side strengthened their battle-lines.

216The battle was set, and they stood face to face; and Agamemnon

217charged first, determined to fight far ahead of all.

221Iphidamas, Antenor's son, a good man and a big one,

222who was reared in Thrace of the deep soil, mother of flocks.

223Cisses raised him in his house when he was little,

224his mother's father, who begot fair-cheeked Theano;

225and when he reached the measure of glorious youth,

226Cisses tried to keep him there, and offered him his daughter.

227But newly wed, out of the bridal chamber he went chasing the report

229The balanced ships he left behind at Percote,

230and came on foot the rest of the way to Ilion;

231and now he came against Atreus' son Agamemnon.

232And when they were close, advancing on one another,

233Atreus' son missed his cast; the spear was turned aside;

234but Iphidamas stabbed at the belt, below the corslet,

235and leaned into the thrust, trusting his heavy hand.

236He did not pierce the glittering war-belt: long before that,

237meeting the silver, the point bent back like lead.

238And wide-ruling Agamemnon caught the shaft in his hand

239and dragged it toward him, furious as a lion, and out of his grip

240wrenched it; and he struck his neck with the sword, and unstrung his limbs.

241So he fell there, and slept a sleep of bronze,20

242pitiable man — far from his wedded bride, helping his townsmen,

263filled up their measure of doom and went down into the house of Hades.

273He sprang into the chariot and told his charioteer

275And he cried a piercing cry, to carry to the Danaans:

276"Friends, leaders and lords of the Argives,

277yours be the task now: keep from the seafaring ships

278the grim din of battle, since Zeus the counselor has not

279allowed me to war with the Trojans all day long."

280So he spoke; and the charioteer whipped the fair-maned horses

281toward the hollow ships, and the two flew, nothing loath;

282their chests were white with foam, dusted beneath with dust,

284And when Hector saw Agamemnon going off,

285he called to Trojans and Lycians with a great shout:

286"Trojans and Lycians and Dardanians who fight at close quarters —

287be men, my friends; remember your rushing courage.

288Their best man is gone, and Zeus the son of Cronos has given

289great glory to me. Drive your whole-hoofed horses straight

290at the powerful Danaans, and win the higher glory."

292As when some hunter sets his white-toothed hounds

293on a wild boar or a lion,

294so on the Achaeans Hector, Priam's son, the equal

295of Ares bane of mortals, set the great-hearted Trojans.

296He himself strode among the foremost, thinking great things,

297and fell upon the battle like a howling squall

298that leaps down and lashes the violet-colored sea.

300Hector, Priam's son, when Zeus gave him the glory?

301Asaeus first, and Autonous, and Opites,

302and Dolops, Clytius' son, and Opheltius, and Agelaus,

317"I will stand, certainly, and I will endure it; but small

318will be our profit, since Zeus the cloud-gatherer

319would rather hand the power to the Trojans than to us."

322killed Molion, the godlike aide of that same lord.

323They left them there, done with fighting for good,

324and the two went through the crowd wreaking havoc, as when two boars

325fall on the hunting hounds, thinking great things;

326so, turning back, they cut down Trojans; and the Achaeans

327gladly caught their breath in their flight from brilliant Hector.

328Then they took a chariot and two men, the best of their district,

329two sons of Merops of Percote, who beyond all men25

330knew the seer's art, and would not let his sons

331go to man-destroying war; but the two would not

332obey him: the fates of black death were leading them on.

333Tydeus' son Diomedes, famed with the spear,

334robbed them of life and breath and took their glorious armor;

335and Odysseus killed Hippodamus and Hypeirochus.

336Then the son of Cronos stretched the battle level between them,

337looking down from Ida; and they went on killing one another.

338Tydeus' son wounded the hero Agastrophus with his spear,

339Paeon's son, at the hip-joint; his horses were not

340near for escape — great was the blindness of his heart:

341his aide was holding them apart, while he on foot

342raged through the front-fighters, until he lost his own life.

343But Hector marked them sharply down the ranks, and rushed at them

344shouting; and with him followed the Trojan battle-lines.

345Diomedes, good at the war-cry, shuddered to see him,

346and quickly spoke to Odysseus, who was near:

347"Here it comes rolling at the two of us, this plague — massive Hector.

348Come, let us stand and beat him off, holding our ground."

349He spoke, and balanced his long-shadowed spear and hurled it,

350and hit — he did not miss — aiming at the head,

351at the helmet's crown; but bronze was turned by bronze

352and never reached the fair skin: the helmet stopped it,

353three-layered, socket-eyed, which Phoebus Apollo gave him.26

354And Hector ran back an immense way, and merged with the crowd,

355dropped to his knees and stayed there, propping himself

356with one thick hand on the earth; and black night covered his eyes.

357But while Tydeus' son went after his spear's cast,

358far through the front-fighters, where he saw it strike the earth,

359Hector caught his breath again, and springing back into his chariot

360drove out into the mass of men, and dodged black doom.

361Rushing after with his spear, strong Diomedes called:

362"Dog! Again you have run out from under death — and yet the evil27

363came close. Now once more Phoebus Apollo has pulled you out —

364to him you must pray whenever you go into the thud of javelins.

365I will finish you yet, meeting you later,

366if any god anywhere stands helper to me too.

367For now I will go after the others, whoever I can catch."

368He spoke, and set to stripping Paeon's spear-famed son.

369But Alexandros, the husband of lovely-haired Helen,

370drew his bow against Tydeus' son, shepherd of the people,

371leaning against the pillar on the man-made mound,

432such a pair of men! — for killing them and stripping their armor,

435through the shining shield the heavy spear went,

1 lines 1–2 — Dawn rises "from her bed beside illustrious Tithonus" — the same couplet opens Odyssey 5, a famous cross-epic doublet. Tithonus is the Trojan prince (brother of Priam, 20.237) whom Dawn carried off as her consort; the story of his withering immortality is later. A new dawn-formula for the register, distinct from "early-born rosy-fingered Dawn": this is the dawn of the poem's longest day, which will not set until 18.239-242. ↩

2 lines 3–14 — Strife (Eris) opens the day as Dream opened Book 2 — Zeus's emissary to a sleeping army. Lines 5-9 repeat the camp geometry of 8.222-226 in our same words: Odysseus's ship lies at the midpoint of the beached fleet, Ajax and Achilles hold the exposed ends. And 13-14 repeat 2.453-454 ("war became sweeter to them than going home...") — there the effect of Odysseus and Athena rallying a mob that had run for the ships; here the work of a shrieking goddess. Ancient critics (Zenodotus omitted, Aristophanes and Aristarchus athetized) judged the couplet wrongly repeated here; the poet's point may be exactly the repetition: what rhetoric did in Book 2, mere din does now. ↩

3 lines 20–23 — Cinyras, legendary king of Cyprus — the island's only appearance in the Iliad. He hears the "great report" (κλέος) of the coming war and buys his distance from it with a corslet: a guest-gift as diplomatic exemption. Later tradition made him promise fifty ships and send one real ship and forty-nine clay models; the poem knows only the elegant bribe. ↩

4 lines 24–28 — κύανος, rendered "blue enamel," is dark-blue glass paste or niello inlay — a genuine Bronze Age luxury technique (the inlaid daggers of Mycenae). Serpents "like rainbows" that Zeus "sets in a cloud, a portent for mortal men": the rainbow as omen of war or storm (17.547-549), armor that carries the day's weather on the king's chest. ↩

5 lines 36–37 — The Gorgon between Terror and Panic (Deimos and Phobos, capitalized personifications since 4.440): the shield's face is the face of battle itself — cf. the aegis at 5.739-742, which carries the same company. ↩

6 lines 45–46 — Athena and Hera thunder in honor of "the king of Mycenae rich in gold" — a unique gesture: thunder is Zeus's instrument, but the arming of the paramount king draws a salute from the two pro-Achaean goddesses. The aristeia of Agamemnon is thus formally announced; it will last some two hundred lines. ↩

7 lines 52–55 — Zeus rains dew dripping with blood — the sky itself hemorrhaging before the killing (again at 16.459, for Sarpedon, where the drops are explicitly tears). Line 55 reads "many strong heads" where the proem (1.3) has "many strong souls"; ancient copies show the variant ψυχάς here too — the echo of the poem's opening is audible either way. ↩

8 lines 62–66 — Hector as the "baleful star" (οὔλιος ἀστήρ) that shows and hides among clouds: the star similes track him and Achilles across the poem until Achilles becomes the Dog Star itself at 22.26-31 — "brightest of all, but an evil sign." ↩

9 lines 67–73 — The reapers: two lines of harvesters facing each other, driving swathes toward the middle of a rich man's field — armies as farmhands, the plain as harvest. "The battle held their heads level" (72): the fighting itself is the subject, holding the two lines like a beam of scales (cf. Zeus's actual scales, 8.69). Then the wolves; and Strife alone of the gods present, rejoicing — the day's one spectator-deity on the field, while Zeus watches from Ida "exulting in his glory." ↩

10 lines 78–83 — The gods sit at home blaming Zeus; he ignores them. Zenodotus omitted the passage and Aristophanes and Aristarchus athetized it, apparently as inconsistent with the gods' oath- bound quiescence of Book 8. But the tableau — all heaven sulking in its fine houses while one god watches the killing with pleasure — is the day's theology in miniature. ↩

11 lines 84–91 — The register morning-line (fixed at 8.66-67) is followed by the poem's homeliest time-stamp: the hour when the woodcutter, arms weary, makes his midday meal. Homer's clock runs on labor, not hours — dawn, the woodcutter's dinner, the unyoking of oxen (16.779). At that hour the line breaks, and the aristeia proper begins. ↩

12 lines 101–112 — Isus and Antiphus: Achilles once caught these same two on Ida, bound them with willow shoots, and released them for ransom — pastoral war, an economy of capture and return. Agamemnon recognizes them and kills them: the third ransom-refusal of the poem (6.45-65 Adrestus; 10.378-457 Dolon) and the mark of what the war has become since Achilles left it. ↩

13 lines 122–147 — Peisander and Hippolochus: their father Antimachus took Paris's gold to block the return of Helen, and proposed murdering the ambassador Menelaus (the embassy of 3.205-224). Agamemnon's sentence — "now you will pay for your father's foul outrage" — is justice as inherited debt, and the execution (hands sheared off, head struck off, the trunk sent "rolling like a stone") is the poem's most clinical mutilation. The frame is a bitter pun kept from the Greek: they beg "with honeyed words" (μειλιχίοις) and hear a voice "unhoneyed" (ἀμείλικτον). Compare 21.98-135, where Achilles will answer a suppliant's plea with the same adjective and worse. ↩

14 lines 159–162 — The empty chariots rattling down the causeways, their drivers "far dearer now to the vultures than to their wives" — the aristeia's cost stated in one hexameter of domestic arithmetic. It prepares the Iphidamas vignette (note [20]). ↩

15 lines 166–168 — The rout streams past the tomb of Ilus and the wild fig tree: the plain's fixed survey-marks (the tomb: 10.415, and again 11.371; the fig tree: 6.433, 22.145). Landmarks let the audience feel the front move — from mid-plain to the Scaean Gates and the oak, and back again within the book. ↩

16 lines 172–178 — "in the milking-dark of night" (νυκτὸς ἀμολγῷ) — an already-obscure formula (recurring 15.324, 22.28, 22.317) connected by the ancients with milking-time; the lion who scatters the whole herd but eviscerates one cow is Agamemnon exactly: the multitude flees, the hindmost dies. ↩

17 lines 179–180 — Omitted by Zenodotus, athetized by Aristarchus (179-180) and Aristophanes (180): the couplet generalizing the slaughter ("many fell from their chariots...") judged a weak doublet of what precedes. ↩

18 lines 185–194 — Zeus's message is the day's script, delivered in advance: Agamemnon's wounding as the trigger-clause, Hector's power to run "until he reaches the ships and the sun goes down and the sacred darkness comes on." Everything from here to 18.242 executes this sentence. Iris relays it with the change the grammar demands and nothing more (202-209 = 187-194) — divine messages are verbatim or they are nothing (cf. 2.23-33). ↩

19 lines 218–220 — The Muse-invocation repeats 2.484 in our fixed line — full formality for a single duel: Iphidamas is granted a catalogue-style entrance, which is his funeral honor too (the other invocations: 2.484, 14.508, 16.112). ↩

20 lines 221–247 — Iphidamas of Thrace: raised by his grandfather Cisses, married to his young aunt (Theano's sister — the poet does not pause over it), he left the bridal chamber for the war and "slept a sleep of bronze" — χάλκεον ὕπνον, the poem's hardest metal laid over its softest noun. The bride-price arithmetic — a hundred cattle down, a thousand head promised — turns the obituary into a ledger: everything paid, nothing enjoyed. This is the Iliad's marriage-economics of death at its most explicit, and the template for Protesilaus (2.700-702) and for Othryoneus later (13.363-382). ↩

21 lines 248–263 — Coon wounds Agamemnon and dies trying to drag his brother's body clear; Agamemnon beheads him over the corpse — the two sons of Antenor "filled up their measure of doom" in one another's arms. Antenor, the Trojan advocate of returning Helen (7.347-353), loses two sons in this book to the king Helen was taken from; the poet says nothing, and the silence does the work. ↩

22 lines 269–272 — The wounded king's pain is a woman's labor: the sharp shaft the Eileithyiai send, "Hera's daughters, keepers of the bitter pangs." The inversion is deliberate and unique — the most masculine act in the heroic economy, the aristeia, ends in the most feminine of agonies; war-wound and birth-pang are the poem's two involuntary cries. The Eileithyiai (birth-goddesses) enter the register here; one returns at 16.187, 19.103. ↩

23 lines 299–300 — The narrator's roll-call question, fixed at 5.703: here it marks the transfer of the aristeia — the same frame that measured Hector-and-Ares now measures Hector alone, with "when Zeus gave him the glory" as the ominous answer-clause. ↩

24 lines 310–319 — The register despair-line (8.130) sounds again, and Diomedes' answer to Odysseus is the day's arithmetic at its most honest: I will stand, "but small will be our profit," since Zeus wills it otherwise. Piety and futility in one breath — the last speech of Diomedes' fighting day. ↩

25 lines 328–334 — The two sons of Merops of Percote fall to Diomedes: lines 329-332 repeat their Catalogue entry (2.831-834) almost verbatim, our wording kept. The Catalogue planted the seer-father who foresaw and forbade and was not obeyed; Book 11 harvests it. Compare 5.148-151 (the sons of the dream-reader). ↩

26 lines 349–356 — Diomedes' cast rings off the helmet "which Phoebus Apollo gave him" — divine equipment as plot-armor — yet Hector goes down; 355-356 repeat 5.309-310 (Aeneas's swoon), our words kept, and the ancients athetized the repeat here. Hector's day of guaranteed glory (192-194) nearly ends at its opening: the promise covered the outcome, not the pain. ↩

27 lines 362–367 — "Dog! Again you have run out from under death..." — this six-line taunt returns, word for word, in Achilles' mouth at 20.449-454. Diomedes has been the poem's stand-in Achilles all through the middle books; here he even pre-speaks him. Reuse the English verbatim there. ↩

28 lines 369–379 — Paris shoots from the tomb of Ilus, leaning on the ancestor's grave-pillar — the city's founder as cover for the city's most criticized fighter — and comes up "laughing very sweetly." Three captains leave the field by his arrows in this book (Diomedes, Machaon, Eurypylus): the day of the bow. ↩

29 lines 385–395 — "Bowman, foul-mouth, glorious with your curls, ogler of girls" — the concentrated contempt-vocabulary of the spear-ethos. "Glorious with your curls" renders κέρᾳ ἀγλαέ, an old crux: "horn" was read by some ancients as the horn bow, by others as a horn-shaped lovelock (the reading our translation follows; the apparatus records the living dispute). Diomedes' disdain — "I care no more than if a woman had hit me, or a witless child" — is the poem's fullest statement of the bow's dishonor; yet the arrow-graze sends him from the field for the rest of the epic's fighting, and an arrow of Paris will one day kill Achilles (19.416-417, 22.359-360). The taunt loses the war. ↩

30 lines 401–410 — Odysseus alone: the poem's first full deliberative soliloquy ("Troubled, he spoke to his own great-hearted spirit" — a frame that will recur for Menelaus, Agenor, Hector). The deliberation runs the heroic code to its bare axiom: "cowards walk away; whoever would be best must stand, hit or hitting." Hector's fatal monologue at 22.99-130 is this scene's dark twin — there the same logic will forbid the retreat that would have saved him. ↩

31 lines 428–433 — Socus — the only man in the poem to wound Odysseus — addresses him with a new-minted title: "Odysseus of the many tales, insatiate of tricks and of labor." Even his enemies speak his epithets; the compliment is the challenge. ↩

32 lines 450–455 — Odysseus over Socus: your parents will not close your eyes; the birds will have you; "but me, when I die, the brilliant Achaeans will bury with honors." The grimmest speech Odysseus makes in the Iliad — burial as the last competitive good, boasted over a corpse (Plutarch quotes the lines in his Consolation). It is the exact inverse of the poem's ending, where the enemy dead is given back for burial. ↩

33 lines 473–484 — The triple simile: jackals round the arrowed stag — until "some god leads in" a lion who scatters them and eats. Ajax arrives as the lion; the rescued Odysseus, uncomfortably, occupies the stag's place, and the "rescue" is another predation. In 480 Zenodotus read "hollow" for "shadowy" grove, and some copies inserted a line naming the shield of Tychius (= 7.220) after 485 — the apparatus notes both; the printed text keeps neither. ↩

34 lines 514–515 — "A healer is a man worth many other men together" — proverbial ever after; the specifying line 515 ("at cutting out arrows and sprinkling gentle drugs") was omitted by Zenodotus and athetized by Aristophanes and Aristarchus as a prosaic gloss. Homeric medicine is exactly these two skills — extraction and pharmacy — practiced by warriors: Machaon and Podaleirius are fighting doctors, and when both are down the craft passes to laymen taught by laymen (830-832: Patroclus, taught by Achilles, taught by Chiron). ↩

35 line 543 — Our printed Greek text genuinely lacks line 543 (the numbering jumps 542 to 544): the line was absent from the medieval manuscripts and is known because Aristotle quotes it, discussing righteous indignation (Rhetoric 1387a; cf. Plutarch's citations of the passage): "for Zeus would be indignant with him, whenever he fought a better man" — Ajax retreating not from Hector but from theology. Editors since Wolf print it in brackets; our source omits it as the OCT records, and the translation reproduces the gap. With 9.458-461, this is the second transmission-wound this project keeps visible. ↩

36 lines 544–565 — The double simile of Ajax's retreat: first the lion driven all night from the cattle-yard by dogs, javelins, and firebrands, going off "sullen at heart" at dawn; then, without pause, the donkey — slow, stick-broken, cropping the deep grain while boys beat him, leaving only when he is full. The lion was athetized by Zenodotus (548-557), and ancient taste flinched at the donkey; but the pair is the whole portrait: nobility from inside Ajax's chest, comedy from outside — the strongest man in the army backing away at his own pace, the only donkey in Homer, and one of the great tonal risks in epic. ↩

37 lines 596–604 — The hinge of the Iliad. Achilles, "gazing at the sheer toil and the tearful rout" from his stern — spectator by choice — sees Nestor pass with a wounded man and calls out Patroclus, who comes out "the equal of Ares — and that was the beginning of his evil" (ἔκμολεν ἶσος Ἄρηϊ, κακοῦ δ' ἄρα οἱ πέλεν ἀρχή). The narrator breaks his own reticence to mark the moment: a door opens, and the poem names it fate. Achilles' stated hope — "now I think the Achaeans will soon stand about my knees begging" — shows the wrath still fed by the sight; his curiosity about one wounded man is what kills his friend. ↩

38 lines 632–637 — The cup of Nestor: golden studs, four handles, doves feeding at each — and the poet's tape-measure detail that another man could barely lift it full, "but Nestor, the old man, raised it without strain." On a clay cup from Pithekoussai (Ischia), c. 730-720 BC, someone scratched three lines: "I am the cup of Nestor, good to drink from; whoever drinks from me, straightway desire of fair-crowned Aphrodite will seize." It is among the oldest surviving Greek writing — and it is a joke that only works on an audience that already knew this passage: the earliest external witness to the Iliad in circulation. Scholars have matched the described cup to Mycenaean finds (the gold "dove cup" from Shaft Grave IV); the poem's furniture is centuries older than the poem. ↩

39 lines 624–641 — Hecamede — Nestor's prize-woman from Tenedos, sacked by Achilles (every comfort in this hut traces back to the man refusing to fight) — mixes the κυκεών, "posset": Pramnian wine, grated goat's-milk cheese, white barley, with honey and onion as relish alongside. Plato quotes the recipe (Ion 538c) — and elsewhere (Republic 405e-406a) makes it a scandal of Homeric medicine that a wine posset is served to wounded men; Machaon, shot through the shoulder, drinks it. The same drink, drugged, is Circe's instrument in Odyssey 10 — the ordinary hospitality this scene shows straight. ↩

40 line 654 — "a terrible man. He would quickly blame even the blameless" — Patroclus on Achilles, the poem's most intimate character-note: fear inside love. Plutarch quotes the line (How to Tell a Flatterer). Patroclus will say the harder version to Achilles' face at 16.29-35 ("your parent was the grey sea and the cliffs"). ↩

41 lines 656–762 — Nestor's Pylian epic, his longest memory: the cattle-raid against Elis, the debt-division at dawn, Heracles' devastation of Pylos (eleven brothers dead), the Moliones saved by their father Poseidon, and Nestor's first aristeia — fifty chariots taken, "two men around each." Its function is paradigm: the young Nestor fought although Neleus forbade him and hid his horses; Achilles, with every power to fight, withholds. (One line of the tale, 699, is flagged in the scholia as suspected spurious.) The close is the old man's whole tragedy in seven words: "Such was I among men — if ever I was" (εἴ ποτ' ἔον γε) — glory so far off that its owner half-doubts it happened. ↩

42 lines 765–790 — The send-off at Peleus's house: the embassy of recruitment, the sacrifice in the courtyard, young Achilles leaping up to seat the guests — the poem's one glimpse of its hero at home, in a scene of pure xenia. Peleus's charge repeats 6.208 word for word in Greek and in our English — "always to be the best, and to hold one's head above all others" — the aristocratic imperative issued identically to Glaucus and to Achilles; and against it Menoetius sets the other ethic: you are lesser-born and weaker, but older — advise him. Patroclus's whole life is in that division of labor. Aristophanes and Aristarchus athetized 767-785 (the reminiscence's core!) as inconsistent in its geography; the vulgate kept it, and so does the printed text. ↩

43 lines 794–803 — The fatal plan, invented by Nestor: if a prophecy holds Achilles back, "let him send you out at least... and let him give you his fine armor to carry into the war, so that the Trojans may take you for him." Patroclus will repeat these lines to Achilles nearly verbatim at 16.36-45 — reuse this English there. Ancient critics chipped at the passage from both ends (794-795 athetized by Zenodotus; 802-803 by Aristarchus, as lifted from 16.44-45 — where they stand in Patroclus's mouth). "You may be a light to the Danaans": the image is of the beacon-fire — and what the disguise actually buys is Patroclus's death, Hector's brief triumph, and Achilles' return. The oldest man in the army, meaning pure good, sets the poem's catastrophe in motion in ten lines. ↩

44 lines 804–848 — The delay that structures the middle of the poem: Patroclus, running back with Nestor's words in him, is stopped at the assembly-ground by wounded Eurypylus, pities him, and stays — "even so I will not abandon you in your suffering." He cuts out the arrow, washes the wound, crushes the bitter root: the healer's craft learned from Achilles, learned from Chiron "the most righteous of the Centaurs" — the gentlest genealogy in the poem, running through its two great killers. Patroclus will still be in this hut at 15.390-404, and what finally moves him is watching the wall fail. The book's last clause — "and the wound began to dry, and the blood stopped" — repeats 267 exactly: Agamemnon's wound opened the day's subtraction, Eurypylus's closes the book; between the two dryings, the poem has chosen its victim. ↩
