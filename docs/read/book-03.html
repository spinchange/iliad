Book III

The duel for Helen: Paris against Menelaus, the view from the wall, and Aphrodite's rescue.

Translator’s note on Book 3

Book 3 reads like the war's first year transplanted into its tenth: a duel between the two men the whole war is about, a truce, and Helen naming the Achaean commanders for Priam as though he had never seen them in nine years of siege. Ancient and modern critics alike noticed the chronological strain; the poem plainly does not care, and the translation does not try to smooth it. What the book actually does is introduce the war's cause and cast in the most economical way possible, and it is built almost entirely out of formal, ceremonial speech — challenge, herald's summons, oath, prayer, curse. Those registers are rendered with deliberate stiffness (the oath-language especially), because Book 9's embassy and later truce scenes will reuse them; the conventions are logged in translation/CONVENTIONS.md.

One idiom is kept literal as a matter of policy: ὅρκια τάμνειν, "to cut oaths" — from cutting the victims' throats — is translated "cut the sworn oaths" throughout, not softened to "swear a treaty." The whole oath scene (hair distributed to the chief men, wine poured as the curse names the perjurers' spilled brains, the lambs' throats cut with the same knife) is a single ritual gesture, and the verb carries it.

Paris is called both Ἀλέξανδρος and Πάρις by the poem, with Ἀλέξανδρος predominating; the translation reproduces whichever name the Greek uses in each line rather than normalizing to "Paris" (see note 2). Helen's voice is this book's discovery: her self-lacerating "dog-face that I am" (note 13), the elders' hushed astonishment, and the confrontation in which she — alone of mortals in the Iliad — talks back to a god to her face and is crushed into obedience (notes [22]-[24]). Aristarchus athetized both that confrontation and her bitter speech to Paris; Zenodotus rewrote the line in which Aphrodite carries her a chair. The ancient editors were visibly uncomfortable with exactly the scenes a modern reader values most, and the notes record each intervention while the translation keeps the vulgate text intact.

The apparatus also preserves, at line 302, four "plus verses" found in a Ptolemaic papyrus but absent from the medieval manuscripts — a live specimen of the pre-Aristarchean fluidity of the text (note 19).

1Now when they were marshaled, each company with its leaders,

2the Trojans came on with clamor and a cry, like birds —

3like the clamor of cranes that rises against the sky,

4when they flee the winter and the measureless rain

5and fly clamoring toward the streams of Ocean,

6bearing slaughter and doom to the Pygmy men,1

7and out of the morning air they carry their evil strife —

8but the Achaeans came on in silence, breathing fury,

9hearts set on standing by one another.

10As when the South Wind pours mist on the peaks of a mountain,

11no friend to shepherds, better than night to the thief,

12and a man sees only as far as a stone's throw —

13so the dust rose swirling under their feet

14as they came on; and very swiftly they crossed the plain.

15And when they were close, advancing on one another,

16godlike Alexandros stood out as the Trojans' champion,2

17with a leopard skin on his shoulders, and curved bow

18and sword; and shaking two spears headed with bronze3

19he challenged all the best of the Argives

20to fight him man to man in dread combat.

21But when Menelaus dear to Ares caught sight of him4

22coming out in front of the throng with long strides,

23then like a lion delighted to light on a great carcass,

24finding a horned stag or a wild goat

25when he is starving — he devours it greedily even though

26the swift dogs and the sturdy young hunters are driving at him —

27so Menelaus was delighted to see godlike Alexandros

28with his own eyes; for he thought to make the offender pay.

29At once he leapt from his chariot to the ground in his armor.

30But when godlike Alexandros saw him

31appear among the front fighters, his heart was struck cold,

32and he shrank back into the crowd of his companions, dodging death.

33As a man who has seen a snake in a mountain glen

34starts back and away, and trembling seizes his limbs,

35and he retreats, and pallor takes his cheeks,

36so back into the throng of the proud Trojans

37slipped godlike Alexandros, in dread of Atreus' son.

38And Hector saw him, and raked him with shaming words:

39"Paris — evil Paris — beauty and nothing more, woman-mad, seducer:

40I wish you had never been born, or had died unmarried.

41That is what I would wish, and it would be far better

42than to have you a disgrace like this, a thing others look at sideways.

43How the long-haired Achaeans must be cackling —

44they thought a prince was champion because a fine

45look is on him; but there is no strength in his heart, no fight.

46Were you really such a man when in the seagoing ships

47you sailed the deep with your trusted companions gathered,

48and mixed with strangers, and carried off a beautiful woman

49from a far land, a bride of spearmen —

50a great grief to your father, to the city, and to all the people,

51a joy to our enemies, a hanging of your own head?

52And will you not stand up to Menelaus dear to Ares?

53You would find out what kind of man it is whose wife in her bloom you keep.

54The lyre would be no help to you then, nor the gifts of Aphrodite,

55that hair and that fine look, when you went down in the dust.

56The Trojans are cowards indeed — otherwise long since

57you would have put on a shirt of stone for all the evil you have done."5

58And godlike Alexandros answered him:

59"Hector, you rake me justly, not more than justly.

60Your heart is always unwearying, like an axe

61driven through timber by a man's swing when he skillfully

62cuts out a ship-beam, and the iron strengthens his stroke:

63so unshaken is the spirit in your chest.

64But do not throw in my face the lovely gifts of golden Aphrodite.

65Not to be flung away are the gods' glorious gifts —

66whatever they themselves give; no man could take them by choosing.

67Now, if you want me to fight and do battle:

68make the other Trojans sit down, and all the Achaeans,

69and put me in the middle with Menelaus dear to Ares

70to fight for Helen and all her possessions.

71Whichever of us wins and proves the stronger,

72let him take all the wealth, and the woman, and lead them home;

73and the rest of you, cutting friendship and sworn oaths,

74may you live on in deep-soiled Troy, and they return

75to horse-pasturing Argos and Achaea of the beautiful women."

76So he spoke, and Hector rejoiced greatly to hear the word,

77and he went into the middle and held back the Trojan lines

78gripping his spear by the middle; and they all sat down.

79But the long-haired Achaeans kept shooting at him,

80aiming with arrows and casting stones,

81until the lord of men Agamemnon called out loud and long:

82"Hold, Argives! Do not shoot, young men of the Achaeans!

83Hector of the flashing helmet stands ready to say something."

84So he spoke, and they held from fighting and fell silent,

85quickly. And Hector spoke between the two armies:

86"Hear from me, Trojans and strong-greaved Achaeans,

87the word of Alexandros, for whose sake this quarrel rose.

88He bids the other Trojans and all the Achaeans

89lay down their fine armor on the earth that feeds so many,

90while he in the middle, with Menelaus dear to Ares,

91fights alone for Helen and all her possessions.

92Whichever of them wins and proves the stronger,

93let him take all the wealth, and the woman, and lead them home;

94and the rest of us — let us cut friendship and sworn oaths."

95So he spoke, and they all went still in silence.

96And among them spoke Menelaus, good at the war-cry:

97"Hear me now also. The pain of this comes closest

98to my heart; and I intend that they part at last,

99Argives and Trojans, since you have suffered many evils

100for the sake of my quarrel, and Alexandros who began it.

101For whichever of the two of us death and fate are prepared,

102let him die; and the rest of you part, and quickly.

103Bring two lambs — one white ram, one black ewe,

104for Earth and Sun; and we will bring another, for Zeus.6

105And lead down the strength of Priam, so he may cut the oaths

106himself, since his sons are overbearing and not to be trusted —

107that no one by transgression may do violence to the oaths of Zeus.

108The minds of younger men are always blowing in the wind;

109but where an old man is present, he looks before and behind

110at once, how the best may come of it for both sides."7

111So he spoke, and Achaeans and Trojans rejoiced,

112hoping to be done with the misery of war.

113They reined the chariots back into the lines, and stepped down,

114and stripped off their armor and laid it on the ground

115close together — there was little field between the armies.

116And Hector sent two heralds to the city

117quickly, to fetch the lambs and to summon Priam;

118and the lord Agamemnon sent Talthybius off

119to the hollow ships, and ordered him to bring

120a lamb; and he did not disobey brilliant Agamemnon.

121Now Iris went as messenger to white-armed Helen,

122in the likeness of her sister-in-law, the wife of Antenor's son —

123the one Antenor's son the lord Helicaon had married,

124Laodice, loveliest of the daughters of Priam.

125She found her in the hall: she was weaving a great web,

126a double cloak of purple,8 and working into it the many struggles

127of the horse-taming Trojans and bronze-shirted Achaeans,

128which for her sake they suffered at the hands of Ares.

129Standing close, swift-footed Iris spoke:

130"Come here, dear bride, to see wonderful doings

131of the horse-taming Trojans and bronze-shirted Achaeans.

132They who before this carried tearful war at each other

133in the plain, ravenous for deadly battle —

134now they sit in silence, and the war has stopped;

135they lean on their shields, and the long spears are stuck in the earth.

136But Alexandros and Menelaus dear to Ares

137will fight for you with their long spears;

138and you will be called the dear wife of the man who wins."

139So speaking, the goddess put sweet longing into her heart

140for her first husband, and her city, and her father and mother.

141At once she veiled herself in shining white linen

142and went out of her chamber, letting fall a soft tear —

143not alone: two handmaids followed with her,

144Aethra, Pittheus' daughter,9 and ox-eyed Clymene.

145And quickly they came to where the Scaean Gates stood.10

146Now those around Priam — Panthous and Thymoetes,

147Lampus and Clytius and Hicetaon, scion of Ares,

148and Ucalegon and Antenor, wise men both —

149sat as elders of the people at the Scaean Gates,

150finished with war now because of age, but speakers

151of quality, like cicadas that in the woods

152perch on a tree and send out their lily-thin voice:11

153such were the leaders of the Trojans sitting on the tower.

154And when they saw Helen coming up onto the tower,

155softly they spoke winged words to one another:

156"No shame that Trojans and strong-greaved Achaeans

157have suffered pain so long for a woman like this one.

158She is terribly like the immortal goddesses to look at.

159But even so — such as she is, let her go home in the ships,

160and not be left here, a grief to us and our children after."

161So they spoke; but Priam called Helen to him:12

162"Come here, dear child, and sit in front of me,

163to see your first husband and your kin and your friends —

164you are not to blame in my eyes: the gods are to blame,

165who set upon me this tearful war with the Achaeans —

166and to tell me the name of that gigantic man there,

167who that Achaean is, so tall and strong.

168There are others taller by a head, indeed;

169but a man so beautiful I have never yet seen with my eyes,

170nor so majestic. He looks like a king."

171And Helen answered him, brilliant among women:

172"You are dear to me, dear father-in-law, and dread to me too.

173How I wish vile death had pleased me on the day I followed

174your son here, leaving my bridal chamber and my kinsmen

175and my own dear daughter and the sweet company of girls my age.

176But that was not to be; and so I waste away with weeping.

177But I will tell you what you ask me and inquire.

178That man is Atreus' son, wide-ruling Agamemnon,

179both things at once: a good king and a strong spearman.

180And he was brother-in-law — to me, dog-face that I am13 — if that ever was."

181So she spoke, and the old man marveled at him, and said:

182"Fortunate son of Atreus, child of destiny, blessed of fortune —

183how many young Achaean men are subject to you!

184Once I went into Phrygia of the vines,

185and there I saw Phrygian men in their multitudes, with their glancing horses,

186the hosts of Otreus and godlike Mygdon,

187encamped then along the banks of Sangarius;

188for I too, as an ally, was counted among them

189on the day the Amazons came, the match of men.14

190But even they were not so many as the bright-eyed Achaeans."

191Second, the old man saw Odysseus, and asked:

192"Come, tell me of this one too, dear child — who he is.

193Shorter by a head than Agamemnon, Atreus' son,

194but broader in the shoulders and chest to look at.

195His armor lies on the earth that feeds so many,

196and he himself ranges the ranks of men like a bellwether ram —

197yes, I liken him to a thick-fleeced ram

198that moves through a great flock of white ewes."

199And Helen, sprung of Zeus, answered him:

200"That is Laertes' son, resourceful Odysseus,

201raised in the land of Ithaca — rocky as it is —

202a man who knows every kind of trick and close-woven plan."

203And wise Antenor said to her in answer:

204"Lady, that word of yours is exactly true.

205For brilliant Odysseus came here once before,

206on an embassy about you,15 with Menelaus dear to Ares;

207and I was their host: I welcomed them in my halls,

208and learned the build of both, and their close-woven plans.

209Now when they mixed with the assembled Trojans,

210standing, Menelaus overtopped him with his broad shoulders,

211but with both seated, Odysseus was the more majestic.

212And when they wove their words and their plans before us all,

213Menelaus, certainly, spoke fluently —

214few words, but very clear: he was no man of many words,

215no rambler — though he was in fact the younger.

216But whenever resourceful Odysseus rose to speak,

217he would just stand there, eyes fixed down on the ground,

218not moving the scepter backward or forward

219but holding it stiff, like a man who knows nothing;

220you would have called him some sulking churl, a plain fool.

221But when he let the great voice out of his chest,

222and the words came like snowflakes in winter,

223then no other mortal could rival Odysseus.

224Then we did not marvel so much at how Odysseus looked."

225Third, the old man saw Ajax, and asked:

226"And who is that other Achaean man, tall and strong,

227towering over the Argives in head and broad shoulders?"

228And Helen of the trailing robe answered, brilliant among women:

229"That is gigantic Ajax, wall of the Achaeans.

230And on the other side Idomeneus stands among the Cretans

231like a god, and the Cretan captains are gathered around him.

232Often Menelaus dear to Ares was his host

233in our house, whenever he came from Crete.

234And now I see all the other bright-eyed Achaeans,

235whom I could well recognize and name their names;

236but two marshals of the people I cannot see —

237Castor breaker of horses and Polydeuces the great boxer,

238my own brothers, whom one mother bore with me.

239Either they did not follow from lovely Lacedaemon,

240or they followed here in the seagoing ships

241but now are not willing to enter the battle of men,

242fearing the shame and the many reproaches that are mine."

243So she spoke. But the life-giving earth already held them under,16

244there in Lacedaemon, in their dear fatherland.

245Now the heralds were carrying through the city the gods' oath-offerings,

246two lambs, and wine that gladdens the heart, fruit of the field,

247in a goatskin bag; and the herald Idaeus carried

248a gleaming mixing-bowl and cups of gold;

249and he stood beside the old king and roused him:

250"Rise, son of Laomedon. The chief men are calling —

251of the horse-taming Trojans and the bronze-shirted Achaeans —

252for you to come down to the plain, to cut the sworn oaths.

253For Alexandros and Menelaus dear to Ares

254will fight with their long spears for the woman;

255and woman and wealth shall go with the winner,

256while the rest, cutting friendship and sworn oaths,

257we should live on in deep-soiled Troy, and they return

258to horse-pasturing Argos and Achaea of the beautiful women."

259So he spoke, and the old man shuddered; but he told his companions

260to yoke the horses, and they promptly obeyed.

261Priam mounted and drew the reins back taut,

262and beside him Antenor mounted the beautiful chariot,

263and through the Scaean Gates they drove the swift horses toward the plain.

264And when they had come among the Trojans and Achaeans,

265they stepped from the chariot onto the earth that feeds so many

266and walked into the space between Trojans and Achaeans.

267At once the lord of men Agamemnon rose,

268and resourceful Odysseus rose; and the proud heralds

269brought together the gods' oath-offerings, and in the bowl

270mixed the wine, and poured water over the kings' hands.

271And Atreus' son drew with his hand the knife

272that always hung beside the great sheath of his sword,

273and cut hairs from the lambs' heads; and then

274the heralds gave them out to the chief men of the Trojans and Achaeans.

275And Atreus' son prayed aloud among them, lifting his hands:

276"Father Zeus, ruling from Ida, most glorious, greatest;

277and Sun, who see all things and hear all things;

278and Rivers, and Earth, and you two below who punish

279the dead, whoever has sworn a false oath:17

280be witnesses; guard the sworn oaths.

281If Alexandros kills Menelaus,

282let him keep Helen and all her possessions,

283and we will go home in the seagoing ships;

284but if tawny-haired Menelaus kills Alexandros,

285then the Trojans are to give back Helen and all her possessions,

286and pay the Argives whatever recompense is fitting,

287such as will be remembered among men still to come.

288And if Priam and Priam's sons refuse

289to pay me that recompense when Alexandros has fallen,

290then I will fight on for the payment owed,

291staying right here, until I reach the end of the war."

292He spoke, and cut the lambs' throats with the pitiless bronze,

293and laid them down on the ground gasping,

294the life going out of them: the bronze had taken their strength.

295Then drawing wine from the bowl into the cups

296they poured it out, and prayed to the gods who are forever;

297and thus would one of the Achaeans and Trojans say:

298"Zeus most glorious, greatest, and you other immortal gods:

299whichever side first does harm against the oaths,

300may their brains run on the ground as this wine runs,

301theirs and their children's, and their wives be subdued to other men."18

302So they spoke; but the son of Cronos would not yet bring it to pass.19

303And Priam, Dardanus' descendant, spoke his word among them:

304"Hear from me, Trojans and strong-greaved Achaeans.

305I am going back to windswept Ilion,

306since I cannot yet bear to watch with my own eyes

307my dear son fighting Menelaus dear to Ares.

308Zeus, I suppose, knows — and the other immortal gods —

309for which of the two the end of death is fated."

310He spoke — a man like a god — and set the lambs in the chariot,

311and mounted himself, and drew the reins back taut,

312and beside him Antenor mounted the beautiful chariot;

313and back again to Ilion the two returned.

314Then Hector, Priam's son, and brilliant Odysseus

315first measured out the ground, and then

316took lots and shook them in a bronze-fitted helmet,

317for which of the two should cast his bronze spear first.

318And the hosts prayed, and lifted their hands to the gods,

319and thus would one of the Achaeans and Trojans say:

320"Father Zeus, ruling from Ida, most glorious, greatest:

321whichever of the two brought these troubles on both peoples,

322grant that he go down, dead, into the house of Hades,

323and that friendship and sworn oaths be ours."

324So they spoke; and great Hector of the flashing helmet shook the lots,

325looking away behind him; and Paris's lot leapt quickly out.

326Then the men sat down in ranks, where each man's

327high-stepping horses and patterned armor lay.

328And he girded the fine armor about his shoulders —20

329brilliant Alexandros, husband of lovely-haired Helen.

330First he set the greaves along his shins,

331beautiful ones, fitted with silver ankle-guards;

332next he girt about his chest the corslet

333of his brother Lycaon — and it fitted him;

334and about his shoulders he slung the silver-studded sword,

335a sword of bronze, and then the shield, great and heavy;

336and on his strong head he set the well-made helmet

337with its horsehair crest — and terribly the plume nodded above him —

338and took up a stout spear, fitted to his grip.

339And in the same way warlike Menelaus put on his gear.

340And when they had armed on either side of the throng,

341they strode into the space between Trojans and Achaeans,

342glaring terribly; and amazement held the watchers,

343the horse-taming Trojans and the strong-greaved Achaeans.

344They took their stand near each other on the measured ground,

345shaking their spears, in fury at one another.

346Alexandros first hurled his long-shadowed spear21

347and struck the shield of Atreus' son, balanced every way;

348but the bronze did not break through: its point was bent back

349in the strong shield. Second, Atreus' son Menelaus

350rose with his bronze, with a prayer to father Zeus:

351"Zeus, lord: grant me to punish the man who wronged me first —

352brilliant Alexandros — and break him under my hands,

353so that many a man may shudder, even among men yet to be born,

354at doing evil to a host who has offered him friendship."

355He spoke, and balancing his long-shadowed spear he hurled it

356and struck the shield of Priam's son, balanced every way.

357Through the shining shield the heavy spear went,

358and through the richly worked corslet it drove;

359and straight on beside his flank the spear sheared through

360the tunic — but he had swerved aside, and dodged black doom.

361Then Atreus' son drew the silver-studded sword,

362rose up, and struck the ridge of his helmet; but around it

363the sword shattered in three and four pieces, and fell from his hand.

364And Atreus' son groaned, looking into the wide sky:

365"Father Zeus, no other god is more destroying than you!

366I thought I had made Alexandros pay for his wickedness,

367and now the sword is broken in my hands, and the spear

368flew from my grip for nothing, and I have not hit him."

369He spoke — and springing on him he seized the horsehair helmet

370and swung round and dragged him toward the strong-greaved Achaeans;

371and the richly stitched strap was strangling the soft throat under it,

372the chin-strap drawn tight beneath his jaw.

373And now he would have dragged him off and won glory past telling,

374had not Aphrodite, Zeus's daughter, seen it sharply:

375she broke the strap — leather of an ox felled by force —

376and the helmet came away empty in his heavy hand.

377The hero sent it spinning away toward the strong-greaved Achaeans

378with a whirl, and his trusted companions gathered it up;

379and back again he sprang, raging to kill

380with his bronze spear. But Aphrodite snatched Paris away —

381easily, as a god can — and hid him in thick mist,

382and set him down in his fragrant, scented chamber.

383Then she went herself to summon Helen; she found her

384on the high tower, with the women of Troy thick around her.

385With her hand she plucked at her nectar-sweet robe and shook it,

386and spoke to her in the likeness of an old woman,

387a wool-comber of long ago, who used to work fine wool

388for her when she lived in Lacedaemon, and whom she dearly loved;

389in her likeness brilliant Aphrodite spoke:

390"Come here. Alexandros calls you home.

391There he is, in the bedchamber, on the turned bed,

392gleaming in his beauty and his clothes. You would not say

393he had come from single combat — rather that he was going

394to a dance, or sitting down just now come from dancing."

395So she spoke, and stirred the heart in Helen's breast;

396and when she noticed the surpassing beauty of the goddess's throat

397and the breasts of desire and the flashing eyes,

398awe took her,22 and she spoke a word, and named her:

399"Strange goddess — why are you so set on cheating me like this?

400Will you carry me somewhere still farther on, to one of the fine

401cities of Phrygia or lovely Maeonia,

402if some mortal man is dear to you there too?

403Is it because Menelaus has beaten brilliant Alexandros

404and wants to lead hateful me home again —

405is that why you stand beside me now, weaving your tricks?

406Go sit beside him yourself. Renounce the paths of the gods;

407never again turn your feet back to Olympus,

408but fret over him forever, and guard him,

409until he makes you his wife — or else his slave.

410I will not go to him — that would be a thing for anger —

411to serve that man's bed. The women of Troy

412would all mock me afterward; and I have griefs past counting in my heart."

413And in anger brilliant Aphrodite answered her:

414"Do not provoke me, hard woman — or I may quit you in my anger

415and hate you as extravagantly as I now love you,

416and devise bitter hatreds between both sides,

417Trojans and Danaans alike — and you would perish miserably."

418So she spoke, and Helen sprung of Zeus was afraid,

419and she went in silence, wrapped in her shining white robe,

420unnoticed by all the Trojan women; and the goddess led.

421When they came to the beautiful house of Alexandros,

422the handmaids turned quickly to their work,

423and she went to the high-roofed bedchamber, brilliant among women.

424And laughter-loving Aphrodite23 took a chair for her —

425a goddess! — carried it, and set it facing Alexandros;24

426there Helen sat, daughter of aegis-bearing Zeus,

427her eyes turned away, and she lashed her husband with her word:

428"You came back from the fight. How I wish you had died there,

429broken by the strong man who was my first husband.

430You used to boast, before, that you were the better man —

431stronger than Menelaus dear to Ares in force and hand and spear.

432Go on then, challenge Menelaus dear to Ares

433to fight you again face to face. — But no; I myself

434advise you to stop: do not fight tawny-haired Menelaus

435man against man, do not battle him,

436like a fool — or you may soon go down under his spear."25

437And Paris answered her with his word:

438"Woman, do not lash my heart with hard reproaches.

439This time Menelaus has won — with Athena's help;

440another time it will be I. There are gods on our side too.

441Come, let us take our joy in love, in bed together.

442Never has desire so wrapped itself around my heart —

443not even when I first carried you off from lovely Lacedaemon

444and sailed away in the seagoing ships,

445and on the island of Kranae26 mixed with you in love and bed —

446as I love you now, and sweet longing takes hold of me."

447He spoke, and led the way to bed; and his wife followed.

448So the two lay together on the corded bed.

449But Atreus' son ranged through the throng like a wild beast,

450hunting for a glimpse of godlike Alexandros.

451And no one of the Trojans or their famed allies

452could show Alexandros to Menelaus dear to Ares;

453not out of friendship, surely, would they have hidden him, had anyone seen him:

454for he was hated by them all like black death.

455And among them spoke the lord of men Agamemnon:

456"Hear me, Trojans and Dardanians and allies.

457Victory clearly belongs to Menelaus dear to Ares.

458Give up, then, Argive Helen and the possessions with her,

459and pay whatever recompense is fitting,

460such as will be remembered among men still to come."

461So spoke Atreus' son, and the rest of the Achaeans gave assent.

Notes

1 lines 3–7 — The cranes migrating to Ocean's stream to war on the Pygmy men — the oldest surviving reference to the Pygmy legend. The simile's real work is the contrast it frames: the Trojans advance with clamor like birds, the Achaeans in disciplined silence (8-9) — a recurring characterization of the two armies. ↩

2 line 16 — "Alexandros" — the poem's usual name for Paris (Πάρις appears only occasionally, e.g. 325, 437). The translation reproduces whichever name the Greek uses in each line rather than normalizing. Hector's opening insult at 39, Δύσπαρι ("Dys-Paris," ill-Paris), is rendered "Paris — evil Paris." ↩

3 lines 18–20 — A rare case of overlapping athetesis by both major Alexandrian editors: Zenodotus rejected 18-20, Aristarchus separately 19-20 (apparatus). The bother was partly Paris's arsenal — leopard skin, bow, sword, and two spears is theatrical overload. Kept: the over-dressed entrance is exactly in character. ↩

4 line 21 — "dear to Ares" (ἀρηΐφιλος) — Menelaus's standing epithet in this book; his other is "tawny-haired" (ξανθός, 284). Both always so rendered. ↩

5 line 57 — "a shirt of stone" (λάϊνον χιτῶνα) — i.e. death by public stoning, the community's punishment for the offense Paris committed; the idiom is kept literal. ↩

6 lines 103–104 — The victims are matched to the oath-witnesses: a white ram for the Sun, a black ewe for Earth, a third lamb from the Achaeans for Zeus. Priam must swear in person because oaths bind the swearer alone — and his sons are "not to be trusted" (106): the war began with Paris violating a solemn bond. ↩

7 lines 108–110 — Aristarchus athetized these three generalizing lines on young men's flightiness (apparatus, Line 108-110). Kept; the sententiousness suits Menelaus's careful legalism in this speech. ↩

8 lines 125–128 — Helen weaving the battles "which for her sake they suffered" — the poem's most self-referential image: she is weaving the subject-matter of the Iliad while living inside it. The Alexandrian editors read "a double cloak of purple" (πορφυρέην, as printed); most manuscripts have "shimmering" (μαρμαρέην) — the apparatus records the split (Line 126). ↩

9 line 144 — Aethra, Pittheus' daughter, is in myth the mother of Theseus, made Helen's slave when the Dioscuri sacked Athens's territory to recover the girl Theseus had abducted — a story alien to Homer, and ancient critics condemned the line as an Athenian interpolation (Aristarchus athetized it; apparatus, Line 144). Translated as printed; the name is either a fossil of that lost epic tradition or a coincidence ancient readers over-read. ↩

10 line 145 — The Scaean Gates: Troy's main gate facing the plain and the battlefield, the fixed stage-set for departures and watching in this poem (and, in tradition beyond it, the place of Achilles' death). ↩

11 lines 151–152 — The old counselors chirp like cicadas with their "lily- thin voice" (ὄπα λειριόεσσαν — literally "lily-like," delicate as a petal): age has left them song without strength. One of the poem's most admired domestic similes. ↩

12 lines 161–244 — The teichoscopia ("viewing from the wall"): Priam, in the war's tenth year, asks Helen to identify Agamemnon, Odysseus, and Ajax as though the enemy were new to him. Critics ancient and modern have noted the impossibility; dramatically it is the audience's own introduction to the Achaean captains, staged through the eyes of the two people most implicated in the war. ↩

13 line 180 — "dog-face that I am" (κυνώπιδος) — Helen turns on herself the poem's harshest shame-insult (cf. Achilles to Agamemnon, Book 1 note [19]). Her self-lacerations ("hateful me," 404; cf. 173-176) are unique in the poem and are rendered at full strength. ↩

14 line 189 — The Amazons, "the match of men" (ἀντιάνειραι) — one of only two Iliad references to them (cf. 6.186); Priam fought against them on the Sangarius in his youth. In post-Homeric epic they come to Troy as allies — here they are still simply a memory of a hard war. ↩

15 lines 205–224 — Antenor recalls the pre-war embassy: Odysseus and Menelaus came to Troy to demand Helen back before hostilities — a tradition the Iliad otherwise passes over. His sketch of Odysseus's oratory (the stiff stance, eyes down, then words "like snowflakes in winter") became the classical archetype of deceptive-looking eloquence. ↩

16 lines 243–244 — The famous ironic close of the teichoscopia: Helen scans the plain for her brothers Castor and Polydeuces, imagining them ashamed of her — "but the life-giving earth already held them under." The narrator states their death with the epithet φυσίζοος ("life-giving") set against the grave: kept in its full contradiction. Homer knows nothing (or says nothing) of their later shared immortality. ↩

17 lines 276–279 — The oath-witnesses: Zeus of Ida, the all-seeing Sun, the Rivers and Earth, "and you two below who punish the dead" — probably Hades and Persephone (some ancients understood the Erinyes). Perjury is the one sin explicitly punished after death in the Iliad. ↩

18 lines 298–301 — The curse enacts itself in the ritual: as the wine is poured out, so may the perjurers' brains pour on the ground. "Their wives be subdued to other men" completes the symmetry — the war is, precisely, about a wife taken by another man. ↩

19 line 302 — A Ptolemaic papyrus preserves four extra lines here — Zeus thundering in answer and planning woes for both sides — absent from the medieval manuscripts (apparatus, Line 302; cf. disputed_lines.md #14 on Ptolemaic "plus verses"). A live specimen of how fluid the text still was before the Alexandrian standardization. The vulgate's laconic "the son of Cronos would not yet bring it to pass" is translated; the papyrus expansion is recorded here. ↩

20 lines 328–338 — The poem's first full arming type-scene, in the fixed order that recurs whenever a hero arms: greaves, corslet, sword, shield, helmet, spear(s). Later arming scenes (Agamemnon in Book 11, Patroclus in 16, Achilles in 19) elaborate the same skeleton; it will not be re-footnoted. Zenodotus athetized 334-335 (apparatus). Paris borrows his brother Lycaon's corslet — the bowman owns none. ↩

21 line 346 — "long-shadowed spear" (δολιχόσκιον ἔγχος) — the fixed epithet of the thrown spear, always so rendered. ↩

22 lines 396–418 — Aristarchus athetized this whole exchange (apparatus, Line 396-418) — Helen seeing through the disguise, defying the goddess to her face ("Go sit beside him yourself... his wife — or else his slave"), and Aphrodite's naked threat. No other mortal in the Iliad speaks to a god like this; the scene lays bare what Aphrodite's "gifts" cost the one who receives them. Kept entire. ↩

23 line 424 — "laughter-loving Aphrodite" (φιλομειδὴς Ἀφροδίτη) — her fixed epithet, always so rendered. ↩

24 lines 424–425 — A goddess carrying a chair for a mortal woman — service so startling the translation lets the narrator's shock show ("a goddess!" renders the emphatic θεά). Zenodotus rewrote 423-426 into a single innocuous line, "and she herself went and sat opposite lord Alexandros" (apparatus, Line 423-426) — flattening exactly the scandal that gives the gesture its force: Aphrodite is herself procuring, and stoops to it. ↩

25 lines 428–436 — Helen's greeting to the rescued Paris — a death-wish, a taunt, and a mid-sentence collapse from "challenge him again" to "no — you would die." Aristarchus athetized 432-436 (apparatus). Kept: the broken movement of the speech is the psychology. ↩

26 line 445 — Kranae ("Rocky Isle") — the island where Paris and Helen first slept together on the flight from Sparta; possibly a proper name, possibly just "a rocky island." Ancient guesses ranged from Cythera to an islet off the Laconian coast later renamed Helene. ↩
