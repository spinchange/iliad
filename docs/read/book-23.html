Book XXIII

The ghost asks for burial and one last embrace; the pyre takes horses, dogs, and twelve men; and then — games: the army that has been killing for nine years spends a day competing for tripods and mares, quarreling justly, yielding handsomely, and laughing — while the man who arranged it all watches, having no future of his own.

Translator’s note on Book 23

Book 23 is the Iliad's demonstration that civilization is a thing people do on purpose, the day after the worst thing in their lives. Its first movement is all night and fire: the Myrmidons' three circuits of the body, the funeral feast where "blood ran in cupfuls," Achilles' refusal to wash until the pyre is built (the fast of Book 19 still governing his body), and the visit of the ghost — Patroclus "in everything like himself, in stature and the beautiful eyes and the voice," standing above the sleeper's head with the poem's most quietly devastating reproach: "You sleep — and you have forgotten me, Achilles" (note 4). What the ghost asks for is administration: burial, quickly, so the other souls will let him across the river; and one thing more — "give me your hand: I am weeping" — which the living man cannot deliver: the embrace closes on nothing, and the soul goes down "like smoke... squeaking," leaving Achilles with the poem's plainest statement of its afterlife: "a soul and an image — but the wits are not in it at all" (note 5). Homer's dead are real enough to make requests and too thin to hold.

The pyre-sequence keeps every terrible ledger the poem has opened: the hair vowed to the river Spercheius given to the dead instead (the vow of homecoming formally cancelled, note 7); and the twelve Trojan youths of the vow of 18.336-337, killed with the narrator's own verdict attached — "and evil was the work his heart intended" — while, two lines later, the gods quietly begin Book 24: Aphrodite keeping the dogs from Hector with rose-oil, Apollo roofing the corpse with cloud (note 8). The winds must be fetched by Iris from a dinner-party to make the pyre burn; and over the all-night fire the poem sets its most terrible simile of grief: Achilles mourning "as a father mourns, burning the bones of his son, a bridegroom" (note 9).

Then the games — two-thirds of the book, and the poem's whole social philosophy in action. Every crisis of the Iliad recurs here in miniature, and every one is resolved: a dispute over a prize (Antilochus's foul and Menelaus's oath-challenge) ends not in wrath but in yielding, a young man handing over the mare and an elder, melted "as dew softens the ears of ripening corn," handing it back (note 21); a spectators' quarrel (Idomeneus and Oilean Ajax) is stopped by — of all men — Achilles, the poem's original quarreler, now its arbiter (note 17); and the book ends with Agamemnon standing up for the javelin-throw and Achilles awarding him the prize uncontested — the two men whose fight over a prize began the epic closing it with a prize freely given (note 29). Around these, the poem's late tenderness is everywhere: Achilles' only smile (at Antilochus, note 20); Epeius's disarming self-knowledge ("no man can be best in everything"); Nestor given a prize for nothing but memory; the biography of a silver bowl that once ransomed Lycaon and is now raced for at the funeral of the man whose death doomed him (note 25); Ajax face-down in cattle-dung while the army "laughed at him sweetly." The dead man gets his games; the living get their order back; and the host of the funeral arranges prizes for a future — his own burial mound, his own bones in the same urn — that he has already scheduled (note 10). It is the gentlest book of the poem, and it earns its gentleness line by line over an open grave.

1So they mourned through the city. And the Achaeans,1

2when they had come to the ships and the Hellespont,

3scattered, each man to his own ship —

4but Achilles would not let the Myrmidons scatter:

5he spoke out among his war-loving companions:

6"Myrmidons of the fast horses, my trusted companions,

7let us not yet loose the single-hoofed horses from the cars:

8with the very horses and chariots let us drive close

9and mourn Patroclus: for that is the honor-right of the dead.

10Then, when we have taken our fill of the deadly lament,

11we will unyoke the horses, and all take supper here."

12So he spoke; and they wailed as one body, and Achilles led.

13Three times around the corpse they drove their well-maned horses,

14mourning; and among them Thetis stirred the desire of lament.

15The sands were wet, and the armor of the men was wet

16with tears: such a machine of rout they missed.

17And the son of Peleus led the thick lament among them,2

18laying his man-slaughtering hands on his companion's chest:

19"Rejoice, O Patroclus, even in the house of Hades:

20for now I am accomplishing all I promised you before —

21to drag Hector here and give him raw to the dogs,

22and before your pyre to cut the throats of twelve

23shining children of the Trojans, in my rage at your slaying."

24He spoke, and devised foul work against brilliant Hector,

25stretching him face-down in the dust beside the bier of Menoetius's son.

26And the men put off their armor, each his gear of glittering bronze,

27and loosed their high-necked horses, and sat down beside the ship

28of the swift-footed son of Aeacus, in their thousands;

29and he set the funeral feast before them, to their hearts' fill.

30Many shining oxen bellowed around the iron3

31as their throats were cut, and many sheep and bleating goats;

32and many white-tusked swine, blooming with fat,

33were stretched to singe across the flame of Hephaestus;

34and everywhere around the corpse the blood ran in cupfuls.

35But the lord, the swift-footed son of Peleus —

36the kings of the Achaeans led him to brilliant Agamemnon,

37persuading him with effort, his heart still angry for his companion.

38And when they came in their going to Agamemnon's hut,

39at once they ordered the clear-voiced heralds

40to set a great tripod over the fire, in hope of persuading

41the son of Peleus to wash off the clotted gore.

42But he refused them flatly, and swore an oath on it:

43"No — by Zeus, who is highest of the gods and best,

44it is not right that water come near this head

45before I lay Patroclus on the fire, and heap his mound,

46and cut my hair for him: since never a second time

47will such grief reach my heart, while I am among the living.

48For now — yes, let us yield to the hateful feast;

49and at dawn, lord of men Agamemnon, drive the men

50to bring in wood, and furnish all that is fitting

51for a dead man to have, going under the murky dark,

52so that untiring fire may burn him the sooner

53out of our eyes, and the army turn back to its work."

54So he spoke; and they listened to him well, and obeyed.

55Quickly, each company, they made the supper ready

56and feasted; and no man's appetite went short of the equal feast.

57And when they had put away the desire for drink and food,

58the rest went off to their rest, each man to his hut;

59but the son of Peleus, on the shore of the loud-roaring sea,

60lay groaning heavily, among his many Myrmidons,

61in a clear space, where the waves washed up along the beach —

62until sleep took hold of him, loosening the cares of his heart,

63poured sweet around him: for his shining limbs were spent

64with chasing Hector toward windy Ilion.

65And there came to him the soul of unhappy Patroclus,4

66in everything like the man himself — in stature, and the beautiful

67eyes, and the voice; and the same clothes were on his body.

68He stood above his head, and spoke a word to him:

69"You sleep — and you have forgotten me, Achilles.

70You were never careless of me living — only dead.

71Bury me with all speed, and let me pass the gates of Hades.

72The souls keep me at a distance — the images of the worn-out —

73and they will not yet let me join them across the river:

74I wander, just as I am, through the wide-gated house of Hades.

75And give me your hand: I am weeping. Never again

76shall I come back out of Hades, once you have given me my fire.

77Never again in life shall we two sit apart

78from our dear companions, making our plans. The hateful

79death-spirit has gaped around me — the one allotted at my very birth.

80And for you too the portion stands, Achilles like the gods:

81to die beneath the wall of the rich Trojans.

82And one thing more I will say, and charge you, if you will listen:

83do not lay my bones apart from yours, Achilles,

84but together — as we were raised together in your house,

85when Menoetius brought me, still a small boy,

86out of Opoeis to your country, for a grim killing:

87the day I killed the son of Amphidamas —

88a child's act; I did not mean it; I was angry over the dice.

89The horseman Peleus took me into his house then,

90and raised me with kindness, and named me your companion.

91So let one vessel enfold the bones of us both —

92the golden two-handled jar your lady mother gave you."

93Then in answer swift-footed Achilles spoke to him:5

94"Why have you come to me here, beloved head,

95and why do you charge me with all these things? For me,

96I will accomplish everything, and bow to what you ask.

97But stand nearer to me: even for a little, let us

98put our arms around each other and take our fill of deadly lament."

99So speaking, he reached out with his own hands —

100and grasped nothing: the soul, like smoke, was gone

101under the earth, squeaking. And Achilles started up, astonished,

102and drove his hands together, and spoke the word of mourning:

103"Ah then — so there is, even in the house of Hades,

104a soul and an image — but the wits are not in it at all!

105All night long the soul of unhappy Patroclus

106stood over me, weeping and wailing,

107and charged me point by point — and it was marvelously like him."

108So he spoke; and in them all he stirred the desire of lament;

109and rosy-fingered Dawn shone out upon them still

110mourning around the pitiful corpse. And lord Agamemnon

111drove out the mules and men from every hut

112to bring in wood; and a good man oversaw them —

113Meriones, the aide of kind-hearted Idomeneus.

114They went out with woodcutters' axes in their hands

115and well-braided ropes; and the mules went on before them;

116and long they climbed — uphill, downhill, sidehill, slantwise.

117But when they came to the spurs of many-springed Ida,

118at once they set to felling the high-crowned oaks

119with the long-edged bronze, in haste; and with a great crashing

120the trees came down. Then the Achaeans split them

121and lashed them behind the mules; and these tore up the ground with their feet,

122straining for the plain through the dense thickets.

123And all the woodcutters carried logs: for so commanded

124Meriones, the aide of kind-hearted Idomeneus.

125They laid them down along the shore in rows, at the place

126where Achilles planned the great mound for Patroclus — and for himself.6

127And when they had laid down measureless wood on every side,

128they sat where they were, all together, and waited. And Achilles

129at once commanded the war-loving Myrmidons

130to belt on their bronze, and each man yoke his horses;

131and they rose and got into their armor,

132and mounted their chariots, fighters and drivers both,

133the horsemen in front, and behind them followed a cloud of foot-soldiers,

134thousands; and in their midst his companions carried Patroclus,

135and they heaped the whole corpse with the locks of hair they cut

136and cast on him. And behind, brilliant Achilles held the head,

137grieving: a blameless companion he was sending down to Hades.

138And when they came to the place Achilles had appointed,

139they set him down, and quickly piled the wood to his heart's fill.

140Then swift-footed brilliant Achilles thought of one thing more:7

141standing apart from the pyre, he cut the tawny lock

142he had been growing long for the river Spercheius,

143and troubled, he spoke, looking out on the wine-dark sea:

144"Spercheius — for nothing did my father Peleus vow to you

145that when I came home there, to my dear fatherland,

146I would cut my hair for you, and offer a holy hecatomb,

147and sacrifice fifty rams into your very sources,

148where your sacred plot and your smoking altar stand.

149So the old man vowed; and you did not accomplish his intent.

150Now — since I am not going home to my dear fatherland —

151let me give my hair to the hero Patroclus, to carry with him."

152So speaking, he laid the hair in the hands of his dear

153companion; and in them all he stirred the desire of lament.

154And the light of the sun would have gone down on their mourning

155had not Achilles quickly stepped to Agamemnon's side:

156"Son of Atreus — you are the man whose word the army

157most obeys. Of lament there can be fill enough.

158Disperse them now from the pyre; tell them to make

159their meal. We to whom the dead is nearest care

160will labor at the rest; and let the leaders stay beside us."

161Now when the lord of men Agamemnon heard him,8

162at once he dispersed the army among the balanced ships;

163and the mourners of the closest care stayed there and heaped the wood,

164and made a pyre a hundred feet this way and that,

165and on the pyre's height they laid the corpse, grieving at heart.

166Many fat sheep and shambling twist-horned cattle

167they flayed and dressed before the pyre; and from them all

168great-hearted Achilles took the fat, and wrapped the corpse

169from head to foot, and heaped the flayed carcasses around him.

170And he set two-handled jars of honey and of oil

171leaning against the bier; and four strong-necked horses

172he flung on the pyre with violence, groaning aloud.

173Nine dogs the lord had, fed from his table;

174and of these he cut the throats of two, and flung them on;

175and twelve noble sons of the great-hearted Trojans

176he butchered with the bronze — and evil was the work his heart intended —

177and into it all he threw the iron strength of fire, to feed.

178Then he wailed aloud, and called his dear companion by name:

179"Rejoice, O Patroclus, even in the house of Hades:

180for now I am accomplishing all I promised you before.

181Twelve noble sons of the great-hearted Trojans —

182the fire is eating all of them with you. But Hector,

183Priam's son, I will not give to the fire to feed on — but to the dogs."

184So he spoke, threatening. But the dogs did not deal with Hector:

185the daughter of Zeus, Aphrodite, kept the dogs from him

186by day and by night, and anointed him with oil of roses,

187ambrosial, so the dragging would not tear him;

188and Phoebus Apollo drew a dark-blue cloud

189down from heaven to the plain, and covered all the ground

190the corpse lay over, so the sun's strength would not

191wither the flesh too soon on his sinews and limbs.

192But the pyre of dead Patroclus would not kindle.9

193Then swift-footed brilliant Achilles thought of one thing more:

194standing apart from the pyre, he prayed to the two winds,

195Boreas and Zephyrus, and promised them fine offerings;

196and pouring many libations from a golden cup he begged them

197to come, so the dead would blaze up in the fire

198with all speed, and the wood take flame. And quickly Iris,

199hearing his prayers, went messenger to the winds.

200They were all indoors at the house of gusting Zephyrus,

201feasting at a banquet, when Iris came running

202and stood on the stone threshold. And the moment their eyes saw her

203they all sprang up, and each one called her to himself.

204But she refused to sit, and said her word:

205"No seat for me: I am away over the streams of Ocean

206to the Aethiopians' land, where they are offering hecatombs

207to the immortals — so I too may share the feast.

208But Achilles is praying to Boreas and loud Zephyrus

209to come, and he promises you fine offerings,

210so you may rouse the pyre to burning, where lies

211Patroclus, whom all the Achaeans mourn aloud."

212So speaking she went her way; and they rose up

213with an unearthly roar, driving the clouds in rout before them.

214Quickly they came to the sea, to blow across it, and the wave

215swelled under their shrill blast; and they reached deep-soiled Troy

216and fell upon the pyre; and the unearthly fire howled huge.

217All night long together they beat the flame of the pyre,

218blowing shrill; and all night long swift Achilles,

219taking a two-handled cup from a golden bowl,

220drew wine and poured it on the ground, and soaked the earth,

221calling upon the soul of unhappy Patroclus.

222As a father mourns, burning the bones of his son,

223a bridegroom, whose death has grieved his unhappy parents:

224so Achilles mourned, burning the bones of his companion,

225dragging himself around the pyre, groaning without pause.

226At the hour the morning star goes forth to herald light over the earth,

227and behind it Dawn in her saffron robe spreads out across the sea,

228at that hour the pyre died down, and the flame was done.

229And the winds set out again, to go back home

230across the Thracian sea; and it groaned, seething with swell.

231And the son of Peleus turned a little from the burning-place

232and lay down, worn out; and sweet sleep leapt upon him.

233But the others gathered in a body around the son of Atreus,

234and the noise and tramp of their coming woke him;

235and he sat upright, and spoke his word to them:

236"Son of Atreus, and you other chiefs of all the Achaeans:10

237first quench the burning-place with glistening wine,

238all of it, wherever the fire's strength has reached. Then

239let us gather the bones of Patroclus, Menoetius's son,

240telling them apart well — and they are easily known:

241he lay in the middle of the pyre; the others burned apart,

242at the edge, horses and men together.

243And let us lay his bones in a golden vessel, in a double

244fold of fat, until I myself am hidden in Hades.

245And no huge mound do I ask you to labor over —

246a seemly one, no more. Then afterward the Achaeans

247may build it wide and high — you who are left

248behind me in the many-benched ships."

249So he spoke; and they obeyed the swift-footed son of Peleus.

250First they quenched the burning-place with glistening wine,

251far as the flame had gone, and the ash had settled deep;

252and weeping, they gathered the white bones of their kindly companion

253into a golden vessel, in a double fold of fat,

254and laid it in the hut, and covered it with soft linen;

255and they drew the circle of the mound, and set its footing-stones

256around the pyre, and heaped the piled earth over it;

257and when they had heaped the mound, they turned to go. But Achilles

258held the army there, and sat them in a wide assembly,

259and out of the ships he brought his prizes: cauldrons and tripods,

260and horses and mules and strong heads of oxen,

261and deep-girdled women, and grey iron.

262For the swift charioteers first he set out shining prizes:11

263a woman skilled in blameless work, to lead away,

264and an eared tripod holding twenty-two measures —

265these for the first man; and for the second he set a mare,

266six years old, unbroken, carrying a mule foal;

267and for the third he set down a cauldron untouched by fire,

268a beautiful one, holding four measures, white as new;

269and for the fourth he set out two talents of gold;

271Then he stood upright, and spoke his word among the Argives:

272"Son of Atreus, and you other strong-greaved Achaeans:12

273these prizes lie in the gathering, waiting for the charioteers.

274If we Achaeans were competing now for any other man,

275I would take the first prize myself, and carry it to my hut:

276you know how far my horses surpass all others in their worth —

277for they are immortal: Poseidon gave them

278to my father Peleus, and he passed them into my hands.

279But I will stay aside, I and my single-hoofed horses:

280such is the driver whose glory they have lost —

281the gentle one, who so many times poured smooth oil

282down their manes, when he had washed them in shining water.

283They stand and mourn him now; their manes trail

284on the ground; they stand with grief in their hearts.

285But the rest of you make ready through the army — whoever

286of the Achaeans trusts his horses and his bolted car."

287So spoke the son of Peleus, and the swift charioteers gathered.13

288Up first, far first, rose Eumelus, lord of men,

289Admetus's dear son, supreme in horsemanship;

290and after him rose Tydeus's son, strong Diomedes,

291and led under the yoke the horses of Tros, which he once

293and after him rose Atreus's son, fair-haired Menelaus,

294the Zeus-born, and led beneath the yoke two swift horses —

295Aethe, Agamemnon's mare, and his own Podargus.

296Her Echepolus, Anchises' son, gave to Agamemnon

297as a gift, so he need not follow him to windy Ilion

298but stay at home and take his ease: for Zeus had given him

299great wealth, and he lived in Sicyon of the wide dancing-floors.

300Her Menelaus led beneath the yoke, athirst for the running.

301And fourth, Antilochus made ready his well-maned horses —

302the shining son of Nestor, the great-hearted lord,

303Neleus's son; and horses bred in Pylos

304drew his car, quick-footed. And his father, standing by him,

305spoke good counsel to a son who knew it for himself:

306"Antilochus — young as you are, Zeus and Poseidon

307have loved you, and taught you horsemanship

308of every kind. So there is no great need to teach you:

309you know well how to wheel the turning-post. But your horses

310are the slowest in the running; so I expect a sorry business.

311Their horses are faster — but the men themselves

312do not know more of cunning than you do.

313Come then, dear boy: lay up cunning in your heart,

314cunning of every kind, so the prizes do not slip past you.

315By cunning, far more than by force, is the woodcutter better;

316by cunning the helmsman on the wine-dark sea

317holds his swift ship on course when the winds are tearing at it;

318by cunning charioteer outdoes charioteer.

319One man, trusting in his horses and his car,

320swings wide, mindlessly, this way and that,

321and his horses wander down the course, and he cannot hold them.

322But the man who knows his advantages, driving lesser horses,

323keeps his eye on the post and turns it close, and never forgets

324how much rein of oxhide to give at the start —

325he holds his line securely, and watches the man in front.

326And the mark itself I will tell you — plain to see; you cannot miss it.

327A dry stump stands there, a fathom high above the ground,

328of oak or of pine, not rotted by the rain,

329and two white stones are set against it, one on either side,

330at the joining of the track; and around it the racing-ground is smooth.

331Either it is the grave-mark of a man dead long ago,

332or it was set for a turning-post by men before our time;

333and now swift-footed brilliant Achilles has made it the goal.

334Drive your car and horses grazing hard against it,

335and lean your own body, in the well-plaited chariot,

336a little to their left; and the right-hand horse —

337goad him and call to him, and give him rein from your hands.

338And let your left-hand horse graze the post so close

339that the hub of the well-wrought wheel seems to touch

340its very edge — but take care not to catch the stone,

341or you will wound the horses and wreck the car:

342joy for the others, and reproach for you.

343Dear boy — keep your wits about you; be watchful.

344For if at the turn you slip past them in your pursuit,

345there is no man who will catch you with a lunge, or pass you —

346not though he drove brilliant Arion behind you,

347Adrastus's swift horse, whose line was from the gods,

348or the horses of Laomedon, best of the breed reared here."

349So speaking, Nestor Neleus's son sat back down in his place,

351And Meriones was the fifth to make ready his well-maned horses.14

352They mounted their chariots, and cast in their lots;

353and Achilles shook them, and out sprang the lot of Nestor's son,

354Antilochus; and after him lord Eumelus drew;

355and after him Atreus's son, spear-famed Menelaus;

400had put fury in his horses, and set the glory on him.

401And after him came Atreus's son, fair-haired Menelaus.

414After them! Press hard — fast as you can!

415And I myself will devise it and manage it —

416a way to slip past in the narrow of the road. It will not escape me."

417So he spoke; and in dread at their master's rebuke

418they ran the harder for a little while; and quickly then

419Antilochus, staunch in battle, saw the narrows of the sunken road.

420There was a break in the ground, where winter-gathered water

421had torn away the track and hollowed the whole place deep;

422there Menelaus held his line, shy of fouling wheels;

423but Antilochus swerved his single-hoofed horses and drove

424outside the track, and pressed him hard, bearing in a little.

425And the son of Atreus was afraid, and shouted to Antilochus:

426"Antilochus — this is mad driving! Hold in your horses!

428you'll wreck the both of us, fouling my car!"

429So he spoke; but Antilochus drove all the harder,

430laying on the goad, like a man who had not heard.

431As far as the swing of a discus flung from the shoulder,

432hurled by a young man testing the strength of his youth,

433so far they ran on together; and then the mares of Atreus's son

434dropped back: he slackened his driving by his own will,

435for fear the single-hoofed horses would collide in the road

436and overturn the well-plaited cars, and the men themselves

437go down in the dust for all their straining after victory.

438And fair-haired Menelaus called after him in rebuke:

439"Antilochus — no mortal man alive is more ruinous than you!

440Go, and be damned — we Achaeans were wrong to call you sane.

441But even so, you shall not carry off the prize without an oath."

442So he spoke, and called out to his horses:

443"Don't hang back on me now, don't stand there grieving!

444Their feet and knees will tire before yours do:

445the pair of them are past their youth."

446So he spoke; and in dread at their master's rebuke

447they ran the harder, and quickly closed the gap.

448Meanwhile the Argives, sitting in the assembly, watched17

449for the horses; and these came flying through the dust of the plain.

450And the first to make them out was Idomeneus, leader of the Cretans:

451for he sat outside the gathering, highest up, on a lookout;

452and hearing the voice of a driver shouting, far off as he was,

453he knew it; and he made out a horse, clear in the lead,

454chestnut over all the rest of him, except that on his forehead

455a white blaze was set, round as the full moon.

456And he stood upright, and spoke his word among the Argives:

457"Friends — leaders and counselors of the Argives —

458am I the only one who sees the horses, or do you see them too?

459Other horses seem to me to be in front now —

460another driver shows. The mares must have come to grief

461out on the plain — the ones that led on the way out:

462I saw them rounding the turn-post first,

463and now I cannot see them anywhere, though my eyes

464go searching everywhere across the Trojan plain.

465Did the reins escape their driver, so he could not

466hold them well around the post, and failed to make the turn?

467He was thrown out there, I think, and the chariot wrecked,

468and the mares bolted off the course, their hearts gone wild.

470make it out for certain. The man in front seems to me

471to be Aetolian by birth — and a lord among the Argives —

472the son of horse-taming Tydeus, strong Diomedes."

473Then swift Ajax, Oileus's son, rounded on him shamefully:

474"Idomeneus — why this blather before the time? The mares

475are far away still, sweeping the wide plain.

476You are not so much the youngest among the Argives,

477and the eyes in your head are not the sharpest lookers —

478yet you are always blathering. There is no need for you

479to blather here: better men than you are present.

480The mares in front are the same that led before —

481Eumelus's; and it is he who stands there holding the reins."

483"Ajax — champion of insult, wit of straw — in all things else

485Come then: let us wager a tripod or a cauldron,

486and let us both make Agamemnon, Atreus's son, our judge —

487whose mares are in front. You will learn as you pay."

488So he spoke; and at once swift Ajax, Oileus's son,

489rose up in anger to answer with hard words —

491had not Achilles himself stood up, and said his word:

492"No longer answer each other with hard words,

493Ajax — Idomeneus — with evil words: it is not seemly.

494You would be indignant at another man who acted so.

495Sit down in the gathering, and watch

496for the horses: they are straining for victory, and they

497will be here soon enough. Then each of you can know

498the Argive horses — who is behind, and who in front."

499So he spoke — and the son of Tydeus came driving in nearly upon them,18

500laying on the whip down from the shoulder, stroke on stroke; and his horses

501lifted high, going fast, devouring the way.

502And the spatters of dust rained without pause on the driver,

503and the chariot, plated with gold and tin,

504ran hard on the heels of the quick-footed horses — and behind them

505the tires left hardly a trace of wheel-track

506in the fine dust: the pair of them flew, straining.

507He pulled up in the middle of the gathering, the sweat

508streaming to the ground from his horses' necks and chests;

509and he himself leapt to the ground from his shining car

510and leaned the whip against the yoke. Nor did strong Sthenelus

511delay: he seized the prize at once, eagerly,

512and gave the woman to his high-hearted companions to lead,

513and the eared tripod to carry; and he loosed the horses from the yoke.

514Next after him Antilochus of Neleus's line drove in his horses —19

516yet even so Menelaus held his swift horses close behind.

517As far as a horse stands from the wheel, when he strains

518drawing his master over the plain with the chariot,

519and the tips of his tail-hairs brush against the tire,

521as he sweeps the wide plain:

522so far was Menelaus behind blameless Antilochus —

525Agamemnon's lovely-maned mare, was rising still;

538the second; and let the son of Tydeus carry off the first."

539So he spoke; and they all approved what he commanded.

543"Achilles, I will be very angry with you if you accomplish

576'Menelaus beat Antilochus down with lies

584lay your hand on your horses, and swear by the earth-upholder,

587"Bear with me now: I am far younger

589You know how a young man's trespasses come about:

590his mind is the quicker, but his judgment thin.

592I give her to you myself. And if you asked of me some greater thing

593from my own stores besides, I would choose to give it to you

594at once, this moment, Zeus-nurtured — rather than fall

596So spoke great-hearted Nestor's son, and led the mare

597and put her into Menelaus's hands. And that man's heart

598was warmed like the dew about the ears of ripening corn,

599when the fields are bristling: so, Menelaus,

601And he spoke to him in answer winged words:

602"Antilochus — now I will yield to you myself, out of my anger:

603for you were never scattered or light of mind before.

604This once, youth got the better of your judgment;

605the next time, take care not to outscheme your betters.

606No other man of the Achaeans would have soothed me quickly —

607but you have suffered much, and labored much for my sake,

608you and your good father and your brother.

609So I will be persuaded by your plea; and the mare —

610mine though she is, I will give her to you — so these men too may know

611that my heart was never overbearing and unbending."

612He spoke, and gave the mare to Noëmon, Antilochus's

613companion, to lead away; and took for himself the shining cauldron.

614And Meriones took up the two talents of gold,

615fourth, as he had driven. But the fifth prize was left over —

616the two-handled bowl. Achilles gave it to Nestor,

617carrying it to him across the gathering of the Argives, and said:

621outright: for you will not box, and you will not wrestle,

622nor enter for the javelins, nor race

623on foot: the hard weight of age is on you now."

624So speaking, he laid it in his hands; and he received it, rejoicing,

625and speaking winged words he addressed him:

626"Yes, child — all this you have said aright.

627My limbs are no longer sound, dear friend — my feet — nor do my arms

628swing light from my shoulders on either side.

629Would that I were young, and my strength as firm

630as when the Epeians buried lord Amarynceus

631at Buprasium, and his sons set out the king's prizes.

632There no man proved my equal — not of the Epeians,

633not of the Pylians themselves, not of the great-hearted Aetolians.

634In boxing I beat Clytomedes, Enops's son;

635at wrestling, Ancaeus of Pleuron, who stood up against me;

636Iphiclus I outran on foot, good runner though he was;

637and with the spear I out-threw Phyleus and Polydorus.

638Only with the horses did the two sons of Actor pass me —

639crowding ahead by force of numbers, grudging me the victory,

640because the biggest prizes had been left for the horses.

641They were twins: one held the reins — held the reins

642for good and all — while the other laid on the whip.

643Such I was once. Now let younger men take on

644such labors: I must bow to grim old age —

645though in my day I shone among the heroes.

646But go: honor your companion too with funeral games.

647This gift I receive gladly, and my heart rejoices

648that you remember me always as your kindly friend, and never forget

649the honor with which it is fitting I be honored among the Achaeans.

650And may the gods, in return, grant you grace to your heart's fill."

651So he spoke; and the son of Peleus went back through the great

652crowd of Achaeans, when he had heard out all the praise of Neleus's son.

657Then he stood upright, and spoke his word among the Argives:

658"Son of Atreus, and you other strong-greaved Achaeans:

660to put up their fists and trade blows. The man to whom Apollo

661grants endurance, and all the Achaeans acknowledge it,

665skilled in fist-fighting — Epeius, Panopeus's son;

669by winning with his fists. I claim to be the best.

670Is it not enough that I fall short in battle? There was never a way,

671it seems, for one man to be skilled in every work there is.

672For I tell you this, and it will be accomplished:

673I will split his skin clean open and stave his bones in.

674Let his next of kin stand waiting here in a body

678son of lord Mecisteus, Talaus's son,

715of bold hands; and the wet sweat ran down;

716and welts thick and many along their ribs and shoulders

Notes

1 lines 1–16 — The Myrmidons mourn mounted: three circuits of the body with horses and chariots — cavalry honors, the war-machine itself made to grieve — with Thetis stirring "the desire of lament" (γόου ἵμερος: grief in Homer is an appetite, roused, fed, and satisfied). Achilles' ruling is the book's charter: "that is the dead man's due" (γέρας θανόντων) — the prize of the dead, in the same word the living quarreled over in Book 1. ↩

2 lines 17–25 — The vow reported to its beneficiary: 22-23 repeat the wording of 18.336-337 (register kept), and Hector is laid "face-down in the dust beside the bier" — the outrage arranged like a grave-good. The poem keeps Achilles' promise-keeping and its horror in one frame: he is exact with the dead on both sides of hate. ↩

3 lines 26–58 — The funeral feast — "everywhere around the corpse the blood ran in cupfuls" — and the refusal to wash: the fast of Book 19 (19.208-210, 305-308) still governs Achilles' body; he yields to the "hateful feast" but swears off water "before I lay Patroclus on the fire." His oath contains the book's quiet self-knowledge: "never a second time will such grief reach my heart, while I am among the living" — true both ways, as he intends it and as the poem knows it: there is not much "while" left. ↩

4 lines 59–92 — The ghost. Patroclus comes "in everything like the man himself" — stature, the beautiful eyes, the voice, even the clothes — and opens with the gentlest rebuke in epic: "You sleep — and you have forgotten me, Achilles." His requests are a dead man's administration: burial now, because the other souls bar him from crossing the river (the poem's one glimpse of the afterlife's waiting-room); the hand ("I am weeping"); and the shared urn — grounded in the biography only now revealed: Patroclus came to Peleus's house as a child-killer, a boy who slew the son of Amphidamas "angry over the dice — a child's act; I did not mean it." The gentlest man in the poem was a refugee from his own temper: gentleness, the lines quietly say, was something he built. Aristarchus athetized 92 (the golden amphora, "which your lady mother gave you" — Dionysus's gift to Thetis in the later tradition; Aeschines' text lacked it), but the request stands: one vessel, two friends, the arrangement the Odyssey (24.74) confirms was kept. ↩

5 lines 93–108 — The failed embrace: Achilles reaches, "and grasped nothing: the soul, like smoke, was gone under the earth, squeaking." His waking cry is the plainest statement of Homeric eschatology: "so there is, even in the house of Hades, a soul and an image (ψυχὴ καὶ εἴδωλον) — but the wits (φρένες) are not in it at all." (Aristophanes athetized the line as too doctrinal; it is the shape of the whole scene.) The dead persist as form without force — enough to be loved, too little to be held: the exact measure of what the living owe them, which is everything this book does. ↩

6 lines 109–140 — The wood-train — Meriones's logistics, the mules, the felled oaks "with a great crashing" — ends at the shore where Achilles plans "the great mound for Patroclus — and for himself": his own funeral entering the arrangements as a fact needing no comment. The army heaps the corpse with cut hair; Achilles carries the head — chief mourner's station, as Thetis held his (18.71). ↩

7 lines 141–154 — The Spercheius lock: hair grown long for a homecoming vow — Peleus's fifty rams at the river's sources — cut now and given to the dead instead: "since I am not going home to my dear fatherland." The vow of return is formally transferred to the grave; no line in the book cancels Achilles' future more quietly. He says it looking at the sea — the road home, declined. ↩

8 lines 161–192 — The pyre, a hundred feet each way, and its freight: fat-wrapped corpse, honey and oil, four horses, two of the nine table-dogs — and the twelve Trojan youths of the vow, butchered with the narrator's own verdict attached: "and evil was the work his heart intended" (κακὰ δὲ φρεσὶ μήδετο ἔργα). The poem does not flinch from human sacrifice and does not condone it: one clause of judgment, unique in the epic, set exactly here. And then, within eight lines, Book 24 quietly begins: Aphrodite keeping the dogs from Hector "by day and by night," the oil of roses, Apollo's sheltering cloud — the gods already voting against the rage they have stopped assisting. ↩

9 lines 193–232 — The winds must be asked: Iris fetches Boreas and Zephyrus from a banquet (declining a seat — she is expected at the Aethiopians': divine society continuing around the edges of grief), and they burn the pyre all night while Achilles pours wine and calls the soul. Over it, the poem's most terrible simile of mourning: "As a father mourns, burning the bones of his son, a bridegroom" — the worst grief the Greek imagination could name, a child dead at the threshold of marriage — given to a friend for a friend, and doubled by the fact that the mourner's own father will soon have precisely this grief and no body to burn. ↩

10 lines 236–257 — The white bones "of their kindly companion" (ἐνηέος — the dead man's adjective) into the golden vessel "until I myself am hidden in Hades"; the mound deliberately modest, "then afterward the Achaeans may build it wide and high — you who are left behind me": Achilles designs his own tomb as an afterthought to his friend's, assigning the enlargement to survivors he has already stopped counting himself among. The funeral is double throughout; only one of the dead is present. ↩

11 lines 257–271 — The pivot of the book: "But Achilles held the army there" — grief converted, by decree, into games. The prize-list is the Iliad's economy in miniature: women, tripods, mares, cauldrons, gold, iron — the same currencies men died over, now to be won by rule. ↩

12 lines 271–286 — Achilles' abdication: his immortal horses would win, so he withdraws — and the reason is their mourning: they stand with manes trailing the ground for "the gentle one" (ἠπίου), who washed them and oiled their manes. Patroclus's epithet-world (kindly, gentle) persists in the memory of animals; the horses that wept at 17.426-440 are still in mourning, and their grief disqualifies the field's best entry. ↩

13 lines 287–351 — Nestor's sermon on μῆτις — "by cunning, far more than by force, is the woodcutter better; by cunning the helmsman... by cunning charioteer outdoes charioteer" — the poem's most explicit praise of intelligence over strength, delivered to a son whose horses are slowest. The turn-post itself is the book's emblem: "either it is the grave-mark of a man dead long ago, or it was set for a turning-post by men before our time; and now Achilles has made it the goal" — a monument whose meaning is already lost, reused as sport: what tombs become, in the long run the poem knows all about. The driving instructions (lean left, goad the right-hand horse, graze the post with the hub) are the oldest coaching manual in literature. ↩

14 lines 352–373 — The start: lots drawn (Antilochus first — the inside track his tactics will need), Phoenix as umpire "to keep the running in memory and report the truth," and the break — dust like cloud or stormwind, manes streaming, the cars now hugging earth, now airborne, "and each man's heart was pounding in his hunger for victory." The poem's joy in speed, absent since the chariots ran for Hector's life, is given back clean. ↩

15 lines 373–402 — The gods play the race as they played the war: Apollo dashes the whip from Diomedes (still angry from Book 5, perhaps — or just partisan); Athena returns it and breaks Eumelus's yoke — the best horseman skinned across mouth and elbows in the dust. Divine interference, which cost Patroclus his life and Hector his, here costs a man a mare and his skin: the same machinery at play-scale, safe at last. ↩

16 lines 402–447 — Antilochus's foul: the speech to his horses (threatening them with Nestor's knife — charioteer's hyperbole; "Aethe will pour shame over you: a female!"), the sunken narrows, and the swerve that forces Menelaus to yield or wreck. The narrator is exact: Menelaus dropped back "by his own will," the prudence of the elder buying both men's safety — and his shout ("you shall not carry off the prize without an oath") files the case the assembly will hear. The young man drives "like a man who had not heard": Nestor's cunning-lecture, over-learned in one generation. ↩

17 lines 448–499 — The spectators' quarrel — Idomeneus's sharp-eyed call (the chestnut with the moon-round blaze), Oilean Ajax's gratuitous insult, the proposed wager with Agamemnon as judge — is Book 1 in a teacup: two proud men, a prize, an arbiter. And it is stopped by Achilles — "it is not seemly... You would be indignant at another man who acted so" — the poem's original quarreler now its peacemaker, speaking to others the sentence nobody managed to speak to him. The reformation is complete and completely unremarked: the poem lets you notice it yourself. ↩

18 lines 499–514 — Diomedes wins — whip-strokes "down from the shoulder," the gold-and-tin car running the horses' heels down, wheel-tracks barely printed in the fine dust (speed measured by what it fails to leave), and the cool gesture at the end: the whip leaned against the yoke. Sthenelus takes the woman and the tripod: Book 5's team, intact, at play. ↩

19 lines 514–533 — The placings, with the poem's own verdict on Antilochus: "by craft, not speed at all" (κέρδεσιν, οὔ τι τάχει γε). The tail-hairs-brushing-the-tire figure measures Menelaus's closing surge; Meriones a spear-cast back; and last, dragging his wreck, "the best man" — Eumelus, whose excellence the race no longer records, which is why the next scene has to. ↩

20 lines 534–566 — Justice, round one: Achilles proposes second prize for Eumelus (pity ranking worth above result), and Antilochus objects on procedure — the mare is won; compensate from your own stores — with the challenge-tag of a man standing on right ("let any man who wants her try me — hand to hand"). And Achilles smiled — his only smile in the Iliad — "delighting in Antilochus, because he was his dear companion." The word ἑταῖρος, freighted with all the book's grief, spent here on the living: the smile is the poem's first evidence that Achilles can still be reached. Eumelus gets Asteropaeus's corslet; everyone is paid; nobody is wronged. ↩

21 lines 566–613 — Justice, round two — the poem's model trial. Menelaus rises "grieved at heart," takes the herald's staff (procedure!), and offers two courses: judgment by the leaders, "and no favor either way," or — better — the self-executing oath: stand before your horses, whip in hand, touch the team, and swear by the earth-upholder, the earth-shaker, that the foul was not deliberate. Antilochus folds precisely at the oath — he will not perjure — with the young man's confession for all time: "his mind is the quicker, but his judgment thin" — and hands over the mare with his whole inheritance in one sentence rather than "be wrong before the gods." And Menelaus melts — the dew-on-the- ripening-corn simile, the gentlest in the poem, for the untensing of an angry heart — and gives the mare back. Compare Book 1: a prize, a grievance, an assembly — and there, wrath; here, yielding met by yielding. This scene is the Iliad showing what the quarrel of Achilles and Agamemnon should have been. ↩

22 lines 615–652 — Nestor's prize for nothing: the leftover bowl, given "a memorial of the burial of Patroclus: for never again will you see him among the Argives." The old man's answer is his career in miniature — the games for Amarynceus at Buprasium, four victories named, and the one defeat: the Molione twins who beat him "by force of numbers... one held the reins — held the reins for good and all — while the other laid on the whip" (the doubled phrase is the Greek's own, an old man's groove in the telling). His thanks name the thing the whole book trades in: "my heart rejoices that you remember me always as your kindly friend" (ἐνηέος — Patroclus's word, 252). Honor, in the end, is being remembered kindly: the games' true currency, and the poem's. ↩

23 lines 653–700 — The boxing: Epeius's magnificent boast with its disarming core — "Is it not enough that I fall short in battle? There was never a way for one man to be skilled in every work there is" — the most honest self-assessment in the epic (this is the man who will build the wooden horse: his skill was coming). Euryalus's pedigree is a funeral-games résumé — he won at the burial of "fallen Oedipus" at Thebes, the Iliad's only notice of Oedipus, dead at home and buried with games. One counter-punch ends it; the fish-leap simile under Boreas's shiver lands him; and Epeius "took him in his hands and set him upright" — courtesy inside violence, the book's whole ethic in one gesture. ↩

24 lines 700–740 — The wrestling, with the poem's price-list attached: the tripod worth twelve oxen; the woman "skilled in many kinds of work," worth four. The Iliad states its world's arithmetic without comment, and no reader is meant to pass it in silence: this is the economy the whole war runs on. Ajax and Odysseus lock like "gable-rafters a famous builder joins"; two falls — Odysseus's knee-trick, then the failed lift and mutual tumble — and Achilles calls it: "Victory to you both." Force and cunning, the poem's two great modes, wrestle to a draw and are sent to dinner. ↩

25 lines 740–798 — The foot-race, and the bowl with a biography: Sidonian work, Phoenician freight, guest- gift to Thoas, and then — the price of Lycaon, paid by Euneus to Patroclus. The object that once bought Lycaon's life (21.40-41) is now the prize at the funeral of the man it was paid to, raced for while Lycaon lies in the river. Objects in Homer remember what men would rather not. The race itself is the book's comedy: Odysseus running so close he treads Ajax's prints "before the dust could settle," breathing on his head (with the weaving-woman simile — the poem's most domestic measure of distance); the prayer; Athena's trip; Ajax face-down in the sacrificial ox-dung, spitting, with the complaint that convulses the army: "she stands by Odysseus like a mother!" — "and they all laughed at him sweetly" (ἡδύ). Laughter, which this poem has mostly given to gods and scorners, comes back to men, sweet, at a funeral. Antilochus's graceful last-place speech ("the immortals honor the elder generation... hard work to race him — except Achilles") wins a half-talent bonus for pure charm. ↩

26 lines 798–826 — The armed duel — for Sarpedon's armor, Patroclus's greatest spoil (16.663-665) — is the one event that edges too close to the real thing: first blood "through armor and dark blood" for Asteropaeus's Thracian sword. Ajax and Diomedes close "glaring terribly; and awe held all the Achaeans"; Diomedes keeps reaching over the shield-rim for the neck — the exact stroke that killed Hector — and the Achaeans, "in fear for Ajax," stop it. Play that can kill is called before it does: the community knows where the line is now. (Aristophanes' text varied the victory-terms; Aristarchus athetized 806 and 810; 824-825 were athetized too — antiquity was uneasy exactly where the game is uneasy.) ↩

27 lines 826–849 — The weight-throw: the prize is the implement — Eëtion's self-smelted iron lump, war-booty from Andromache's father's city, now five years' supply for a winner's shepherd and plowman: iron passing from a king's sport through sack and spoil to farm-tools — the poem's whole material history in one object. Polypoetes's cast, measured by a herdsman's throwing-staff "spinning away through the herds," wins it for the ships. ↩

28 lines 850–883 — The archery: a dove on a cord at the mast-head. Teucer shoots without vowing the hecatomb — "Apollo grudged him that" — and cuts the cord; Meriones, arrow already in hand, vows, and takes the bird under the wing as she circles the clouds. Piety as marksmanship's missing variable, scored like the contest it is; and the poem's last death — after Hector, after the twelve — is a dove's, drooping her neck on the mast "and the life flew swift from her limbs." The Iliad's killing ends in miniature, feathered, at a game. ↩

29 lines 884–897 — The close: Agamemnon stands up for the javelin — and Achilles awards him the prize without a throw: "we know how far you surpass all others... take this prize" — with the spear to Meriones. The poem's first quarrel was these two men over a prize; its last prize passes between them ungrudged, unthrown-for, by the loser of Book 1 now presiding in perfect courtesy. Agamemnon hands the cauldron to Talthybius — the herald who once came to Achilles' hut to take Briseis (1.320-348) now receives, from Agamemnon's own hands, a gift born of Achilles' graciousness. The circle shuts without a word of comment: the games have done what nine years of war could not. All that remains is a corpse behind the hut, and a father across the plain — Book 24. ↩
