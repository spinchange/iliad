Book XXIV

The gods vote against the rage; a mother tells her son to eat, to sleep, to love; an old man kisses the hands that killed his sons — and the two of them weep, eat, and look at each other. The poem that began with the wrath ends with a burial done properly.

Translator’s note on Book 24

The Iliad does not end with a fall of a city or a death of a hero. It ends with an act of mercy so difficult that heaven has to engineer it and two broken men have to perform it — and with a funeral. Book 24 begins where Book 23's community ended: everyone else asleep, Achilles alone on the beach, cycling through his postures of grief, harnessing at each dawn to drag Hector three times round the tomb — and going nowhere, because Apollo keeps the corpse whole with the golden aegis. The mutilation has become a machine that grinds and does not feed. The gods' council states the poem's verdict through Apollo's indictment — Achilles "ravages dumb earth in his fury," having lost pity and that aidōs "that harms men greatly, and profits them"; even a man who loses a brother or a son "weeps, and laments, and lets go: for the Fates gave mankind an enduring heart" (note 3). Zeus's ruling refuses both theft and equal honor: Hector was dearest of Troy's mortals to the gods; Achilles shall have the glory — of giving him back. Mercy is arranged as an honor, because that is the only currency the recipient still accepts (note 4).

The double embassy — Thetis down to Achilles, Iris down to Priam — produces the book's two impossible journeys: a mother telling her doomed son to eat, sleep, and lie with a woman, "for you will not be with me long" (note 6); and an old man driving a treasure-wagon at night into the camp of the man who has killed his sons, against his wife's plea (her wish to eat Achilles' liver raw is the third and most terrible of the poem's raw-eating wishes, because it is hers; note 8). Hermes — the god whose dearest thing is "to be man's companion" — walks Priam through the sleeping army disguised as a princely squire with his first beard, and delivers, along the way, the poem's kindest sentence: Hector lies twelve days unburied and "dewy-fresh... the blessed gods care for your son though he is nothing now but a dead man, because they loved him at heart" (note 13).

Then the scene the whole epic exists for. Priam enters unnoticed, clasps Achilles' knees, and kisses "the terrible, man-slaughtering hands that had killed so many of his sons" — while the poem compares him, the suppliant king, to a murderer arriving in exile at a rich man's hall: the roles of killer and victim deliberately exchanged in the simile (note 15). "Remember your father" opens the plea that ends: "I have endured what no other mortal on earth has yet endured: to lift to my lips the hand of the man who killed my son." And the two of them weep together — Priam for Hector, Achilles "now for his father, now again for Patroclus" — two griefs, one sound, filling the house (note 17). What follows is the consolation of the two jars — no unmixed good exists for mortals; Peleus's splendor and Priam's fifty sons end the same way; "Bear it... you will not raise him up" — and then supper, and the strangest, most humane moment in Homer: the two enemies simply look at each other, each marveling at what the other is, "and when they had taken their fill of looking" (note 22). The old rage flickers once — "Do not provoke me now, old man" — and its live presence under the mercy is exactly what makes the mercy real (note 19).

The supplication-arc of the late books (Tros refused, Lycaon refused, Hector refused) closes with supplication accepted; and with it the poem's largest ring: an aged father comes with ransom to redeem his child from a king's wrath — Chryses, Book 1, line 12 — and this time the king says yes. Achilles washes and lifts the body himself, apologizes to Patroclus, promises a share of the ransom, and grants eleven days' truce with his hand on the old man's wrist "so his heart should not be afraid." Briseis, in one quiet clause, sleeps at his side again. Priam drives home past the ford of Xanthus (the register's line, its third appearance); Cassandra cries the city awake; and the three laments — Andromache with the head in her hands, Hecuba, and last Helen, mourning the one man in Troy who was kind to her — carry the poem to the pyre, the golden box, the purple robes, the mound raised under guard, the funeral feast. "So they busied themselves with the burial of Hector, the tamer of horses": the epic of wrath ends on rites performed — grief, at last, allowed to do its work properly (note 30).

Notes

1 lines 1–21 — The book opens on the community dissolving back into ordinary life — supper, sweet sleep — and one man exempt: Achilles cycling through the postures of grief (side, back, face-down, up and pacing), unreached by "sleep that subdues all things." The dawns are iterative — the Greek's tenses make the dragging a routine: harness, drag three times round the tomb, rest, leave him face-down. And the routine accomplishes nothing: Apollo with the golden aegis keeps the flesh whole, "pitying the man even in death." Rage has become machinery — running, grinding, and feeding no one. That futility is the book's premise. ↩

2 lines 22–30 — The divine majority wants the corpse stolen; three gods refuse — Hera, Poseidon, Athena — because of the poem's oldest wound: "the blindness of Alexandros, who insulted the goddesses when they came to his sheep-fold, and praised the one who fed his grievous lust." This is the Iliad's only mention of the Judgment of Paris, and Aristarchus athetized the passage — partly because the poem nowhere else knows the story, partly for the coarse word μαχλοσύνη ("lust"). Kept here: at the very end, the poem finally names the beginning — a beauty contest on a hillside as the seed of everything we have watched. ↩

3 lines 31–54 — Apollo's indictment is the poem's ethical summit-speech: Achilles has "destroyed pity," and lost aidōs — the shame-before-others "which harms men greatly, and profits them too": the double-edged social emotion that the whole epic runs on, defined in one line. The lion comparison strips the aristeia of its glamour (savagery "at the flocks of men, to take his feast"); and the norm is stated with terrible gentleness: a man may lose a brother or a son, "and yet, when he has wept and lamented, he lets go: for the Fates gave mankind an enduring heart" (τλητὸν γὰρ Μοῖραι θυμὸν θέσαν ἀνθρώποισιν) — endurance as the species-defining gift. The close is the book's moral in five words: "it is dumb earth he defiles in all his fury" — the dead man is beyond harm; the harm is all the living man's own. ↩

4 lines 55–76 — Hera's counter is pure rank (a breast-fed mortal cannot be priced with a goddess's son — with her swipe at Apollo's lyre-playing at the very wedding: "companion of evils, faithless forever"); and Zeus's ruling splits the difference with statesman's care: no equal honor — but Hector was "dearest to the gods of all mortals in Ilion" for the oldest reason in the poem's theology, the unfailing altar. The theft is rejected on practical grounds (Thetis's watch), but the true design appears in one phrase: "I attach this glory (κῦδος) to Achilles" — the return of the body is arranged not as defeat but as honor, mercy denominated in the only currency the recipient still values. ↩

5 lines 77–119 — Iris's dive "like the lead sinker that rides the horn of a field-ox down the line" — the most homely simile ever given a goddess: heaven's messenger as fishing-tackle. Thetis's veil is the poem's blackest object ("there is no garment blacker"): she dresses for her son's death to attend a council about his mercy. On Olympus the courtesies are exact — Athena yields her place, Hera hands the cup — the divine family arranging itself gently around a bereaved mother whose bereavement has not yet happened. ↩

6 lines 120–142 — Thetis delivers Zeus's rebuke and adds what no god scripted: "it is good to lie with a woman in love. For you will not be with me long..." (Aristarchus athetized 130-132 as indecorous; they are the scene's heart). A mother's last advice to a doomed son is bread, bed, and love — the whole mortal portion, urged because the portion is nearly spent. And Achilles' answer, after three books of iron: "So be it" (τῇδ' εἴη) — the wrath that filled an epic ends in two words of assent, the moment the demand comes from the one person he cannot refuse, carrying an honor he can accept. ↩

7 lines 143–187 — Iris's commission to Priam repeats Zeus's wording verbatim (146-158 = 175-187, kept identical here): heaven's mercy delivered twice, unedited — the poem's formulaic economy made providential. The house she enters is a tableau of collective ruin: sons weeping in the courtyard, daughters and sons' wives wailing within, and the king wrapped in his mantle with dung on his head and neck, "gathered on himself with his own hands." The message lands on a man already performing his own burial. ↩

8 lines 188–228 — The marriage scene: Priam tells Hecuba, and her answer is the poem's final maternal ferocity — the wish to "fasten on that man's liver, and eat it raw." The raw-eating triplet closes: Hera (4.35), Achilles (22.346), and now Hecuba — goddess of the winning side, killer, and grieving mother speaking one idiom; grief and hate converge in the same appetite. Priam's answer sets the book's terms: he has seen the god face to face; and if the price is death, "I choose it. Let Achilles kill me on the spot — once I have taken my son in my arms, and put away my fill of weeping." The embassy is a father's calculated exchange: his life, if need be, for one embrace. ↩

9 lines 229–264 — The ransom counted out (the poem loves the inventory: robes, cloaks, blankets, mantles, tunics, twelve of each; ten talents; tripods, cauldrons, the Thracian cup "not even this did the old man spare") — and then Priam's rage: first at the Trojans ("Have you no mourning of your own at home?"), then the terrible roll- call of the nine surviving sons — "liars, dancers, heroes of the beaten dance-floor, plunderers of their own people's lambs and kids" — against the dead: Mestor, Troilus, "and Hector, who was a god among men." Grief in old men, the poem knows, comes out as fury at the living for not being the dead. (Among the cursed: Helenus, Paris, Deiphobus — the city's remaining leadership, measured and found wanting by their father in public.) ↩

10 lines 265–282 — The yoking, in loving procedural detail — the box-wood yoke, the knob, the guide-rings, the nine-cubit lashing wound three times each way. The poem slows to craftsman's tempo exactly as it did for Hera's chariot and Priam's own libation to come: ritual care in the doing is how the Iliad marks an act as sacred. This wagon is an altar being assembled. ↩

11 lines 283–322 — Hecuba's libation-counsel — having failed to stop him, she arms him with piety: pour to Zeus, ask for the eagle on the right; "but if wide-seeing Zeus will not give you his messenger, I at least would not urge you." The omen comes: the dusky hunter-eagle, wings as wide "as the door of some rich man's high-roofed treasure-chamber" — the simile drawn from precisely the world of stored wealth Priam has just emptied for the ransom. And the exit: all Troy follows the wagon "mourning without end, as if he were going to his death" — which is, as far as anyone knows, the truth. ↩

12 lines 322–362 — Zeus's commission to Hermes defines the god: "to you, beyond all gods, it is dearest to be man's companion" (ἀνδρὶ ἑταιρίσσαι) — the escort-god, the one Olympian whose office is company. He comes as a princely youth "with the first down on his lip — the age when youth has its highest grace," and the meeting at the ford by Ilus's tomb, at dusk, is built as pure terror first: the herald's whisper, the old man's hair standing on his bent limbs — before the god takes his hand. Every earlier night-crossing in this poem (Book 10) ended in blood; the poem uses that memory to make this one tremble. ↩

13 lines 362–440 — The interrogation is Hermes at play — "You are the image of my own dear father"; the invented Myrmidon biography (seventh son of Polyctor, chosen by lot) — but its center is the report on the corpse: twelve days dead, dragged daily, and "his flesh does not decay... You would marvel yourself, if you came, at how he lies there dewy-fresh, the blood all washed from him, no stain anywhere; and every wound has closed... Such is the care the blessed gods take for your son, a dead man though he is — because they loved him at heart." The kindest sentence in the Iliad, spoken by a disguised god to a father in the dark. Priam's response makes it doctrine — "how good a thing it is to give the immortals their due gifts" — with the one heartbreaking parenthesis, "if ever he truly was" (εἴ ποτ' ἔην γε): grief's suspicion that the beloved might have been a dream. Hermes' refusal of the cup — no gifts "behind Achilles' back" — teaches with a light hand the order of things: even gods respect what is owed to Achilles; mercy must come from the man himself. ↩

14 lines 440–468 — Into the camp: sleep poured on the sentries, the gates opened — and the poem pauses to build Achilles' hut in full (fir beams, meadow-thatch, the palisade court) for the sake of one detail: the fir-wood bar that "three Achaeans would drive home and three would draw... though Achilles would ram it home alone." The door is a strength-gauge; Hermes opens it for an old man. His parting instructions are the supplication-manual: knees, and the names — father, mother, child. ↩

15 lines 469–485 — The entrance. Priam walks in unseen (a god's last gift), and the recognition is staged in gestures before any word: knees clasped, and the kiss — "the terrible, man-slaughtering hands that had killed so many of his sons" (χεῖρας δεινὰς ἀνδροφόνους): the fixed epithet of Hector's hands, then Achilles', pressed now to a father's lips. Then the simile that turns the world over: as when a man who has killed someone in his own country arrives in exile at a rich man's hall, "and wonder holds all who see him" — the innocent suppliant king compared to a murderer, the killer cast as the startled host. The reversal is the scene's meaning: guilt and grief have changed places so many times in this poem that only an exchanged simile can measure the moment. "And the others wondered too, and looked at one another" — the poem's people, like its audience, checking that everyone is seeing this. ↩

16 lines 486–507 — The plea. "Remember your father" — the argument Hermes assigned and Priam perfects: Peleus, same age, same encircling enemies — but with hope, because his son lives; "But I am doomed to the last": fifty sons, nineteen from one womb, and the guardian of all of them killed "days ago, fighting for his fatherland: Hector" — the name held back to the end of its clause. Then the two lines on which the Iliad closes its account of what humans can do: "I have endured what no other mortal on earth has yet endured — to lift to my lips the hand of the man who killed my son." Endurance — Apollo's τλητὸν θυμόν of line 49 — performed past its imaginable limit. ↩

17 lines 507–518 — The weeping. Priam's words stir in Achilles "the desire to weep for his father" — grief answering grief by way of substitution — and the poem's quietest, deepest sentence: "the two of them remembered: the one wept long and sore for man-slaughtering Hector, huddled at Achilles' feet, and Achilles wept for his own father — and now again for Patroclus; and the sound of their mourning filled the house." Two men weeping for different dead in one sound: shared mourning without shared objects — the only communion the Iliad believes in, and enough. When it is spent, Achilles lifts the old man up by the hand, "pitying the grey head and the grey beard" — the exact grey head and beard Priam foresaw defiled by his own dogs (22.74): the image returns, and is pitied instead. ↩

18 lines 518–553 — The consolation of the jars. Achilles' speech is the poem's theology spoken by its protagonist: "this is the way the gods have spun it for wretched mortals: to live in grief. And they themselves are free of care." Two jars on Zeus's floor — evils and blessings — and only two possible mixtures: mixed, or purely evil. No mortal draws unmixed good; the best life alternates. The proofs are the two fathers in the room: Peleus — wealth, kingship, a goddess-wife — and one doomed son who gives him "no care in his old age"; Priam — wealth and sons from Lesbos to the Hellespont — and this. The counsel is Apollo's from line 49, now spoken by the man it indicted: "Bear it... you will not raise him up." Achilles has become the teacher of the endurance he could not practice; saying it to Priam is how he begins to practice it. ↩

19 lines 552–573 — The flash of the old fire: Priam pushes — no chair while Hector lies uncared-for — and Achilles darkens: "Do not provoke me now, old man... or I may not spare even you... suppliant though you are, and may trespass against the commands of Zeus." The rage is not cured; it is contained, and knows itself. That self- knowledge — Achilles managing his own danger like a man banking a fire — is what makes the mercy trustworthy: the poem does not sentimentalize its hero into safety; it shows him holding himself, barely and deliberately, on the right side of the line. "And the old man was afraid, and obeyed." ↩

20 lines 574–597 — The ransoming, managed with the same self- knowledge: the body washed and anointed away from Priam's sight, "lest the grief in his heart not master his anger... and Achilles' own spirit be stirred, and he kill him" — mercy engineering its own conditions. Then Achilles himself lifts Hector onto the bier: the hands that dragged him now carry him — the body given back by the same strength that destroyed it. And the apology to Patroclus, with its scrupulous accounting ("the ransom he gave was not unworthy — and I will give you your share"): even now, the dead friend is a partner whose consent matters, and the transaction must be squared with the grave. ↩

21 lines 598–622 — Niobe. The paradigm is chosen with terrifying precision: the mother of twelve dead children "remembered food" — therefore eat. Inside it, the poem's darkest wonder-tale: nine days the children lay in blood "and there was no one to bury them: the son of Cronos had turned the people to stones. On the tenth day the gods of heaven buried them" — a world petrified by grief until heaven itself performs the funeral; and Niobe on Sipylus, "stone though she is, she broods on the griefs the gods gave her" (614-617 were athetized by Aristophanes and Aristarchus — the mourner turned mountain, weeping forever, kept here). Eating, in this logic, is not the end of grief but the body's assent to go on being mortal: Niobe ate; Achilles ate (24.475-476; 19's fast long broken); now Priam must. The shared meal is the sacrament of the enduring heart. ↩

22 lines 623–635 — The meal and the gaze. Achilles slaughters, his companions dress, Automedon serves, Achilles portions — the host's offices performed in full for the father of his enemy. And then the poem's most humane astonishment: "Priam marveled at Achilles — how big he was, how beautiful: like the gods to look upon; and Achilles marveled at Priam... And when they had taken their fill of looking at each other" (the verb of satiety the book has used for grief and food now given to sight). Two men who have destroyed each other's lives sit and behold each other as marvels. It is the closest the Iliad comes to peace: not reconciliation, not forgiveness — just the full, mutual, unhurried sight of another human being. ↩

23 lines 636–660 — Priam's first sleep-request since his son died ("my eyes have never closed... I have done nothing but groan"), and his first food ("Before this I had touched nothing" — his fast ending in the same hut where Achilles' ended). Achilles' bed-arrangement comes "with an edge in his voice" (ἐπικερτομέων): sleep outside, lest a wandering Achaean councilor see you and Agamemnon "delay the release of the body" — the grim comedy of a mercy that must be hidden from its own army, and a last flick at the king he quarreled with. Then the question no one could have predicted in Book 22: "how many days do you need for the burial of brilliant Hector?" ↩

24 lines 661–678 — The truce: nine days of mourning, the tenth for burial and the feast, the eleventh for the mound — "and on the twelfth we will fight again — if we must" (εἴ περ ἀνάγκη: the war resumed as necessity, not appetite; no one in this room wants it anymore). Achilles grants it with the book's definitive gesture: he takes the old man's right hand "at the wrist, so his heart should not be afraid" — the killer's hand as guarantee of safety. And then, in one clause with no comment: "and fair-cheeked Briseis lay beside him." The woman the wrath began over is home; Thetis's counsel (130-132) is kept; the poem's first quarrel closes in a domestic sentence. ↩

25 lines 679–695 — Hermes' midnight warning turns the ransom grammar around — "for you — alive — your sons left behind would pay three times as much": Priam himself is the last treasure in the camp — and the departure crosses "the ford of the fair-flowing river, eddying Xanthus, whom immortal Zeus begot": the register's line (14.433-434 = 21.1-2), at its third and last station. Hector was revived at this ford; the rout was split here; now his body crosses it homeward. Hermes leaves at the boundary; Dawn in her saffron robe spreads over the whole earth; the poem's last day begins. ↩

26 lines 696–724 — Cassandra — her only appearance in the poem's action, "like golden Aphrodite" — is Troy's watcher now (climbing Pergamus as Priam climbed the tower), and her cry is the city's whole relation to Hector in one sentence: "come and see Hector — if ever, while he lived, you rejoiced when he came home from battle." The gate-scene reprises Book 22's discovery in the key of ritual — wife and mother first, hands on his head — and Priam's crowd-order ("you may glut yourselves with weeping — when I have brought him home") is grief marshaled into procession. The bed, the singers, the antiphony: at last, the apparatus of proper mourning around the body that was promised to dogs. ↩

27 lines 725–749 — Andromache's lament — holding "the head of man-slaughtering Hector in her hands": the epithet of the killing hands (479, 509) given now to the head a widow cradles; the poem's fiercest word gentled by its final position. Her lament is all future tense: the child who "will not reach manhood," the city sacked, the ships, the slavery — and the alternative she imagines for Astyanax, flung from the wall by an Achaean avenging what "Hector killed — his brother, or his father, or his son": the fate the tradition indeed assigns him, spoken here as a mother's reasoned dread. Her last grievance is the tenderest in the poem: "you did not die in bed, reaching your arms to me, you spoke no close last word that I might have remembered always" — not the death, but the missing goodbye. ↩

28 lines 750–763 — Hecuba's lament is a theodicy: her sold sons (Samos, Imbros, "Lemnos of the smoke") against this one, killed, dragged — "though not even so did he raise him up" (the futility of the outrage stated by the victim's mother) — and yet lying "dewy-fresh, as if newly slain, like one whom Apollo of the silver bow has visited and killed with his gentle arrows." The gods' care, which Hermes reported to Priam in the dark, is now visible in Troy by torchlight: the body itself has become the proof that heaven loved him. ↩

29 lines 764–779 — Helen's lament, third and last — the poem's final speech given to the woman the war was fought over. Twenty years from home, and her praise of Hector is the smallest and most exact in the three laments: "never once did I hear from you an evil word, or a slighting one" — and when others reviled her, "you would turn them aside... with your gentle-mindedness and your gentle words." She mourns him as the last kindness in Troy — "there is no one else now in wide Troy gentle to me, or kind: they all shudder at me" — so that the poem's cause weeps for the poem's best man as her only friend. The crowd's groan answers her: the city, for one moment, grieving with the woman it shudders at. ↩

30 lines 780–804 — The ending: the truce honored (wood gathered nine days under Achilles' pledge), the pyre, rosy-fingered Dawn, the wine-quench and white bones — the rites following, step for step, the liturgy of Patroclus's funeral in Book 23: the two enemies buried by the same book of rites. The bones in the golden box, the soft purple robes, the hollow grave roofed with great stones, the mound raised quickly with watchers posted (grief efficient under threat — the war visible at the edges of the funeral), and the feast "in the house of Priam, the king nurtured of Zeus." Then the last line: "So they busied themselves with the burial of Hector, the tamer of horses" — the poem of wrath ends with no death, no fall, no victory: with rites performed, a feast eaten, and a single dead man given his due — the γέρας θανόντων Achilles named at 23.9. Everything else — Achilles' death, the horse, the sack, the fates Andromache numbered — stands just outside the final line, known to every hearer and refused entry. (Antiquity's rhapsodes felt the door ajar: a variant continuation ran "So they busied themselves with the burial of Hector; and there came the Amazon, the daughter of great-hearted Ares..." — splicing the epic onward into the Aethiopis. Our text ends where the poet ended: on the funeral, the feast, and the name of the man the city buried.) The Iliad begins with a father begging a king for his child and being refused; it ends with a father begging a king for his child and being answered. That is the distance the poem travels; the wrath of Achilles is the space between. ↩
