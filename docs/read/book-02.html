Book II

The lying Dream, the testing of the army, Thersites beaten — and the Catalogue of Ships.

Translator’s note on Book 2

Book 2 falls into two very different halves — the night-intrigue, failed test, and Thersites scene (1-493), and then the Catalogue of Ships with its Trojan counterpart (494-877) — and the main book-wide decision here was how to handle the second half. The Catalogue has been suspected since antiquity's critics and modern Analysts alike of being an originally independent composition, and several manuscripts omit the span entirely (see note 20). I have translated it as fully integral, and deliberately kept its litany register rather than flattening it into a gazetteer: one English line per Greek line as elsewhere, every place-epithet translated ("Thisbe of the many doves," "rocky Aulis"), leader-genealogies given their full ceremonial weight. The incantatory repetition of the ship-count refrains ("With him followed forty black ships") is preserved exactly where the Greek repeats and varied only where the Greek varies. The little narrative windows that open out of the list — Thamyris maimed by the Muses, Protesilaus' half-built house, Philoctetes on Lemnos, Nireus beautiful and negligible — are the Catalogue's own pulse, and the notes flag them rather than the geography.

The other live decision was tonal: Odysseus' two-tier crowd control (kings get "gentle words," commoners get the scepter across the back). Aristarchus athetized 193-197, and the passage is independently missing from Xenophon's quotation of the scene (note 11); I translate as printed and let the class asymmetry stand at full strength, since Thersites' beating immediately afterward depends on it. Thersites himself I have kept rough — "endless babble," "word-slinging insulter" — because the poem's own verdict on him is rough; this is the one place in the Iliad where the army's laughter is turned on a named common soldier, and softening it misrepresents the scene.

Zenodotus is unusually active in this book: he condensed the Dream's triple-repeated message (notes [4]), athetized Agamemnon's atē-speech (11-118, note 7), the Thersites portrait-lines, the Nireus anaphora (note 26), and the Achilles-passage inside the Catalogue (note 27). As in Book 1, the translation follows the printed vulgate text throughout and records atheteses in the notes.

Two carried-over threads from Book 1 to watch: Agamemnon here blames his quarrel with Achilles on Zeus-sent atē ("blindness," 111, and openly "it was I who began the anger," 378) — the same vocabulary Achilles used of him at 1.412; and the Catalogue's Achilles-paragraph (686-694) holds the plot's tension in place mid-list. New recurring epithets are footnoted at first occurrence and logged in translation/CONVENTIONS.md; the famous crux at 318 (did the god make the serpent-portent "conspicuous" or "invisible"?) is note 14, and the Salamis couplet with its ancient forgery accusation is note 23.

1The other gods, and men who fight from chariots,

2slept the whole night through; but sweet sleep did not hold Zeus.

3He was turning over in his mind how he might honor

4Achilles, and destroy many beside the Achaean ships.

5And this plan seemed to his heart the best:

6to send baneful Dream to Atreus' son Agamemnon.1

7And speaking winged words he addressed him:

8"Up, go, baneful Dream, to the Achaeans' swift ships.

9Come to the hut of Agamemnon, Atreus' son,

10and tell him everything exactly as I charge you.

11Bid him arm the long-haired Achaeans2

12in full force: for now he might take the wide-wayed city

13of the Trojans. The immortals who keep their homes on Olympus

14are no longer divided; Hera has bent them all

15by her begging, and sorrows are fastened on the Trojans."

16So he spoke, and Dream went when he had heard the word.

17Swiftly he came to the Achaeans' swift ships

18and went to Atreus' son Agamemnon. He found him

19sleeping in his hut, ambrosial slumber poured around him.

20He stood above his head in the likeness of Neleus' son

21Nestor, whom Agamemnon honored most of the elders;

22in his likeness the divine Dream spoke:

23"You sleep, son of war-minded Atreus, breaker of horses.3

24A man of counsel should not sleep the whole night through,

25a man to whom peoples are entrusted, who has so much to care for.

26Now hear me quickly: I am a messenger of Zeus,

27who far away cares greatly for you, and pities you.

28He bids you arm the long-haired Achaeans

29in full force: for now you might take the wide-wayed city

30of the Trojans. The immortals who keep their homes on Olympus

31are no longer divided; Hera has bent them all

32by her begging, and sorrows are fastened on the Trojans

33from Zeus. Keep this in your mind, and let no forgetting

34take you, when honey-hearted sleep lets you go."

35So he spoke and went away, and left him there

36believing in his heart things that were not to be:

37he thought he would take Priam's city on that very day —

38the fool: he did not know what work Zeus was planning,

39who meant to lay yet more griefs and groans

40on Trojans and Danaans alike through the strong encounters of war.

41He woke from sleep, the divine voice drifting around him,

42and sat upright, and pulled on his soft tunic,

43fine and new-made, and threw his great cloak around him,

44and beneath his shining feet he bound fair sandals,

45and slung about his shoulders the silver-studded sword,

46and took up the ancestral scepter, imperishable forever,

47and with it went down along the ships of the bronze-shirted Achaeans.

48Now the goddess Dawn went up to high Olympus,

49announcing light to Zeus and the immortals;

50and he ordered the clear-voiced heralds

51to summon the long-haired Achaeans to assembly.

52They made the summons, and the men gathered quickly.

53But first he seated a council of great-hearted elders

54beside the ship of Nestor, the Pylos-born king,

55and calling them together he framed his shrewd plan:

56"Hear me, friends. A divine Dream came to me in sleep

57through the ambrosial night, and in face, in build, in bearing

58it was most like brilliant Nestor.

59It stood above my head, and it said to me:

60'You sleep, son of war-minded Atreus, breaker of horses.4

61A man of counsel should not sleep the whole night through,

62a man to whom peoples are entrusted, who has so much to care for.

63Now hear me quickly: I am a messenger of Zeus,

64who far away cares greatly for you, and pities you.

65He bids you arm the long-haired Achaeans

66in full force: for now you might take the wide-wayed city

67of the Trojans. The immortals who keep their homes on Olympus

68are no longer divided; Hera has bent them all

69by her begging, and sorrows are fastened on the Trojans

70from Zeus. Keep this in your mind.' So the Dream spoke

71and flew away, and sweet sleep released me.

72Come then — let us see if we can arm the sons of the Achaeans.

73But first I will test them with words, as is the custom,5

74and order them to flee with the many-benched ships;

75and you, from your places, one here, one there, hold them back with words."

76So he spoke and sat down; and among them rose

77Nestor, who was king of sandy Pylos.

78With good will toward them he spoke and addressed them:

79"Friends, leaders and lords of the Argives,

80if any other Achaean had told us this dream,

81we would call it a lie and turn our backs on it;

82but the man who saw it claims to be far the best of the Achaeans.

83Come then — let us see if we can arm the sons of the Achaeans."

84So he spoke, and led the way out of the council,

85and the scepter-holding kings rose and obeyed

86the shepherd of the people; and the troops came streaming.

87As when the tribes of thronging bees come out

88of some hollow rock, always arriving anew,

89and fly in clusters over the flowers of spring,

90and some swarm thick this way, and some that way —

91so the many tribes of men from the ships and huts

92marched in companies along the deep beach

93to the assembly; and among them Rumor blazed like fire,

94Zeus's messenger, driving them on; and they kept gathering.

95The assembly was in turmoil, and the earth groaned beneath

96as the troops sat down, and there was uproar. Nine heralds,

97shouting, tried to hold them back — to stop the clamor

98and make them listen to the Zeus-nurtured kings.

99With effort the people were seated, held in their places,

100and left off shouting. Then the lord Agamemnon stood up,

101holding the scepter Hephaestus had labored to make.6

102Hephaestus gave it to lord Zeus, son of Cronos;

103then Zeus gave it to the guide, the slayer of Argus;

104lord Hermes gave it to Pelops, driver of horses;

105Pelops in turn gave it to Atreus, shepherd of the people;

106Atreus, dying, left it to Thyestes rich in flocks;

107and Thyestes in turn left it to Agamemnon to carry,

108to be lord over many islands and all of Argos.

109Leaning on it, he spoke his words to the Argives:

110"Friends, Danaan heroes, squires of Ares,

111Zeus, Cronos' great son, has bound me fast in heavy blindness —7

112the hard god: he promised me once, he bowed his head,

113that I should sack well-walled Ilion before returning;

114now he has planned a cruel deceit, and orders me

115back to Argos in disgrace, with half my people lost.

116Such, it seems, must be the pleasure of overmastering Zeus,

117who has undone the crowns of many cities

118and will undo more yet: his power is the greatest.

119This is a shameful thing even for men to come to hear of,

120that so fine an Achaean host, so great,

121fought a fruitless war and battled

122fewer men, with no end yet in sight.

123For if we wished — Achaeans and Trojans both —

124to swear a solemn truce and count ourselves,

125the Trojans mustering all who have their hearths in the city,

126and we Achaeans forming up in tens,

127and each ten chose one Trojan man to pour its wine,

128many tens would go without a wine-pourer.

129By so much, I tell you, the sons of the Achaeans outnumber

130the Trojans who live in the city. But there are allies,

131spear-wielding men from many cities,

132who beat me far off my course and will not let me

133sack Ilion's well-settled citadel, for all my wanting it.

134Nine years of great Zeus have gone already,

135and now the ships' timbers are rotten, the rigging loosed,

136and our wives, no doubt, and our little children

137sit in our halls waiting; while the work

138for which we came here stands as undone as ever.

139Come then — as I say it, let us all obey:

140let us flee with the ships to our dear fatherland.

141We shall not take wide-wayed Troy now."

142So he spoke, and stirred the heart in the breast

143of everyone in the crowd who had not heard the council.

144The assembly moved like the long waves of the sea,

145the Icarian deep, when East Wind and South Wind together

146rouse them, rushing down from the clouds of father Zeus;

147or as when the West Wind comes and moves deep grain,

148driving hard, and the field bows its ears —

149so all that assembly was moved. With a roar

150they rushed for the ships, and under their feet the dust

151rose and hung; and they shouted to one another

152to lay hold of the ships and drag them to the bright sea,

153and they cleared out the launching-channels; the cry went up to the sky

154as they strained for home; and they pulled the props from under the hulls.

155Then a homecoming beyond fate would have come to the Argives,

156if Hera had not spoken a word to Athena:

157"Shame! Unwearied one, child of aegis-bearing Zeus —8

158is it like this, homeward, to their dear fatherland,

159that the Argives are to flee over the broad back of the sea,

160and leave to Priam and the Trojans their boast —

161Argive Helen, for whose sake so many Achaeans

162have perished at Troy, far from their dear fatherland?

163Go now through the host of the bronze-shirted Achaeans;

164with your gentle words hold back every man;

165do not let them drag the balanced ships down to the sea."

166So she spoke, and the goddess grey-eyed Athena did not disobey.

167Down from the peaks of Olympus she went darting

168and swiftly came to the Achaeans' swift ships.

169There she found Odysseus, the equal of Zeus in counsel,9

170standing still: he had not laid a hand on his black

171well-benched ship, for grief had reached his heart and spirit.

172Standing close, grey-eyed Athena spoke to him:

173"Zeus-sprung son of Laertes, Odysseus of many devices,10

174is it like this, homeward, to your dear fatherland,

175that you will all flee, tumbling into the many-benched ships,

176and leave to Priam and the Trojans their boast —

177Argive Helen, for whose sake so many Achaeans

178have perished at Troy, far from their dear fatherland?

179Go now through the host of the Achaeans; hold back no longer;

180with your gentle words hold back every man;

181do not let them drag the balanced ships down to the sea."

182So she spoke, and he knew the voice of the goddess speaking.

183He set off at a run, and threw off his cloak — the herald

184Eurybates of Ithaca, who attended him, picked it up —

185and coming face to face with Agamemnon, Atreus' son,

186he took from him the ancestral scepter, imperishable forever,

187and with it went along the ships of the bronze-shirted Achaeans.

188Whenever he met a king, or a man of note,

189he would stand beside him and hold him back with gentle words:

190"Friend, it is not right to threaten you like a coward;

191but sit down yourself, and seat the rest of your people.

192You do not yet clearly know the mind of Atreus' son:

193now he is testing, and soon he will bear down on the sons of the Achaeans.11

194Did we not all hear in council what he said?

195May he not in anger do some harm to the sons of the Achaeans.

196Great is the anger of Zeus-nurtured kings;

197their honor is from Zeus, and Zeus the counselor loves them."

198But whatever man of the people he saw, and found him shouting,

199him he would drive with the scepter and berate aloud:

200"Fool — sit still and listen to what others tell you,

201your betters. You are unwarlike and without strength,

202never counted, whether in battle or in council.

203We cannot all be kings here, we Achaeans.

204The rule of many is no good thing: let there be one ruler,12

205one king, to whom the son of crooked-counseled Cronos gave

206the scepter and the ordinances, to do the ruling for his people."

207So, masterfully, he went through the army setting it in order; and they

208came streaming back to the assembly from the ships and huts

209with a roar, as when a wave of the loud-roaring sea

210thunders on a great beach, and the deep crashes in answer.

211Then the others sat down and were held in their places —

212all but Thersites, alone, still brawling on, unmeasured in speech,13

213whose mind was full of words, disorderly and many,

214to quarrel with kings — recklessly, in no kind of order,

215but with whatever looked likely to raise a laugh

216among the Argives. And he was the ugliest man who came under Ilion:

217bandy-legged, lame in one foot, his two shoulders

218hunched and drawn in over his chest; and above them

219a head that came to a point, with sparse stubble growing on it.

220He was hatefullest of all to Achilles and Odysseus,

221for those two he was forever reviling. But now it was brilliant Agamemnon

222he went at, shrieking shrill abuse; the Achaeans

223were furious at him, and indignation rose in every heart.

224Bawling loudly, he berated Agamemnon:

225"Son of Atreus, what are you complaining of now? What more do you want?

226Your huts are full of bronze; there are many women

227in your huts, choice ones, whom we Achaeans

228give you first of all whenever we take a town.

229Or do you still lack gold as well, that some son

230of the horse-taming Trojans will bring out of Ilion as ransom

231for a son that I bound and led away — I, or another Achaean?

232Or a young woman, to lie with in love,

233one you keep apart for yourself? It is not right

234for the commander to march the sons of the Achaeans into misery.

235Soft things! Vile disgraces! Achaean women — Achaean men no longer!

236Let us go home, home with the ships, and leave this man

237here in Troy to digest his prizes, so he may learn

238whether we others are any help to him or not —

239he who even now dishonored Achilles, a man

240far better than himself: he has taken and keeps his prize; he seized it himself.

241But there is no anger in Achilles' heart; he lets it go.

242Otherwise, son of Atreus, this outrage would be your last."

243So he spoke, reviling Agamemnon, shepherd of the people —

244Thersites. But quickly brilliant Odysseus was at his side,

245and glaring at him darkly he lashed him with hard words:

246"Thersites — endless babble, clear-voiced speaker though you are:

247stop; do not stand up alone to quarrel with kings.

248I say there is no viler mortal than you

249of all who came under Ilion with the sons of Atreus.

250So you had better not take kings into your mouth when you hold forth,

251hurling insults at them, with one eye on the voyage home.

252We do not even know clearly yet how these things will end —

253whether we sons of the Achaeans come home well or badly.

254So now you sit here reviling Agamemnon, Atreus' son,

255shepherd of the people, because the Danaan heroes

256give him so much. You speak in mockery.

257But I tell you this outright, and it will be accomplished:

258if I find you playing the fool again as you are now,

259then may Odysseus' head no longer sit on his shoulders,

260may I no longer be called the father of Telemachus,

261if I do not seize you and strip you of your own clothes,

262the cloak and the tunic that cover your shame,

263and send you off to the swift ships in tears,

264whipped out of the assembly with strokes of disgrace."

265So he spoke, and with the scepter he struck his back

266and shoulders. Thersites doubled up, and a fat tear fell from him,

267and a bloody welt rose up out of his back

268under the golden scepter. He sat down, terrified,

269and hurting, with a helpless look, he wiped away the tear.

270And the men, vexed at heart though they were, laughed at him sweetly,

271and thus one would say, with a glance at his neighbor:

272"Well now! Odysseus has done ten thousand good things,

273leading in good counsel, marshaling war;

274but this is the best thing he has done among the Argives:

275he has shut this word-slinging insulter out of our debates.

276That proud heart of his will not send him back again

277to rail at kings with words of abuse."

278So spoke the multitude. Then the sacker of cities, Odysseus,

279stood up with the scepter, and beside him grey-eyed Athena,

280in the likeness of a herald, called the host to silence,

281so that near and far the sons of the Achaeans

282might hear his word alike, and take in his counsel.

283With good will toward them he spoke and addressed them:

284"Son of Atreus — now, lord, the Achaeans mean

285to make you the most despised of mortal men,

286and they will not fulfill the promise they undertook

287while still on the way here from horse-pasturing Argos:

288that you should sack well-walled Ilion before returning.

289Like little children or widowed women

290they wail to one another about going home.

291And truly, there is toil here to send a man home disheartened:

292a man kept even one month from his wife

293frets by his many-benched ship when the winter gales

294and the rising sea shut him in;

295and for us this is the ninth year, circling round,

296that we have stayed here. So I cannot blame the Achaeans

297for fretting beside the beaked ships. But even so —

298it is shameful to wait so long and then come home empty.

299Bear up, friends, and stay a while longer, till we learn

300whether Calchas prophesies truly or not.

301For this we know well in our hearts, and you are all

302witnesses — all whom the fates of death have not carried off:

303only yesterday, it seems, when the Achaean ships were gathering

304at Aulis, bearing evils for Priam and the Trojans,

305and we, around a spring, at the holy altars,

306were offering the immortals perfect hecatombs

307under a beautiful plane tree, where the bright water ran:

308there a great sign appeared. A serpent, its back blood-dark,

309terrible, which the Olympian himself had sent into the light,

310darted from under the altar and made for the plane tree.

311There were nestling sparrows there, baby chicks,

312on the topmost branch, cowering under the leaves —

313eight of them, and the mother who hatched them made nine.

314There he devoured them, cheeping piteously,

315while the mother fluttered around, crying for her dear chicks;

316and coiling up he caught her by the wing as she shrieked about him.

317But when he had eaten the sparrow's children and the bird herself,

318the god who had shown him made him a sign conspicuous:14

319the son of crooked-counseled Cronos turned him to stone,

320and we stood there marveling at what had happened.

321So then: since this dread portent entered the gods' hecatombs,

322Calchas straightway spoke in prophecy:

323'Why have you fallen silent, long-haired Achaeans?

324For us Zeus the counselor has shown this great sign,

325late, late of fulfillment, whose fame will never perish.

326As this serpent ate the sparrow's children and the bird herself —

327eight, and the mother who hatched them made nine —

328so shall we make war here for as many years,

329and in the tenth we shall take the wide-wayed city.'

330That is how he spoke; and now it is all being fulfilled.

331Come then, stay, all of you, strong-greaved Achaeans,

332right here, until we take the great city of Priam."

333So he spoke, and the Argives roared aloud — and around them the ships

334echoed terribly under the shouting of the Achaeans —

335in praise of the word of godlike Odysseus.

336And among them spoke the Gerenian horseman Nestor:15

337"Shame! You hold assembly like little boys,

338infants with no interest in deeds of war.

339Where then will our compacts go, and our oaths?

340Into the fire, then, with every counsel and plan of men,

341the unmixed libations, the right hands we trusted!

342We are battling with words, and nothing else; a remedy

343we cannot find, for all our long time here.

344Son of Atreus — hold your counsel unshaken, as before,

345and lead the Argives through the strong encounters;

346and let them rot, the one or two of the Achaeans

347who take counsel apart — they will accomplish nothing —

348all for going back to Argos before we know

349whether the promise of aegis-bearing Zeus is a lie or not.

350For I say the overmastering son of Cronos bowed his head

351on that day when the Argives went aboard their swift-faring ships

352bearing slaughter and doom for the Trojans:

353lightening on the right, showing signs of good omen.

354Therefore let no man press to be gone homeward

355before he has lain beside the wife of some Trojan,

356and avenged the struggles and groans of Helen.16

357But if any man is wildly set on going home,

358let him just lay a hand on his black well-benched ship,

359and before all others he will meet his death and doom.

360And you, lord — take good counsel yourself, and listen to another;

361not to be tossed aside is the word I speak:

362divide the men by tribes and by clans, Agamemnon,

363so clan may stand by clan, and tribe by tribe.

364If you do this, and the Achaeans obey,

365you will know then which of your leaders is a coward, and which of your men,

366and which is brave: for they will be fighting among their own.

367And you will know whether it is by the gods' will you fail to take the city,

368or by men's cowardice and folly in war."

369Answering him spoke the lord Agamemnon:

370"Once again, old man, you outdo the sons of the Achaeans in speech.

371O father Zeus, Athena, Apollo —

372if I had ten such counselors among the Achaeans,

373then the city of lord Priam would soon bow its head,

374taken and sacked beneath our hands.

375But instead aegis-bearing Zeus, Cronos' son, gives me griefs,

376throwing me into fruitless quarrels and feuds.

377I and Achilles fought over a girl

378with head-on words — and it was I who began the anger.

379If ever we take counsel as one, then no longer

380will there be any putting off of evil for the Trojans, not for a moment.

381Now go to your meal, so we may join battle.

382Let every man sharpen his spear well, and set his shield right,

383let him give his swift-footed horses their feed,

384let him look over his chariot on every side, and put his mind on war,

385so that all day long we may measure ourselves in hateful battle.

386For there will be no pause, not the least,

387until night comes on and parts the fury of men.

388The strap of the body-covering shield will be sweated through

389on many a chest, and the hand will ache around the spear,

390and many a horse will sweat, straining at the polished chariot.

391And whomever I see minded to hang back from the fighting,

392lingering by the beaked ships — for him there will be

393no hope after that of escaping the dogs and the birds."

394So he spoke, and the Argives roared aloud, like a wave

395against a high headland when the South Wind comes and drives it

396on a jutting cliff — the waves never leave it,

397whatever wind is blowing, whether they rise this side or that.

398They stood up and scattered, hurrying to the ships,

399and made their fires smoke among the huts, and took their meal.

400And each man sacrificed to one of the gods who are forever,

401praying to escape death and the grind of Ares.

402But Agamemnon, lord of men, slaughtered an ox,

403fat, five years old, to the overmastering son of Cronos,

404and called the elders, the chief men of all the Achaeans:

405Nestor first of all, and lord Idomeneus,

406then the two Ajaxes and Tydeus' son,

407and sixth Odysseus, the equal of Zeus in counsel.

408And Menelaus, good at the war-cry, came unbidden,17

409for he knew in his heart how his brother labored.

410They stood around the ox and took up the barley-grains,

411and praying among them the lord Agamemnon spoke:

412"Zeus, most glorious, greatest, lord of the dark cloud, dwelling in the sky:

413let not the sun go down, nor the darkness come,

414until I have thrown down Priam's hall headlong,

415blackened with smoke, and burned its doors with hostile fire,

416and slashed the tunic on Hector's chest to rags

417with the bronze; and around him may his companions, many of them,

418fall face-down in the dust and bite the earth."

419So he spoke; but the son of Cronos would not yet grant it.

420He accepted the offering — and piled up joyless toil.

421Then, when they had prayed and thrown the barley-grains,

422they first drew back the victims' heads, cut their throats, and flayed them,

423carved out the thigh-pieces and wrapped them in fat,

424making a double fold, and laid raw flesh upon them;

425these they burned on dry leafless billets,

426and spitted the inner parts and held them over the flame of Hephaestus.

427When the thighs were burned and they had tasted the inner parts,

428they cut up the rest and threaded it on spits,

429roasted it with care, and drew it all off.

430Then when they had finished the work and made the meal ready,

431they feasted, and no heart lacked a fair share of the feast.

432And when they had put away desire for eating and drinking,

433the Gerenian horseman Nestor began to speak among them:

434"Most glorious son of Atreus, lord of men Agamemnon,

435let us sit talking here no longer, nor delay

436any further the work the god is putting in our hands.

437Come: let the heralds of the bronze-shirted Achaeans

438make their cry and gather the host along the ships,

439and let us go down together, as we are, through the wide Achaean camp,

440to waken sharp Ares the sooner."

441So he spoke, and the lord of men Agamemnon did not disobey.

442At once he ordered the clear-voiced heralds

443to summon the long-haired Achaeans to battle;

444they made the summons, and the men gathered quickly.

445The Zeus-nurtured kings around Atreus' son

446hurried about marshaling them, and among them grey-eyed Athena,

447holding the aegis — precious, unaging, immortal,18

448from which a hundred tassels of pure gold float free,

449all finely woven, each worth a hundred oxen.

450With it she flashed through the Achaean host,

451driving them on; and in each man's heart she roused strength

452to war and fight without ceasing.

453And at once war became sweeter to them than going home

454in the hollow ships to their dear fatherland.

455As an annihilating fire blazes up through a boundless forest

456on the peaks of a mountain, and the glare shows from far away,

457so, as they marched, from the tremendous bronze

458a gleam went flashing through the upper air to the sky.

459And as the many tribes of winged birds —

460geese, or cranes, or long-necked swans —

461on the Asian meadow around the streams of Caystrius

462fly this way and that, glorying in their wings,

463settling with cries, always advancing, and the meadow rings —

464so their many tribes from the ships and huts

465poured out onto the Scamandrian plain, and beneath them the ground

466thundered terribly under the feet of men and horses.

467They stood in the flowering Scamandrian meadow,

468tens of thousands, as many as the leaves and flowers in their season.

469As the many tribes of thronging flies

470that swarm through a herdsman's steading

471in the season of spring, when milk wets the pails,

472so many were the long-haired Achaeans who stood in the plain

473facing the Trojans, mad to break them.

474And as goatherds easily sort out

475their wide-wandering flocks of goats when they mingle at pasture,

476so the leaders sorted the men this way and that

477to go into battle, and among them the lord Agamemnon:

478in eyes and head like Zeus who delights in thunder,

479in girdle like Ares, in chest like Poseidon.

480As a bull stands out far the finest of all in the herd,

481conspicuous among the gathering cattle,

482such did Zeus make Atreus' son on that day,

483conspicuous among many, preeminent among heroes.

484Tell me now, Muses, who keep your homes on Olympus —19

485for you are goddesses, you are present, you know all things,

486while we hear only the report and know nothing —

487who were the leaders and lords of the Danaans.

488The multitude I could not tell nor name,

489not if I had ten tongues and ten mouths,

490a voice unbreakable, and a heart of bronze within me,

491unless the Olympian Muses, daughters of aegis-bearing

492Zeus, remembered for me all who came under Ilion.

493But the captains of the ships I will tell, and all the ships.

494Of the Boeotians, Peneleos and Leïtus were leaders,20

495with Arcesilaus and Prothoenor and Clonius:

496they who dwelt in Hyria and rocky Aulis,

497in Schoenus and Scolus and Eteonus of the many ridges,

498Thespeia, Graea, and spacious Mycalessus;

499who dwelt around Harma and Eilesium and Erythrae;

500who held Eleon and Hyle and Peteon,

501Ocalea and Medeon, that well-built citadel,

502Copae, Eutresis, and Thisbe of the many doves;

503who held Coroneia and grassy Haliartus;

504who held Plataea; who dwelt in Glisas;

505who held Hypothebae, that well-built citadel,

506and holy Onchestus, Poseidon's shining grove;

507who held Arne rich in grapes, and Mideia,

508sacred Nisa, and Anthedon at the land's edge:

509of these came fifty ships, and in each one

510a hundred and twenty young men of the Boeotians.

511And those who lived in Aspledon and Minyan Orchomenus

512were led by Ascalaphus and Ialmenus, sons of Ares,

513whom Astyoche bore in the house of Actor, Azeus' son —

514a modest maiden: she went up to her upper chamber,

515and mighty Ares lay with her in secret.

516With them thirty hollow ships sailed in order.

517And the Phocians were led by Schedius and Epistrophus,

518sons of great-hearted Iphitus, Naubolus' son:

519they who held Cyparissus and rocky Pytho,

520holy Crisa and Daulis and Panopeus;

521who dwelt around Anemoreia and Hyampolis;

522who lived along the brilliant river Cephisus;

523who held Lilaea at the springs of Cephisus.

524With them followed forty black ships.

525Their leaders were busy ranging the Phocian ranks,

526and they armed close beside the Boeotians, on the left.

527Of the Locrians, swift Ajax led, Oïleus' son —

528the lesser Ajax, in no way so big as Telamonian Ajax

529but far smaller: a little man, in a linen corslet,21

530but with the spear he surpassed all Hellenes and Achaeans:

531they who dwelt in Cynus and Opoeis and Calliarus,

532Bessa and Scarphe and lovely Augeae,

533Tarphe and Thronium, around the streams of Boagrius.

534With him followed forty black ships

535of the Locrians who live across from holy Euboea.

536And the Abantes, breathing fury, who held Euboea —

537Chalcis and Eretria and Histiaea rich in grapes,

538Cerinthus by the sea and the steep citadel of Dios;

539who held Carystus; who dwelt in Styra —

540these were led by Elephenor, scion of Ares,

541Chalcodon's son, chief of the great-hearted Abantes.

542With him followed the swift Abantes, hair grown long behind,

543spearmen eager with outstretched ash-wood spears

544to tear the cuirasses on the chests of their enemies.

545With him followed forty black ships.

546And those who held Athens, that well-built citadel,

547the district of great-hearted Erechtheus, whom once Athena22

548raised, Zeus's daughter — the grain-giving earth had borne him —

549and settled in Athens, in her own rich temple,

550where the young men of the Athenians seek his favor

551with bulls and rams as the years come round —

552these were led by Menestheus, son of Peteos.

553No man on the face of the earth was his equal

554at marshaling horses and shield-bearing men;

555Nestor alone rivaled him, for he was older.

556With him followed fifty black ships.

557And Ajax led twelve ships from Salamis,23

558and brought them and set them where the Athenian ranks stood.

559And those who held Argos and walled Tiryns,

560Hermione and Asine that lie on the deep gulf,

561Troezen, Eïones, and vine-clad Epidaurus,

562and the young Achaean men who held Aegina and Mases —

563these were led by Diomedes, good at the war-cry,

564and Sthenelus, dear son of famed Capaneus;

565and with them went a third, Euryalus, a godlike man,

566son of lord Mecisteus, Talaus' son;

567but the leader of them all was Diomedes, good at the war-cry.

568With them followed eighty black ships.

569And those who held Mycenae, that well-built citadel,

570and wealthy Corinth and well-built Cleonae;

571who dwelt in Orneiae and lovely Araethyrea

572and Sicyon, where Adrastus first was king;

573who held Hyperesia and steep Gonoessa

574and Pellene; who dwelt around Aegium

575and through all Aegialus and around broad Helice —

576of their hundred ships the leader was the lord Agamemnon,

577Atreus' son. With him followed by far the most

578and the best troops; and he among them, clad in gleaming bronze,

579glorying, stood out preeminent among all the heroes,

580because he was the best, and led by far the most men.

581And those who held hollow Lacedaemon full of ravines,

582Pharis and Sparta and Messe of the many doves;

583who dwelt in Bryseiae and lovely Augeae;

584who held Amyclae and Helos, the citadel by the sea;

585who held Laas and dwelt around Oetylus —

586these his brother led, Menelaus, good at the war-cry,

587with sixty ships; and they armed apart.

588He himself moved among them, trusting his own eagerness,

589urging them to war; and beyond all others his heart was set

590on avenging the struggles and groans of Helen.

591And those who dwelt in Pylos and lovely Arene,

592Thryon, the ford of Alpheius, and well-built Aepy;

593who lived in Cyparisseeis and Amphigeneia,

594Pteleos and Helos and Dorium — where the Muses24

595met Thamyris the Thracian and ended his singing,

596as he came from Oechalia, from Eurytus the Oechalian:

597for he had vowed in his boasting that he would win, even if

598the Muses themselves should sing, the daughters of aegis-bearing Zeus;

599and in their anger they maimed him: they took away

600his wondrous song and made him forget his harping —

601these were led by the Gerenian horseman Nestor.

602With him ninety hollow ships sailed in order.

603And those who held Arcadia under the steep mountain of Cyllene,

604beside the tomb of Aepytus, where men fight hand to hand;

605who dwelt in Pheneos and Orchomenus rich in flocks,

606Rhipe and Stratie and windswept Enispe;

607who held Tegea and lovely Mantinea;

608who held Stymphelus and dwelt in Parrhasia —

609these were led by Ancaeus's son, the lord Agapenor,

610with sixty ships, and in each ship many

611Arcadian men went aboard, skilled fighters.

612For the lord of men Agamemnon himself had given them

613the well-benched ships to cross the wine-dark sea —

614Atreus' son — since the works of the sea were no concern of theirs.

615And those who dwelt in Buprasium and brilliant Elis,

616all that Hyrmine and Myrsinus at the land's edge

617and the Olenian rock and Alesium enclose between them —

618of these there were four leaders, and ten swift ships

619followed each man, with many Epeians aboard.

620Two of them, Amphimachus and Thalpius, led —

621sons, one of Cteatus, one of Eurytus, of Actor's line;

622of the third company strong Diores was leader, Amarynceus' son;

623and of the fourth godlike Polyxeinus was leader,

624son of lord Agasthenes, Augeias' son.

625And those from Dulichium and the holy Echinae,

626the islands that lie across the sea facing Elis —

627these were led by Meges, the equal of Ares,

628Phyleus' son, whom the horseman Phyleus dear to Zeus begot —

629who once, angered at his father, had moved away to Dulichium.

630With him followed forty black ships.

631And Odysseus led the great-hearted Cephallenians,

632who held Ithaca and Neriton with quivering leaves;

633who dwelt in Crocyleia and rugged Aegilips;

634who held Zacynthus; who dwelt around Samos;

635who held the mainland and dwelt on the facing shores —

636these Odysseus led, the equal of Zeus in counsel,

637and with him followed twelve ships with cheeks of vermilion.25

638And the Aetolians were led by Thoas, Andraemon's son:

639they who dwelt in Pleuron and Olenus and Pylene,

640Chalcis by the shore and rocky Calydon.

641For the sons of great-hearted Oeneus were no more,

642and he himself was no more, and fair-haired Meleager was dead;

643so on Thoas the whole rule of the Aetolians had been laid.

644With him followed forty black ships.

645Of the Cretans, Idomeneus, famed with the spear, was leader:

646they who held Cnossos and walled Gortyn,

647Lyctus and Miletus and chalk-white Lycastus,

648Phaestus and Rhytium, fine cities to live in,

649and the others who dwelt through hundred-citied Crete.

650Of these Idomeneus, famed with the spear, was leader,

651with Meriones, the equal of Enyalius, slayer of men.

652With them followed eighty black ships.

653And Tlepolemus, Heracles' son, tall and strong,

654led nine ships from Rhodes, of the lordly Rhodians,

655who dwelt through Rhodes in three divisions:

656Lindus, Ialysus, and chalk-white Cameirus.

657Their leader was Tlepolemus, famed with the spear,

658whom Astyocheia bore to the might of Heracles —

659he brought her out of Ephyra, from the river Selleïs,

660after sacking many towns of Zeus-nurtured fighting men.

661Now when Tlepolemus was grown in the well-built hall,

662he killed, on the instant, his own father's dear uncle,

663Licymnius, scion of Ares, already growing old;

664and quickly he built ships, gathered a great following,

665and fled away over the sea, for the others threatened him —

666the sons and the grandsons of the might of Heracles.

667He came at last to Rhodes, a wanderer, a man of sorrows;

668and there they settled in three parts, tribe by tribe, and were loved

669by Zeus, who is king over gods and men,

670and the son of Cronos poured wondrous wealth upon them.

671And Nireus led three balanced ships from Syme —26

672Nireus, son of Aglaïa and lord Charopus;

673Nireus, the most beautiful man who came under Ilion

674of all the Danaans, after the blameless son of Peleus.

675But he was a slight man, and few people followed him.

676And those who held Nisyrus and Crapathus and Casus,

677and Cos, city of Eurypylus, and the Calydnae islands —

678these were led by Pheidippus and Antiphus,

679two sons of lord Thessalus, Heracles' son.

680With them thirty hollow ships sailed in order.

681Now all those who dwelt in Pelasgian Argos,

682who lived in Alos and Alope and Trachis;

683who held Phthia and Hellas of the beautiful women —

684and they were called Myrmidons, and Hellenes, and Achaeans —

685of their fifty ships Achilles was captain.

686But they had no thought of grim-voiced war;27

687there was no one to lead them into the ranks.

688For swift-footed brilliant Achilles lay among the ships,

689in wrath over the girl, Briseis of the lovely hair,

690whom he had taken out of Lyrnessus with great toil,

691when he sacked Lyrnessus and the walls of Thebe

692and cut down Mynes and Epistrophus, the spear-mad

693sons of lord Euenus, Selepius' son.

694Grieving for her he lay there; but he was soon to rise.

695And those who held Phylace and flowering Pyrasus,

696Demeter's precinct, and Iton, mother of flocks,

697Antron by the sea, and Pteleos deep in meadow-grass —

698these warlike Protesilaus had led

699while he lived; but by then the black earth held him under.

700His wife, cheeks torn with mourning, was left behind in Phylace,

701his house but half-built: a Dardanian man had killed him28

702as he leapt from his ship, far the first of the Achaeans.

703Yet the men were not leaderless, though they longed for their leader:

704Podarces, scion of Ares, set them in order,

705son of Iphiclus rich in flocks, Phylacus' son,

706own brother of great-hearted Protesilaus,

707the younger-born; the elder and the better man

708was the hero Protesilaus the warlike. So the people

709lacked no leader, though they longed for the brave man gone.

710With him followed forty black ships.

711And those who dwelt in Pherae beside Lake Boebeïs,

712in Boebe and Glaphyrae and well-built Iolcus —

713these Admetus' dear son led with eleven ships:

714Eumelus, whom Alcestis bore to Admetus —

715brilliant among women, loveliest of the daughters of Pelias.

716And those who dwelt in Methone and Thaumacia;

717who held Meliboea and rugged Olizon —

718these Philoctetes led, the master bowman,29

719with seven ships; and in each one fifty rowers

720had come aboard, all skilled bowmen, strong fighters.

721But he lay on an island, suffering hard pains,

722on sacred Lemnos, where the sons of the Achaeans had left him

723tormented by the evil wound of the murderous water-snake.

724There he lay grieving. But soon the Argives by their ships

725were to remember lord Philoctetes.

726Yet the men were not leaderless, though they longed for their leader:

727Medon set them in order, Oïleus' bastard son,

728whom Rhene bore to Oïleus, sacker of cities.

729And those who held Tricca and terraced Ithome;

730who held Oechalia, city of Eurytus the Oechalian —

731these were led by the two sons of Asclepius,

732the good healers Podaleirius and Machaon.

733With them thirty hollow ships sailed in order.

734And those who held Ormenion and the spring Hypereia;

735who held Asterium and the white peaks of Titanus —

736these Eurypylus led, Euaemon's shining son.

737With him followed forty black ships.

738And those who held Argissa; who dwelt in Gyrtone,

739Orthe and Elone and the white city Oloosson —

740these were led by Polypoetes, steadfast in battle,

741son of Peirithous, whom immortal Zeus begot;

742famed Hippodameia bore him to Peirithous

743on the day he took vengeance on the shaggy beast-men

744and drove them out of Pelion, and pushed them to the Aethices.

745Not alone: with him was Leonteus, scion of Ares,

746son of high-hearted Coronus, Caeneus' son.

747With them followed forty black ships.

748And Gouneus led two and twenty ships from Cyphus,

749and with him followed the Enienes and the battle-steadfast Peraebians,

750who set their homes around wintry Dodona;

751who worked the fields around lovely Titaressus,

752the river that pours his fair-flowing water into Peneius —

753yet he does not mingle with the silver eddies of Peneius,

754but flows along on top of him like oil:

755for he is a branch broken off the water of Styx, the dread oath.

756And the Magnetes were led by Prothoos, Tenthredon's son:

757they who dwelt around Peneius and Pelion

758of the quivering leaves. Swift Prothoos was their leader,

759and with him followed forty black ships.

760These, then, were the leaders and lords of the Danaans.

761Now which was far the best of them — tell me, Muse —

762of the men, and of the horses, that followed the sons of Atreus?

763Best by far of the horses were the mares of Pheres' line,

764which Eumelus drove: swift-footed as birds,

765matched in coat and matched in years, their backs as level as a plumb-line.

766Apollo of the silver bow had raised them in Pereia,

767both of them mares, carrying with them the panic of war.

768And of the men, far the best was Telamonian Ajax —

769while Achilles kept his wrath: for he was mightiest by far,

770he, and the horses that carried the blameless son of Peleus.

771But he lay among his beaked seagoing ships,

772nursing his rage at Agamemnon, shepherd of the people,

773Atreus' son; and his men along the surf-line of the sea

774amused themselves throwing the discus and the hunting-spear,

775and shooting with bows; and the horses, each beside its chariot,

776stood cropping lotus and marsh-grown parsley,

777while the chariots lay well covered in the huts

778of their lords; and the men, longing for their leader dear to Ares,

779drifted here and there through the camp, and did not fight.

780On the others came, as if all the land were swept with fire;

781and the earth groaned beneath them, as under Zeus who delights in thunder

782in his anger, when he lashes the earth around Typhoeus30

783among the Arimi, where they say Typhoeus has his bed:

784so the earth groaned greatly under their feet

785as they came on; and very swiftly they crossed the plain.

786And to the Trojans went wind-footed swift Iris, a messenger31

787from aegis-bearing Zeus, with a grievous message.

788They were holding assembly at Priam's doors,

789all gathered together, the young men and the old.

790Standing close, swift-footed Iris spoke to them,

791likening her voice to Priam's son Polites,

792who sat as lookout of the Trojans, trusting the speed of his feet,

793on the very top of the tomb of old Aesyetes,

794watching for when the Achaeans should rush out from their ships;

795in his likeness swift-footed Iris spoke:

796"Old man, endless talk is always dear to you,

797as once it was in peacetime; but relentless war is upon us.

798Truly I have gone into very many battles of men,

799but never yet have I seen such a host, so great:

800for like the leaves, like the sands, in their multitude

801they are coming over the plain to fight for the city.

802Hector, you above all I charge to do this:

803there are many allies through Priam's great city,

804and tongue differs from tongue among men of scattered lands.

805Let each man give the word to those he rules;

806let him marshal his own townsmen, and lead them out."

807So she spoke, and Hector did not fail to know the goddess's voice.

808At once he dissolved the assembly, and they rushed to arms.

809All the gates were flung open, and the host streamed out,

810foot-soldiers and horsemen; and a great din arose.

811There is a steep mound out before the city,

812apart in the plain, with open ground on every side;

813men call it Batieia,

814but the immortals call it the tomb of Myrine, light of step.32

815There the Trojans and their allies formed their divisions.

816The Trojans were led by great Hector of the flashing helmet,33

817Priam's son; with him by far the most and the best

818troops armed themselves, eager with their spears.

819The Dardanians were led by the good son of Anchises,

820Aeneas, whom brilliant Aphrodite bore to Anchises

821on the spurs of Ida, a goddess bedded with a mortal.

822Not alone: with him were Antenor's two sons,

823Archelochus and Acamas, skilled in all fighting.

824And those who dwelt in Zeleia under the lowest foot of Ida,

825men of wealth, who drink the dark water of Aesepus,

826Trojans — these were led by Lycaon's shining son,

827Pandarus, to whom Apollo himself gave the bow.

828And those who held Adresteia and the district of Apaesus;

829who held Pityeia and the steep mount of Tereia —

830these were led by Adrestus and Amphius of the linen corslet,

831two sons of Merops of Percote, who beyond all men

832knew the seer's art, and would not let his sons

833go to man-destroying war; but the two would not

834obey him: the fates of black death were leading them on.

835And those who dwelt around Percote and Practius;

836who held Sestus and Abydos and brilliant Arisbe —

837these were led by Hyrtacus' son Asius, marshal of men —

838Asius, Hyrtacus' son, whom horses carried from Arisbe,

839tall and fire-colored, from the river Selleïs.

840And Hippothous led the tribes of the Pelasgian spearmen,

841those who dwelt in deep-soiled Larisa;

842these were led by Hippothous and Pylaeus, scion of Ares,

843two sons of Pelasgian Lethus, Teutamus' son.

844And the Thracians were led by Acamas and the hero Peiroos —

845all those the strong-flowing Hellespont encloses.

846And Euphemus was captain of the Ciconian spearmen,

847son of Zeus-nurtured Troezenus, Ceas' son.

848And Pyraechmes led the Paeonians of the curved bows,

849from far away, from Amydon, from the wide-flowing Axius —

850Axius, whose water spreads fairest over the earth.

851And the Paphlagonians were led by Pylaemenes of the shaggy heart,

852from the Eneti, where the breed of wild mules is born:

853they who held Cytorus and dwelt around Sesamus,

854whose famed homes stood around the river Parthenius,

855Cromna and Aegialus and the heights of Erythini.

856And the Halizones were led by Odius and Epistrophus,

857from far away, from Alybe, where silver has its birthplace.

858And the Mysians were led by Chromis, and Ennomus the bird-reader —

859but his birds did not save him from black doom:

860he was broken under the hands of the swift-footed son of Aeacus34

861in the river, where Achilles cut down Trojans and others with them.

862And Phorcys led the Phrygians, with godlike Ascanius,

863from far Ascania; and they were eager to fight in the press of battle.

864And the Maeonians were led by Mesthles and Antiphus,

865the two sons of Talaemenes, whom the Gygaean lake bore;

866they led the Maeonians, born under Tmolus.

867And Nastes was captain of the Carians of barbarous speech,35

868who held Miletus and the mountain of Phthires, dense with leaves,

869the streams of Maeander and the steep peaks of Mycale;

870these were led by Amphimachus and Nastes —

871Nastes and Amphimachus, the shining children of Nomion.

872And one of them went to war decked in gold, like a girl —

873the fool: it did not ward off wretched destruction from him;

874he was broken under the hands of the swift-footed son of Aeacus

875in the river, and war-minded Achilles carried off the gold.

876And Sarpedon led the Lycians, with blameless Glaucus,

877from far Lycia, from the eddying Xanthus.

Notes

1 line 6 — "baneful Dream" (οὖλον ὄνειρον) — Dream is a personified agent here, capitalized accordingly. That Zeus sends a deliberately lying dream scandalized ancient readers (Plato cites this passage disapprovingly, Republic 383a); the poem itself is unembarrassed — the deception is the mechanism of Zeus's promise to Thetis. ↩

2 line 11 — "long-haired Achaeans" (κάρη κομόωντες Ἀχαιοί) — stock epithet of the army, literally "letting the hair grow long on the head"; always so rendered. ↩

3 line 23 — "breaker of horses" (ἱπποδάμοιο) — here of Atreus; later in the poem it is the standing epithet of the Trojans and of Hector ("horse-taming Trojans," 230). Rendered "breaker(s) of horses" for individuals, "horse-taming" for the people. ↩

4 lines 60–70 — The Dream repeats Zeus's instructions verbatim, and Agamemnon repeats them verbatim again to the council — standard epic messenger technique. Zenodotus' text drastically condensed such repetitions (apparatus, Line 60-70), and he omitted 157-168 below (Hera's speech restated by Athena) entirely, replacing it with a single line (apparatus, Line 156, 157-168). The vulgate keeps the full repetitions; so does this translation, in identical English each time. ↩

5 line 73 — "I will test them with words, as is the custom" (ἣ θέμις ἐστί) — the "testing" (peira) is one of the poem's strangest plot devices: no custom of testing armies is otherwise known, and the plan nearly destroys the expedition. The phrase may mean only "as is my right." Translated neutrally; the narrative logic is left as the poem leaves it — precarious. ↩

6 lines 101–108 — The scepter's pedigree — Hephaestus, Zeus, Hermes, Pelops, Atreus, Thyestes, Agamemnon — is a title-deed in miniature: Agamemnon's authority is literally handed down from Zeus. "The slayer of Argus" (ἀργεϊφόντης) is Hermes' fixed epithet, of disputed meaning ("Argus-slayer" is the traditional reading and is kept); "the guide" (διάκτορος) is his companion title. ↩

7 lines 111–118 — Zenodotus athetized this opening of the speech (apparatus). Lines 111-118 recur nearly verbatim at 9.18-25, where Agamemnon speaks them in earnest; here they are play-acted despair. Note the vocabulary: Zeus "bound me in heavy blindness" (ἄτη) — the same word Achilles used of him at 1.412, and Agamemnon concedes at 378 that "it was I who began the anger." ↩

8 line 157 — "Unwearied one" (Ἀτρυτώνη) — obscure cult title of Athena, traditionally derived from "not to be worn down"; rendered by that traditional sense. ↩

9 line 169 — "the equal of Zeus in counsel" (Διὶ μῆτιν ἀτάλαντον) — Odysseus' loftiest epithet, crediting him with mētis, the cunning practical intelligence that is also Zeus's; always so rendered. ↩

10 line 173 — "Zeus-sprung son of Laertes, Odysseus of many devices" (διογενὲς Λαερτιάδη πολυμήχανʼ Ὀδυσσεῦ) — the full-line address formula used for Odysseus throughout both epics; πολυμήχανος ("of many devices") is kept distinct from πολύμητις ("resourceful"). ↩

11 lines 193–197 — Aristarchus athetized these five lines, and they are independently absent from Xenophon's quotation of the passage (Memorabilia 1.2.56) — two converging hints they may not be original (apparatus, Line 193-197; disputed_lines.md #3). Without them, Odysseus' speech to the kings loses its "he is only testing" explanation. Translated as printed; the two-tier treatment of kings and commoners on either side of this line is in any case secure. One crux inside the passage: 194 is printed here as a rhetorical question ("Did we not all hear in council what he said?" — so Lattimore), but Kirk reads it as a statement — "in council we did NOT all hear what he said" — which better explains why the man addressed needs Odysseus's telling. The question-rendering stands; the alternative is recorded. ↩

12 line 204 — "The rule of many is no good thing: let there be one ruler" (οὐκ ἀγαθὸν πολυκοιρανίη· εἷς κοίρανος ἔστω) — the most quoted political sentence in Homer (Aristotle cites it in the Metaphysics in defense of monarchy). In context it is crowd-control rhetoric, not constitutional theory. ↩

13 lines 212–219 — Thersites is the only common soldier in the Iliad who is named, described, and given a speech — and the description is a catalogue of deformities, the epic's physiognomic shorthand for baseness (contrast every hero's beauty). His name suggests θέρσος, "audacity." Zenodotus athetized 220-223 (apparatus). Notice that his complaints against Agamemnon are substantively Achilles' complaints from Book 1, voiced one rung down the social ladder — and answered with a beating the assembly applauds. ↩

14 lines 318–319 — "made him a sign conspicuous" — the vulgate reads ἀρίζηλον ("very plain to see"): the petrified serpent becomes a lasting marker of the portent. An ancient variant read ἀΐζηλον ("unseen"): the god made the serpent vanish. Aristarchus athetized 319 (the petrification line) outright (apparatus, Line 318, 319). Translated from the printed text: conspicuous, then turned to stone. ↩

15 line 336 — "the Gerenian horseman Nestor" (Γερήνιος ἱππότα Νέστωρ) — his fixed title henceforth; "Gerenian" was obscure already in antiquity (usually connected with Gerenia in Messenia, where Nestor was said to have been raised). Always so rendered. ↩

16 line 356 — "the struggles and groans of Helen" (Ἑλένης ὁρμήματά τε στοναχάς τε) — ambiguous since antiquity: either Helen's own strivings and griefs (a Helen carried off unwilling), or the struggles and groans fought and suffered over her. The English keeps the genitive's ambiguity. The line recurs at 590 of Menelaus. ↩

17 line 408 — "good at the war-cry" (βοὴν ἀγαθός) — fixed epithet of Menelaus and Diomedes: the carrying battlefield voice as a command skill. Always so rendered. Menelaus coming "unbidden" to his brother's feast was proverbial in antiquity. ↩

18 lines 447–449 — The aegis (see Book 1, note [21]) here gets its fullest description: a hundred all-gold tassels, "each worth a hundred oxen" (ἑκατόμβοιος) — value is reckoned in cattle, the Homeric world's standard of price; there is no money in the poem. ↩

19 lines 484–493 — The poet's second and most elaborate invocation of the Muses, marking the Catalogue as a feat of memory beyond unaided human power ("ten tongues, ten mouths"). The Muses know because they are present and see; the poet otherwise has only kleos — "the report" — at second hand. ↩

20 lines 494–759 — The Catalogue of Ships: 29 Achaean contingents, some 190 place names, 1,186 ships by the traditional count. It has been argued since antiquity to reflect an originally separate composition (its geography fits mainland Greece of an earlier era better than the poem's own dramatic situation — Boeotia leads, plausibly from the muster at Aulis), and several manuscripts omit the whole span (apparatus, Line 494-877: om.; disputed_lines.md #2). Translated as fully integral, in the same line-for-line verse as the narrative; see commentary. ↩

21 lines 528–530 — The disambiguation of the two Ajaxes. Zenodotus athetized 528 and Aristarchus 529-530 (apparatus) — the "linen corslet" (λινοθώρηξ) bothered critics, linen being light armor at odds with heroic bronze. Kept: the lesser Ajax's smallness and spear-skill are both plot-relevant later. ↩

22 lines 546–551 — Erechtheus, the primeval Athenian king, is "borne by the grain-giving earth" and raised by Athena in her own temple — the earliest statement of the Athenian autochthony myth, and one of the very few references to hero-cult practice (annual bull and ram offerings) in the Iliad. ↩

23 lines 557–558 — The most notorious couplet in the poem. Aristarchus athetized 558, and ancient tradition (Strabo 9.1.10, Diogenes Laertius 1.48) reports that Solon or Pisistratus inserted it to support Athens's claim to Salamis against Megara — the Megarians circulated a rival version continuing Ajax's line with Megarian towns instead (quoted in the apparatus at Line 557-558). Whether or not the accusation is true, Ajax — the war's second-best fighter — receives a strikingly minimal two-line entry. Translated as printed, with the controversy recorded here (disputed_lines.md #2a). ↩

24 lines 594–600 — Thamyris the Thracian bard boasted he could outsing the Muses; they "maimed" him (πηρόν — later tradition specifies blinding, but the word is unspecific), stripped his song, and made him forget his lyre-craft. A dark in-poem parable of song as the Muses' revocable gift, placed pointedly just after the poet's own humble invocation of them. ↩

25 line 637 — "ships with cheeks of vermilion" (μιλτοπάρῃοι) — Odysseus' ships have bows painted with red ochre; the word transfers the "cheek" of the human face-epithets to the ship's prow. ↩

26 lines 671–675 — The Nireus entry: his name opens three consecutive lines (anaphora unique in the Catalogue) — beautiful, well-born, and militarily negligible. Zenodotus athetized 673-675 and omitted 674 (apparatus). The rhetorical polish of the entry made it famous; it is kept at full strength. ↩

27 lines 686–694 — Zenodotus athetized this Achilles-paragraph (apparatus, Line 686-694). It is the Catalogue's load-bearing link to the poem's plot: the fifty ships are mustered, and their leader lies apart "in wrath over the girl" — with the quiet promise "but he was soon to rise." ↩

28 lines 698–702 — Protesilaus, "far the first of the Achaeans" ashore, killed as he leapt from his ship (an oracle had promised death to the first to land — the poem alludes without telling). His "house but half-built" (δόμος ἡμιτελής) is ambiguous: an unfinished hall, or a marriage cut off half-made; the wife with torn cheeks favors hearing both. ↩

29 lines 716–725 — Philoctetes, master of Heracles' bow, abandoned on Lemnos with the festering water-snake wound. "Soon the Argives were to remember" him: prophecy held that Troy could not fall without the bow — the story of Sophocles' Philoctetes. One of the Catalogue's several forward windows beyond the Iliad's own frame. ↩

30 lines 782–783 — Typhoeus, the monstrous challenger of Zeus, lies lashed by his thunderbolts "among the Arimi" (location already disputed in antiquity — Cilicia, Lydia, and volcanic Ischia were all proposed). The marching army makes the earth groan like Zeus's cosmic punishment: the grandest of the muster-similes. ↩

31 line 786 — "wind-footed swift Iris" (ποδήνεμος ὠκέα Ἶρις) — the rainbow goddess as messenger of Zeus; her fixed epithet, always so rendered. ↩

32 lines 813–814 — Men call the mound Batieia; the gods call it "the tomb of Myrine" (an Amazon, in later tradition). Second instance of divine-versus-human double naming (cf. Briareus/Aegaeon, Book 1 note [33]) — the gods' name preserves the older, true history of the landmark. ↩

33 line 816 — "Hector of the flashing helmet" (κορυθαίολος Ἕκτωρ) — his defining epithet, first appearance in the poem; literally "with glancing/shimmering helm." Always so rendered. ↩

34 lines 860–861 — Aristarchus athetized these lines (and obelized the parallel pair at 874-875): they anticipate, in the future tense of the narrator, Achilles' massacre in the river Scamander — which belongs to Book 21 — and ancient critics disliked the Catalogue "spoiling" it. Kept: the Trojan catalogue's habit of glancing at its captains' deaths is part of its elegiac texture. ↩

35 line 867 — "Carians of barbarous speech" (βαρβαροφώνων) — the first occurrence in Greek literature of any "barbar-" word. Not yet the later blanket term "barbarian": in the whole poem only the Carians get this label, and the Trojans themselves speak with the Achaeans unhindered. Rendered "of barbarous speech" to keep the word's echoic, language-centered sense. ↩
