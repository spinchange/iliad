Book VII

The duel of Hector and Ajax, the burial truce, and the building of the Achaean wall.

Translator’s note on Book 7

Book 7 is the poem's book of institutions. Its duel replays Book 3 with the war's real fighters — and the poem says so in its own medium: the spear-exchange at 244-254 reuses the Paris-Menelaus lines of 3.355-360 verbatim, and this translation repeats its own Book 3 English word for word (note 16). Around the duel stand the machinery of managed violence: the challenge with terms for the corpse, the marked lots, the heralds parting the fighters at dusk ("It is good to yield to night as well"), the exchange of gifts — Hector's sword and Ajax's belt, on which later tradition hung both their deaths (note 20) — the burial truce, the diplomatic offer and its refusal, and at last the wall and ditch that will be the stage-set of Books 8-15.

The wall carries the book's strangest scene: Poseidon's complaint that the Achaeans built it "and gave the gods no glorious hecatombs," and Zeus's answer licensing its future erasure — sweep it into the sea, hide the beach under sand again. All three Alexandrian critics athetized the exchange (443-464), and it is easy to see why it unsettled them: the poem pauses to arrange the demolition of its own scenery, as if to certify that no trace of the fiction will contradict the visible coastline (note 27; the demolition is narrated at 12.13-33). Nestor's proposal that each man's bones be carried "home to the children" (334-335) was also athetized — the custom looks Athenian, not Homeric — and Antenor's motion to hand Helen back completes the pattern of a book in which every institution for ending the war is tried and fails (notes [22], [23]).

Small register notes: αἴσιμα παρειπών, the disputed narratorial phrase from the Adrestus scene (6.62), recurs at 120-121 in a context where it is plainly benign — evidence worth weighing back into that dispute. Poseidon "the earth-shaker" enters the register, with "green fear," the "bronze-greaved" hapax at 41, and the slave-trade line at 475 — the only occurrence of ἀνδράποδα in Homer, athetized by all three critics, and rendered "slaves" under the project's policy (note 28).

1So speaking, glorious Hector swept out through the gates,

2and with him went Alexandros his brother; and in their hearts

3both were raging for war and for battle.

4As a god gives to longing sailors

5a following wind, when they have worn themselves out at the polished

6pine oars, driving the sea, and their limbs are unstrung with weariness:

7so those two appeared to the longing Trojans.

8Then each took his man: Paris the son of lord Areithous,

9Menesthius, who had his home in Arne — whom the club-man1

10Areithous fathered with ox-eyed Phylomedusa;

11and Hector hit Eioneus with his sharp spear

12in the neck, under the fine bronze helmet-rim, and unstrung his limbs.

13And Glaucus, Hippolochus' son, captain of the Lycian men,

14hit Iphinous, Dexias' son, with his spear in the strong encounter

15as he leapt up behind his fast mares,

16in the shoulder; he dropped from the car to the ground, and his limbs were unstrung.

17Now when the goddess grey-eyed Athena marked them

18killing Argives in the strong encounter,

19down from the peaks of Olympus she went darting

20to holy Ilion; and Apollo rose to meet her,

21looking out from Pergamos, wanting victory for the Trojans;

22and the two of them met each other beside the oak.

23Lord Apollo, Zeus's son, spoke to her first:

24"Why so eager, this time, daughter of great Zeus?

25Why come from Olympus? What great passion sent you?

26Is it to give the Danaans victory that turns the battle's tide?

27You have no pity, none, for the Trojans as they die.

28But if you would let me persuade you, it would be far better:

29let us stop the war and the fighting

30for today. Later they will fight again, until they find

31the appointed end of Ilion — since it is so dear to the hearts

32of you immortal goddesses to sack this city."

33Then the goddess grey-eyed Athena answered him:

34"So be it, worker-from-afar. With that very thought

35I came from Olympus among the Trojans and Achaeans.

36Come then — how do you mean to stop the men's fighting?"

37And lord Apollo, Zeus's son, answered her:

38"Let us rouse the strong fury of Hector, breaker of horses,

39to see if he will challenge some one of the Danaans

40to fight him man to man in dread combat,

41and the bronze-greaved Achaeans, stung to honor,

42will send out one man alone to fight brilliant Hector."

43So he spoke, and the goddess grey-eyed Athena did not disobey.

44And Helenus, Priam's dear son, put together in his heart

45their counsel — the plan that had pleased the plotting gods;

46and he came and stood by Hector and spoke his word:

47"Hector, son of Priam, the equal of Zeus in counsel,

48would you let me persuade you? I am your brother.

49Make the other Trojans sit down, and all the Achaeans,

50and yourself challenge whoever is best of the Achaeans

51to fight you man to man in dread combat.

52It is not yet your fate to die and meet your doom:

53so I heard it in the voice of the gods who are forever."2

54So he spoke, and Hector rejoiced greatly to hear the word,

55and he went into the middle and held back the Trojan lines,

56gripping his spear by the middle; and they all sat down,

57and Agamemnon seated the strong-greaved Achaeans.

58And Athena and Apollo of the silver bow,

59in the likeness of vulture birds, settled

60on the tall oak of father Zeus who bears the aegis,3

61delighting in the men; and the ranks of them sat close,

62bristling with shields and helmets and spears.

63As a shiver from the West Wind spreads down over the deep

64when the wind first rises, and the sea darkens beneath it,

65such were the ranks of the Achaeans and Trojans sitting

66in the plain. And Hector spoke between the two armies:

67"Hear from me, Trojans and strong-greaved Achaeans,

68so I may say what the heart in my chest commands me.

69Our sworn oaths — the high-throned son of Cronos did not seal them,

70but plans evil for both sides, and marks it out,

71until either you take fair-towered Troy

72or are broken yourselves beside your seagoing ships.

73Among you are the chief men of all the Achaeans:

74of these, let the man whose heart bids him fight me

75come out here now, before you all, to champion you against brilliant Hector.

76And this is my word — let Zeus be our witness to it:

77if that man takes my life with the long-edged bronze,

78let him strip my armor and carry it to the hollow ships,

79but give my body back to my home, so that in death

80the Trojans and the Trojans' wives may give me my due of fire.

81But if I take him, and Apollo gives me the glory,

82I will strip his armor and carry it to holy Ilion

83and hang it before the temple of Apollo the far-striker;

84but the corpse I will give back to the well-benched ships,

85so the long-haired Achaeans may give him burial

86and heap him a mound beside the wide Hellespont.

87And someday a man will say, even among men born late,

88sailing his many-benched ship over the wine-dark sea:

89'This is the mound of a man who died long ago,

90whom once, fighting his best, glorious Hector killed.'

91So someone will say; and my fame will never perish."4

92So he spoke, and they all went still in silence:

93ashamed to refuse, and afraid to accept.

94At long last Menelaus stood up and spoke among them,

95raking them with his scorn, groaning deep at heart:

96"Ah me — you threateners! Women of Achaea, men of Achaea no longer!5

97Surely this disgrace will be grim beyond grimness

98if no Danaan now goes out to meet Hector.

99May every one of you turn to water and earth,

100sitting there where you sit, heartless, fameless.

101Against this man I will arm myself; the ends of victory

102are held above us, in the hands of the immortal gods."

103So speaking he began to put on his beautiful armor.

104And then, Menelaus, the end of your life would have shown

105at Hector's hands, since he was far the stronger,

106had not the kings of the Achaeans sprung up and caught you —

107and Atreus' son himself, wide-ruling Agamemnon,

108took him by the right hand, and spoke a word, and named him:

109"You are raving, Zeus-nurtured Menelaus — and this raving

110is not required of you. Hold back, though it stings,

111and do not, for pride's sake, fight a better man than you,

112Hector, Priam's son — a man the others shudder at.

113Even Achilles, in the battle where men win glory,

114shivered to meet this man — and he is far better than you.6

115Go now and sit among the company of your companions;

116the Achaeans will put up another champion against this one.

117Fearless though he is, and never sated with the grind of battle,

118I say he will be glad to bend his knee in rest, if he escapes

119out of the fury of war and the dread combat."

120So speaking the hero turned his brother's mind,

121for his urging was of things due; and he obeyed. Then his aides,7

122rejoicing, lifted the armor from his shoulders;

123and Nestor stood up among the Argives and spoke:

124"Shame! Great grief comes upon the Achaean land.

125How the old horseman Peleus would groan aloud,8

126the good counselor of the Myrmidons, their speaker —

127who once in his own house delighted in questioning me

128for the lineage and the birth of every man of the Argives.

129If he heard of them now, cowering, every one, before Hector,

130he would lift his dear hands to the immortals, over and over,

131praying his spirit down out of his body into Hades' house.

132O father Zeus, Athena, Apollo —

133would I were young, as when by quick-running Celadon

134the Pylians and the spear-mad Arcadians met and fought

135by the walls of Pheia, around the streams of Iardanus.

136Their champion stood forth: Ereuthalion, a man like a god,9

137wearing on his shoulders the armor of lord Areithous —

138brilliant Areithous, whom men called by his surname

139Club-man — the men, and the fair-belted women —

140because he fought with no bow, no long spear:

141with a club of iron he smashed the companies apart.

142Lycurgus killed him — by craft, not force at all —

143in a narrow lane, where the iron club was no use

144to ward destruction off: Lycurgus was in first,

145pinned him through the middle with his spear, and he crashed back on the earth;

146and he stripped the armor brazen Ares had given him.

147That armor he wore himself, after, in the grind of Ares;

148and when Lycurgus was growing old in his halls

149he gave it to Ereuthalion, his dear aide, to wear —

150and wearing it, Ereuthalion challenged all our best.

151They trembled, they were terrified; no man dared.

152But my much-enduring heart drove me on

153to fight him with its daring — and I was youngest-born of all —

154and I fought him, and Athena gave me my glory.

155The tallest and the strongest man I ever killed:

156a great sprawl of him lay there, this way and that.

157Would that I were young like that, my strength still steady!

158Hector of the flashing helmet would soon have his fight.

159But you — you who are the chief men of all the Achaeans —

160not even you are heartily eager to go and face Hector."

161So the old man raked them; and nine men in all stood up.10

162First by far to rise was the lord of men Agamemnon;

163after him rose Tydeus' son, strong Diomedes;

164after them the two Ajaxes, clothed in rushing courage;

165after them Idomeneus, and Idomeneus' comrade

166Meriones, the equal of Enyalius, slayer of men;

167after them Eurypylus, Euaemon's shining son;

168and up rose Thoas, Andraemon's son, and brilliant Odysseus:

169all these were willing to fight brilliant Hector.

170And among them spoke the Gerenian horseman Nestor:

171"Shake now the lots, all of you, for the man who draws it:

172he is the one who will profit the strong-greaved Achaeans,

173and he will profit his own heart too, if he escapes

174out of the fury of war and the dread combat."

175So he spoke, and each man marked his lot

176and they threw them into the helmet of Atreus' son Agamemnon;

177and the armies prayed, and lifted their hands to the gods,

178and thus would one say, looking into the wide sky:

179"Father Zeus, let it be Ajax, or Tydeus' son,

180or the king himself of Mycenae rich in gold."

181So they spoke; and the Gerenian horseman Nestor shook,

182and out of the helmet leapt the lot they had wished for —

183Ajax's. And a herald carried it everywhere through the crowd,

184showing it left to right to all the chief men of the Achaeans;

185and each, not knowing the mark, denied it was his.

186But when, carrying it everywhere through the crowd, he came

187to the man who had marked it and thrown it in the helmet — glorious Ajax —

188Ajax held out his hand, and the herald stood by him and dropped it in,

189and he knew his lot's mark at a glance, and rejoiced at heart.11

190He threw it to the ground beside his foot, and called out:

191"Friends — the lot is mine! And I am glad

192with my whole heart, for I think I shall beat brilliant Hector.

193Come then: while I put on my battle armor,

194you pray to lord Zeus, the son of Cronos —

195in silence, to yourselves, so the Trojans do not hear —

196or aloud, for that matter, since we fear no man in any case.12

197For no man by force, by his will against my will, shall drive me off,

198nor by know-how either; I hope I was not born in Salamis

199and raised there to be such a know-nothing as that."

200So he spoke, and they prayed to lord Zeus, son of Cronos,

201and thus would one say, looking into the wide sky:

202"Father Zeus, ruling from Ida, most glorious, greatest:

203grant Ajax the victory and the winning of shining glory;

204but if you love Hector too, and care for him,

205then give to both of them equal strength and glory."

206So they spoke; and Ajax armed himself in gleaming bronze.

207And when he had put all his armor about his body,

208he charged out, huge as Ares comes on

209when he goes to war among men whom the son of Cronos

210has thrown together to fight in the fury of heart-devouring strife.

211Such was gigantic Ajax as he rose, wall of the Achaeans,

212smiling with his grim face; and beneath him his feet

213went in long strides, and he shook his long-shadowed spear.

214And the Argives rejoiced to look at him,

215but a dread trembling came into the limbs of every Trojan,

216and Hector's own heart hammered in his chest;

217but there was no way now to shrink back or retreat

218into the crowd of his people: he had made the challenge.

219Ajax came near, carrying his shield like a tower,

220bronze, of seven oxhides, which Tychius labored to make,13

221far the best of leather-cutters, whose home was in Hyle:

222he made him the glancing shield of seven hides

223from well-fed bulls, and hammered on an eighth layer of bronze.

224Carrying it before his chest, Telamonian Ajax

225stood close in front of Hector, and spoke to him in threat:

226"Hector, now you will learn, plainly, man to man,

227what sort of chief men the Danaans have among them

228even after Achilles, breaker of men, the lion-heart.14

229He lies among his beaked seagoing ships,

230nursing his rage at Agamemnon, shepherd of the people;

231but we are such as can stand up against you —

232and there are many of us. Begin the fight."

233Then great Hector of the flashing helmet answered him:

234"Ajax, Zeus-sprung son of Telamon, marshal of the people:

235do not test me like some feeble boy

236or a woman, who does not know the works of war.

237I know battles; I know the killings of men.

238I know how to swing my dried oxhide to the right,

239I know how to swing it left — that, to me, is shieldcraft that lasts;

240I know how to charge into the tumult of fast mares;

241and I know, at close quarters, the dance of hostile Ares.15

242But I have no wish to hit a man of your size

243by stealth, watching my moment: openly, if I can."

244He spoke, and balancing his long-shadowed spear he hurled it,

245and struck the terrible sevenhide shield of Ajax

246on the outermost bronze, the eighth layer upon it:

247through six folds the unwearying bronze went shearing,

248and in the seventh hide it held. Second in turn,

249Zeus-sprung Ajax hurled his long-shadowed spear

250and struck the shield of Priam's son, balanced every way.16

251Through the shining shield the heavy spear went,

252and through the richly worked corslet it drove;

253and straight on beside his flank the spear sheared through

254the tunic — but he had swerved aside, and dodged black doom.

255Then the two of them, pulling out the long spears with their hands,

256fell on each other like lions that eat flesh raw

257or wild boars, whose strength does not give out.17

258Priam's son stabbed the shield full in the middle with his spear,

259but the bronze did not break through: its point was bent back.

260And Ajax, leaping in, pierced the shield: straight through

261the spear went, and staggered him in his fury;

262it came cutting in a graze at his neck, and the black blood welled up.

263But even so Hector of the flashing helmet did not leave the fight:

264he gave ground, and seized in his thick hand a stone

265lying in the plain, black and rough and huge;

266with it he struck the terrible sevenhide shield of Ajax

267full on the central boss, and the bronze rang out around.

268Then Ajax in turn lifted a far greater stone,

269whirled, and threw it, putting measureless force behind it,

270and broke the shield inward with the millstone rock

271and hurt his knees under him; and he was stretched on his back,

272pinned to the shield. But Apollo straightened him at once.18

273And now they would have been stabbing hand to hand with swords,

274had not the heralds, messengers of Zeus and of men,

275come between — one from the Trojans, one from the bronze-shirted

276Achaeans: Talthybius and Idaeus, wise men both.

277They held their staffs between the two; and the herald Idaeus,

278whose mind was full of wise counsel, spoke his word:

279"Fight no more, dear sons; battle no longer.

280Zeus the cloud-gatherer loves you both alike,

281and both of you are spearmen: that we all know.

282And night is coming on. It is good to yield to night as well."19

283Answering him spoke Telamonian Ajax:

284"Idaeus, tell Hector to say those things:

285it was he who challenged all our best men to the fight.

286Let him lead; and I will follow whatever he does."

287Then great Hector of the flashing helmet answered him:

288"Ajax — since a god has given you stature and strength

289and good sense, and with the spear you are strongest of the Achaeans,

290let us stop our fighting and our combat

291for today. Later we will fight again, until the god

292decides between us, and hands victory to one side or the other.

293And night is coming on. It is good to yield to night as well —

294so you may gladden all the Achaeans by the ships,

295your kinsmen above all, and the companions who are yours;

296and I, through the great city of lord Priam,

297will gladden the Trojans and the Trojan women with their trailing gowns,

298who will go into the gods' assembly-place with prayers for me.

299Come — let us give each other glorious gifts,

300so that Achaeans and Trojans alike will say:

301'The two of them fought in heart-devouring strife,

302and parted again joined together in friendship.'"

303So speaking, he gave him his silver-studded sword,

304bringing it with its sheath and its well-cut baldric;

305and Ajax gave his war-belt, bright with crimson.20

306So the two parted: one went to the Achaean host,

307the other to the crowd of the Trojans. And they rejoiced

308to see him come to them alive and unhurt,

309escaped from the fury of Ajax and his untouchable hands,

310and led him to the city, scarcely believing him safe.

311And on the other side the strong-greaved Achaeans led Ajax,

312glad in his victory, to brilliant Agamemnon.

313And when they were gathered in the huts of Atreus' son,

314the lord of men Agamemnon slaughtered an ox for them,

315a male of five years, to the overmastering son of Cronos.

316They flayed it and dressed it, and cut it all apart,

317and deftly chopped it small and threaded it on spits,

318roasted it with care, and drew it all off.

319Then when they had finished the work and made the meal ready,

320they feasted, and no heart lacked a fair share of the feast;

321and Ajax was honored with the long cuts of the chine21

322by the hero, Atreus' son, wide-ruling Agamemnon.

323And when they had put away desire for eating and drinking,

324the old man first of all began to weave his counsel —

325Nestor, whose plan had shown itself best before as well.

326With good will toward them he spoke and addressed them:

327"Son of Atreus, and you other chief men of all the Achaeans:

328many of the long-haired Achaeans are dead,

329whose dark blood sharp Ares has scattered now

330along fair-flowing Scamander, and their souls have gone down to Hades.

331Therefore with dawn have the Achaeans stop the fighting;

332and let us gather and wheel the corpses back here

333with oxen and mules, and burn them

334a little off from the ships, so each man may carry the bones

335home to the children, when we return to our fatherland.22

336And around the pyre let us heap a single mound, drawn up

337from the plain, one mound for all alike; and against it build

338with all speed high towers, a shelter for the ships and ourselves.

339And in them let us make close-fitted gates,

340so that a road for chariots runs through;

341and outside, close by, let us dig a deep ditch

342to circle it, and hold off chariots and men,

343so the war of the proud Trojans never breaks over us."

344So he spoke, and all the kings approved.

345Now the Trojans held assembly on the height of Ilion,

346a dread assembly, all in tumult, at Priam's doors;

347and among them wise Antenor began to speak:

348"Hear from me, Trojans and Dardanians and allies,

349so I may say what the heart in my chest commands me.

350Come now: Argive Helen and her possessions with her

351let us give to the sons of Atreus to lead away. Now it is with sworn

352oaths made false that we fight; and so I have no hope

353that anything will end well for us, unless we do this."23

354So he spoke and sat down; and among them rose

355brilliant Alexandros, husband of lovely-haired Helen,

356who spoke to him in answer with winged words:

357"Antenor, this talk of yours pleases me no longer;

358you know how to think another word, better than this one.

359But if you truly say this thing in earnest,

360then the gods themselves have destroyed your wits.

361And I will speak out among the horse-taming Trojans:

362I say it straight out — the woman I will not give back.

363But the goods, all I brought from Argos to our house —

364all these I am willing to give, and to add more of my own."

365So he spoke and sat down; and among them rose

366Priam, Dardanus' descendant, the equal of the gods in counsel.

367With good will toward them he spoke and addressed them:

368"Hear from me, Trojans and Dardanians and allies,

369so I may say what the heart in my chest commands me.

370Now take your supper through the city, as before,

371and remember the watch, and stay awake, every man.

372At dawn let Idaeus go to the hollow ships

373to tell the sons of Atreus, Agamemnon and Menelaus,

374the word of Alexandros, for whose sake this quarrel rose;

375and to say this shrewd word too: whether they are willing

376to rest from the harsh-crying war until we burn

377the dead. Later we will fight again, until the god

378decides between us, and hands victory to one side or the other."

379So he spoke, and they listened well to him and obeyed;

380and they took their supper through the army, in their companies.

381At dawn Idaeus went to the hollow ships,

382and found the Danaans, squires of Ares, in assembly

383by the stern of Agamemnon's ship; and among them,

384standing in the middle, the far-calling herald spoke:

385"Son of Atreus, and you other chief men of all the Achaeans:

386Priam and the other proud Trojans command me

387to tell you — if it should be welcome and sweet to you —

388the word of Alexandros, for whose sake this quarrel rose.

389The goods that Alexandros brought to Troy in his hollow

390ships — how he should have perished first! —

391all these he is willing to give, and to add more of his own.

392But the wedded wife of glorious Menelaus

393he says he will not give — though the Trojans, I tell you, command it.

394And they told me to say this too: whether you are willing

395to rest from the harsh-crying war until we burn

396the dead. Later we will fight again, until the god

397decides between us, and hands victory to one side or the other."

398So he spoke, and they all went still in silence.

399At long last Diomedes, good at the war-cry, spoke:

400"Let no man now accept the goods of Alexandros —

401nor Helen either. It is known, even to a plain fool,

402that the cables of destruction are fastened on the Trojans already."24

403So he spoke, and all the sons of the Achaeans shouted,

404marveling at the word of Diomedes, breaker of horses;

405and then the lord Agamemnon spoke to Idaeus:

406"Idaeus — you hear the word of the Achaeans yourself,

407how they give you their answer. And it pleases me so.

408But as for the dead — I do not grudge their burning.

409There is no stinting toward the bodies of the dead:

410once they are gone, be quick with the fire's appeasement.

411And let Zeus the loud-thunderer, Hera's lord, witness the oaths."

412So speaking he lifted up the scepter to all the gods,

413and Idaeus went back toward holy Ilion.

414The Trojans and Dardanians were sitting in assembly,

415all gathered together, waiting for the moment

416Idaeus should come; and he came, and delivered his message,

417standing in the middle. Then they made ready with all speed,

418in two parties: one to bring the corpses, one to fetch wood;

419and the Argives on their side, from the well-benched ships,

420hurried too — some to bring the corpses, others to fetch wood.

421The sun was just striking the fields with its first light,

422climbing into the sky out of the deep, soft-flowing

423stream of Ocean, when the two sides met each other.

424Then it was hard to know each man again;

425but washing off the clotted gore with water,

426and letting warm tears fall, they lifted them onto the wagons.

427But great Priam would not let them wail; so in silence25

428they heaped the corpses on the pyre, grieving at heart,

429and when they had burned them in the fire, went to holy Ilion.

430And just so, on the other side, the strong-greaved Achaeans

431heaped the corpses on the pyre, grieving at heart,

432and when they had burned them in the fire, went to the hollow ships.

433And when it was not yet dawn, but the night still half-light,26

434then a picked company of Achaeans woke around the pyre,

435and they heaped around it a single mound, drawn up

436from the plain, one mound for all alike; and against it they built

437a wall with high towers, a shelter for the ships and themselves.

438And in them they set close-fitted gates,

439so that a road for chariots ran through;

440and outside they dug a deep ditch hard against it,

441wide and great, and planted a row of stakes within it.

442So the long-haired Achaeans labored;

443and the gods, sitting beside Zeus the lord of lightning,

444watched the great work of the bronze-shirted Achaeans.

445And Poseidon the earth-shaker began to speak among them:

446"Father Zeus, is there a mortal left on the boundless earth

447who will still tell the immortals his mind and purpose?

448Do you not see? The long-haired Achaeans

449have built themselves a wall to landward of the ships, and driven

450a ditch around it — and gave the gods no glorious hecatombs!

451The fame of this will spread as far as the dawn-light scatters,

452and men will forget that wall which I and Phoebus Apollo

453built for the hero Laomedon, laboring hard."

454Deeply troubled, Zeus the cloud-gatherer answered him:

455"Shame, wide-ruling earth-shaker — what a thing to say!

456Some other god might tremble at this thought,

457one far feebler than you in hand and fury:

458your fame will spread as far as the dawn-light scatters.

459Come now: when the long-haired Achaeans are gone

460again with their ships to their dear fatherland,

461break the wall up, sweep it all into the sea,

462and hide the wide beach over again with sand,

463so the great wall of the Achaeans may be effaced."27

464So they spoke such things to one another;

465and the sun went down, and the Achaeans' work was finished,

466and they slaughtered oxen through the huts, and took their supper.

467And ships had come in from Lemnos, carrying wine,

468many of them, sent out by Jason's son Euneus,

469whom Hypsipyle bore to Jason, shepherd of the people.

470Separately, for the sons of Atreus, Agamemnon and Menelaus,

471Jason's son had given a thousand measures of wine to carry.

472From these ships the long-haired Achaeans bought their wine,

473some with bronze, and some with gleaming iron,

474some with hides, and some with the live cattle themselves,

475and some with slaves; and they set the rich feast going.28

476All night long the long-haired Achaeans

477feasted, and the Trojans through the city, and their allies;

478and all night long Zeus the counselor devised evil against them,

479thundering terribly. And green fear took hold of them:29

480they poured the wine from their cups onto the ground, and no man

481dared drink before he poured to the overmastering son of Cronos.

482Then they lay down, and took the gift of sleep.

Notes

1 lines 8–10 — Paris kills Menesthius, son of Areithous "the club-man" — the very Areithous whose story Nestor will tell in full at 136-149: the son dies at the book's door, the father's legend follows. A plant-and-payoff inside a single book. ↩

2 lines 44–53 — Helenus "put together in his heart" the gods' private plan — how the seer overheard a conversation between Apollo and Athena was already a problem for ancient critics; Aristarchus athetized 53 ("so I heard it in the voice of the gods"), the line that makes the eavesdropping explicit (apparatus, Line 53). Kept: seercraft as reception of divine speech is exactly Helenus's office (6.76). ↩

3 lines 58–61 — Athena and Apollo settle on the oak of Zeus "in the likeness of vulture birds, delighting in the men" — the gods as spectators, perched carrion-fashion above the duel they arranged. The poem's frankest image of the divine audience. ↩

4 lines 76–91 — Hector's terms and the mound-speech: armor to the victor, the corpse home for its "due of fire" — and then the imagined sailor of a later age reading the tomb by the Hellespont. The poem's clearest statement of the kleos-economy, spoken from the winner's side of an imagined victory: even the man Hector kills is conscripted into Hector's undying fame. (The Iliad's audience knew such mounds on that shore.) ↩

5 line 96 — "Women of Achaea, men of Achaea no longer" — Menelaus borrows Thersites' taunt verbatim (2.235): the loudmouth's insult has become respectable under shame's pressure. (Echo note; English repeats 2.235's rendering.) ↩

6 lines 113–114 — "Even Achilles... shivered to meet this man." The absent hero as unit of measure again (cf. 2.768-770, 5.788-791) — here bent into hyperbole: nothing in the poem shows Achilles fearing Hector, and Book 22 will show the reverse. Agamemnon's flattery of Hector serves his need to stop his brother. ↩

7 lines 120–121 — αἴσιμα παρειπών — "his urging was of things due" — the same narratorial formula that follows Agamemnon's atrocity-counsel at 6.62. Here the counsel is unimpeachable (saving Menelaus from a fatal mismatch), which weighs on the Book 6 dispute: the formula commends the persuasion's aptness, not its morality. ↩

8 lines 124–131 — Nestor conjures Peleus at home — the old man who once joyfully catalogued the Argives' lineages, now imagined praying for death at news of their shame. First full sounding of the Peleus-motif: the father at home as the war's moral audience (Odysseus will weaponize the same figure to Achilles at 9.252-259). ↩

9 lines 132–156 — The Ereuthalion story, promised at 4.319 and delivered here: Areithous the Club-man with his iron club (iron again the marked exception, cf. 4.123), killed by craft in a lane too narrow to swing in; the armor's passage from Ares' gift to Lycurgus to Ereuthalion; and young Nestor, "youngest-born of all," taking down the giant. This Lycurgus is an Arcadian mortal — not the Thracian god-fighter of 6.130-140 who shares the name. ↩

10 lines 161–169 — The nine volunteers — the poem's explicit roster of the best available Achaeans with Achilles out: both Atreidae, Diomedes, both Ajaxes, Idomeneus, Meriones, Eurypylus, Thoas, Odysseus. The lot-prayer at 179-180 then ranks the top three in the army's own eyes: Ajax, Diomedes, Agamemnon. ↩

11 lines 175–189 — The lot-procedure: each man scratches his mark (σῆμα) on his token; the herald carries the leaping lot around until its owner recognizes it. Like the "baneful signs" of 6.168, marks that identify without being read as language — the poem's world writes tokens, not texts. ↩

12 lines 195–199 — Ajax's instructions — pray silently so the Trojans don't hear, "or aloud, for that matter, since we fear no man" — athetized by Zenodotus, Aristophanes, and Aristarchus all three (apparatus, Line 195-199). The swagger is pure Ajax; kept. ↩

13 lines 219–223 — The tower shield: seven oxhides and an eighth face of bronze, made by Tychius of Hyle, "far the best of leather-cutters" — the craftsman-vignette again (cf. Phereclus, 5.59-64). Ajax's body-length shield is older military technology than the round shields elsewhere in the poem — a fossil the tradition preserved with the hero it belonged to. ↩

14 lines 226–232 — "even after Achilles, breaker of men, the lion-heart" — Ajax concedes the ranking in the act of boasting; the poem's second-best hero is the one who says "second" out loud (cf. 2.768-769). ↩

15 lines 237–241 — Hector's credo: shield-work right and left, the charge, and "at close quarters, the dance of hostile Ares" — war as a craft with figures and steps (μέλπεσθαι, properly of dance and song). The self-description of a man who has made himself skilled at what he tells Andromache he has "learned" to do (6.444-445). ↩

16 lines 244–254 — The spear-exchange replays the Paris-Menelaus duel verbatim: 250-254 = 3.355-360, and the English here repeats the Book 3 rendering word for word. The rematch of Book 3 with the war's real principals is scored on the same notes — except that this time both men are too good for the first exchange to decide anything. ↩

17 lines 255–257 — The lions-and-boars simile — athetized by Zenodotus (apparatus, Line 255-257); nearly the same pair as 5.782-783. Kept, as the moment the formal duel becomes simply combat. ↩

18 lines 268–272 — The stones: Hector's blow rings the bronze; Ajax's millstone rock breaks the shield inward and folds Hector's knees — "but Apollo straightened him at once." The god's whole intervention in five words; without it the duel (and the poem) ends here. ↩

19 lines 279–282 — The heralds part them with staffs, and Idaeus — the Trojan herald of 3.248 — gives the evening its proverb: "It is good to yield to night as well." Night as the arbiter both armies obey; Hector repeats the line back with full agreement (293). ↩

20 lines 287–305 — The gift-exchange: Hector's silver-studded sword for Ajax's crimson war-belt, "so that Achaeans and Trojans alike will say... they parted again joined together in friendship." Tradition made both gifts fatal: Ajax falls on Hector's sword (Sophocles' Ajax), and Hector's corpse is dragged by Ajax's belt — "the gifts of enemies are no gifts." The poem itself says nothing; the exchange is left shining. (Aristarchus athetized 295, the "kinsmen and companions" line, within Hector's speech.) ↩

21 lines 321–322 — The chine — the unbroken length of the back-cut — is the portion of honor, awarded to Ajax by Agamemnon: the feast as scoreboard (cf. Idomeneus's ever-full cup, 4.257-263). ↩

22 lines 327–343 — Nestor's double proposal: burn the dead, and build the wall. Aristarchus athetized 334-335 — carrying the bones "home to the children" reflects classical Athenian practice (the state funeral), not Homeric cremation-and-mound, and the couplet has long been suspected as an Athenian interpolation (apparatus, Line 334-335). The wall itself is the poem's new stage-set: Books 8-15 will be fought around it. ↩

23 lines 345–353 — Antenor moves to give Helen back — the appeasement party in Troy made explicit (he hosted the pre-war embassy, 3.205-224; later tradition spared his house at the sack for it). Aristarchus athetized 353. Paris's counter-offer — goods yes, "the woman I will not give back" — and Priam's meek relay of it complete the pattern: Troy's assembly knows the right answer and cannot act on it. ↩

24 lines 399–402 — Diomedes' refusal: take neither the goods "nor Helen either — the cables of destruction are fastened on the Trojans already." The rope-image answers Menelaus's "ends of victory... held above" (101-102): the gods hold the rope-ends of outcomes. The young man silent under rebuke in Book 4 now answers embassies for the army, with the crowd shouting assent — the arc runs straight on to 9.32-49. (Echo note.) ↩

25 lines 421–432 — The shared field at dawn: both sides washing the clotted blood from the dead they cannot recognize, "letting warm tears fall" — and Priam forbidding lament, as the Achaeans mourn unforbidden. The armies' identical griefs are given identical lines (428-429 = 431-432, Ilion for the ships); the translation keeps the symmetry exact. ↩

26 lines 433–441 — "not yet dawn, but the night still half-light" (ἀμφιλύκη νύξ — only here in Homer): in the grey hour, the Achaeans raise one mound "for all alike" (ἄκριτον — undivided, no man singled) — the common grave of a common army, in contrast to every individual mound the poem imagines (cf. 86-91). ↩

27 lines 443–464 — The gods audit the wall: Poseidon's professional jealousy (he and Apollo built Laomedon's wall — the last time Troy's ruler cheated them, 21.441-457), the missing hecatombs, and Zeus's remedy: erase it when the war ends, sea and sand over everything. Zenodotus, Aristophanes, and Aristarchus athetized the whole exchange (apparatus, Line 443-464); Aristotle admired it as the poet "effacing" his own invention — the poem arranges in advance why no trace of its greatest earthwork survives on the real coast (the demolition comes at 12.13-33). ↩

28 lines 467–475 — The wine-fleet from Lemnos, sent by Euneus, son of Jason and Hypsipyle — the poem's one glance at the Argonaut generation, now middle-aged commerce. The market-list prices the camp's economy: bronze, iron, hides, live cattle — "and some with slaves" (ἀνδραπόδεσσι, the only occurrence of the classical word for chattel-slaves in Homer; all three Alexandrians athetized the line, partly for that anachronism). Rendered "slaves" under the project's policy: the traffic the poem's captives fear is here, briefly, a line-item. ↩

29 lines 478–482 — The book closes on the uneasy feast: Zeus thundering all night "devising evil," green fear (χλωρὸν δέος — the fixed color of dread) on both armies, and no man drinking undedicated wine. After a day of institutions — duel, truce, wall, market — the last word is the god's weather, and it is against them. ↩
