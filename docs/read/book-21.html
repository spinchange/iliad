Book XXI

Half the rout is driven into the river, and the river fights back: Lycaon's bared throat, Asteropaeus's two spears, Scamander rising against the man who has choked him with corpses — water against fire, gods against gods — until Troy's gates gape for the routed, and Apollo, wearing a man's face, runs Achilles out of the way.

Translator’s note on Book 21

Book 21 is the wildest book of the Iliad — the one where the fighting bursts every frame that holds it: ransom, river-banks, even the difference between the elements. It opens at the ford of Xanthus (the lines repeat 14.433-434, where the stunned Hector was laid at this same crossing: the river of Trojan refuge becomes the trap), where Achilles cuts the rout in two, drives half into the water, and wades in after them with a sword — locusts before a grass-fire, fish before a dolphin. He takes twelve boys alive for Patroclus's pyre, collecting the vow of 18.336-337, and then meets Lycaon.

The Lycaon scene (34-135) is the moral center of the second half of the poem. The poem gives him the fullest biography of any minor figure — caught at night cutting fig-shoots for chariot-rails, sold to Lemnos, ransomed, home eleven days, and on the twelfth delivered by "the god" back into the same hands — precisely so that his death will be the death of the old economy: Achilles had sold him once, because "sparing was dearer to my heart"; now "Fool — offer me no ransom." What follows the refusal is the most quoted consolation in Greek literature, spoken with the spear raised: "So, friend — you die too. Why lament it so? Patroclus died, and he was a far better man than you... over me too stand death and strong fate: there will be a dawn, or an evening, or a midday..." (note 6). The word is φίλος — friend — and it is not irony: it is the fellowship of the doomed, which is the only fellowship Achilles has left. Lycaon opens his arms and takes the sword; the body goes to the fish, with a taunt that cancels every funeral rite the poem holds dear — and the river begins to be angry.

Asteropaeus, who fights with a spear in each hand and draws the only blood Achilles loses in the Iliad, dies over the argument his corpse then illustrates: rivers against Zeus. Achilles' boast descends the ladder — Zeus beats rivers; not even Achelous, "not even the great strength of deep-flowing Ocean, from whom all rivers and all the sea and all springs and deep wells flow," can face the thunderbolt. Zenodotus's text omitted the Ocean line, crowning Achelous the first of waters — the oldest recorded Greek quarrel about cosmology, sitting in our apparatus (note 10). Then the river itself has had enough: "my lovely streams are full of corpses... and you kill and kill." The battle with Scamander (233-382) is the poem's one war between a man and an element, and it strips Achilles to something no enemy has seen: running, outrun, wailing to Zeus that his mother lied, dreading the "wretched death" of a boy swept down a gully — death by water, without glory, without a grave. The river's threat makes that explicit in the poem's bitterest mock-burial: sand and shingle heaped so deep "the Achaeans will not know where to gather his bones... he will need no heaped tomb" (note 16). Rescue comes as plot: Poseidon and Athena, hand in hand, dictate the rest of the epic — pen the Trojans, kill Hector, take the glory — and Hera throws Hephaestus at the water. Fire drinks the plain; the riverside trees burn by name; eels tumble; the god of water surrenders while boiling "like a cauldron melting the lard of a fatted hog."

Then the Theomachy the poem has owed us since Book 20 — and it is played as comedy under one laughing spectator ("Zeus heard, sitting on Olympus, and his own heart laughed for joy"). Athena fells Ares with a boundary-stone and Aphrodite with a fist; Hera boxes Artemis's ears with her own bow; Hermes politely forfeits to Leto; Artemis ends in Zeus's lap in tears. Only one exchange is in earnest: Poseidon reminds Apollo of Laomedon's cheat — the wall built for a wage never paid, the threatened ear-lopping — and Apollo refuses combat with the most devastating thing any god says about us in the poem: mortals are not worth a fight, "who like the leaves now flourish in their blaze... and now wither heartless away" — Glaucus's leaf-simile of 6.146 returned from above, as contempt (note 24). The gods go home; Achilles remains — smoke of a burning city — and the book ends with Priam opening the gates, Agenor's lonely stand (the soliloquy where a frightened man discovers that Achilles too "has one life"), and Apollo in Agenor's shape running the fastest man alive across the plain, "always just a little ahead," while everything that is left of Troy's army pours inside the walls — everything except the man who will be standing outside them when the next book begins.

2eddying Xanthus, whom immortal Zeus begot,

3there he cut them in two: one half he chased over the plain

4toward the city, where the Achaeans had fled in terror

5the day before, when glorious Hector raged —

6there they streamed in rout, and Hera spread deep mist

7in front of them, to hold them; and the other half

8were rolled into the deep-flowing, silver-eddying river.

9In they fell with a great crash, and the sheer current roared,

10and the banks rang loud around them; and shouting they

11swam this way and that, spun about in the eddies.

12As when locusts rise on the air before the rush of fire

13and flee toward a river: the unwearying flame burns on,

14leaping up suddenly, and they cower down in the water:

15so before Achilles the sounding stream of Xanthus,

16the deep-eddying, was choked with horses and men together.

17And the Zeus-born one left his spear there on the bank,2

18leaned on the tamarisks, and leapt in like more than man,

19holding only his sword, with dark work in his heart,

20and struck on every side; and an ugly groaning rose

21from the men the blade hit, and the water reddened with blood.

22As before a huge-mawed dolphin the other fish

23flee and fill the inlets of a fair-anchoring harbor,

24terrified — for he devours whatever he catches:

26cowered under the overhangs. And when his hands were weary of killing,

29He led them out dazed, like fawns,

31they wore on their own supple tunics,

33Then back he sprang, wild to be killing.

34There he met a son of Priam, Dardanus's descendant,3

35running out of the river: Lycaon — whom he himself

36had taken once by night, and led away unwilling

37from his father's orchard: with the sharp bronze he was cutting

40That time he shipped him to well-built Lemnos and sold him,

41and the son of Jason paid the price;

42from there a guest-friend ransomed him — he paid a fortune —

43Eëtion of Imbros, and sent him to shining Arisbe;

44and slipping away from there, he reached his father's house.

45Eleven days he delighted his heart among his dear ones,

46home from Lemnos; and on the twelfth the god threw him

47once more into the hands of Achilles, who was to send him

48down to the house of Hades — not willing to go.

49Now when swift-footed brilliant Achilles saw him —4

50naked, without helmet or shield, and no spear either:

51he had thrown it all to the ground, for the sweat wore him down

52as he ran from the river, and weariness broke his knees under him —

53troubled, Achilles spoke to his own great-hearted spirit:

54"Shame! — a great marvel is this I see with my eyes:

55so the great-hearted Trojans I have killed

56will rise again out of the murky dark below,

57the way this man has come back, escaping the pitiless day —

58sold into holy Lemnos! And the deep of the grey sea

59did not hold him, which holds so many against their will.

60Well then: now he shall taste the point of our spear

61as well — so I may see in my mind, and learn for certain,

62whether he will come back equally from down there, or be held

63by the life-giving earth, which holds down even the strong."

64So he weighed it, and waited. And the man came near him, dazed,5

65desperate to touch his knees: for his heart's whole wish

66was to escape from evil death and the black spirit of destruction.

67Brilliant Achilles lifted the long spear

68meaning to stab; but he ducked under and grasped his knees,

69crouching; and the spear passed over his back and stood in the earth,

70planted, still hungry for the flesh of a man.

71With one hand Lycaon held his knees and begged,

72and with the other gripped the sharp spear, and would not let go,

73and speaking winged words he addressed him:

74"By your knees, Achilles: respect me, and pity me.

75I stand to you, Zeus-nurtured, as a suppliant with a claim:

76it was at your table first that I ate the grain of Demeter,

77on the day you took me in the well-built orchard,

78and led me away, far from father and friends, and sold me

79into holy Lemnos: I brought you the price of a hundred oxen.

80I was freed for three times that; and this dawn

81is my twelfth since I came back to Ilion

82after much suffering. And now murderous fate has put me

83in your hands again. I must be hateful to father Zeus,

84who gave me to you twice. To a short life my mother

85bore me — Laothoe, daughter of the old man Altes:

86Altes, who is lord of the war-loving Lelegians,

87holding steep Pedasus on the Satnioeis.

88Priam had his daughter — among his many wives;

89and of her we two were born, and you will butcher both.

90One you have broken already among the front-rank foot-fighters —

91godlike Polydorus, when you hit him with your sharp spear;

92and now, here, it will be evil for me: for I do not hope

93to escape your hands, since the god has driven me into them.

94But I will say one thing more — take it into your heart:

95do not kill me. I am not from the same womb as Hector,

96who killed your companion, the gentle and the strong."

97So the shining son of Priam spoke to him,6

98a speech of begging; but the voice he heard was without honey:

99"Fool — offer me no ransom. Make me no speeches.

100Before Patroclus met his day of destiny,

101sparing Trojans was dearer to my heart:

102many I took alive, and sold abroad.

103Now there is not one who escapes death — not one the god

104delivers into my hands in front of Ilion —

105not one of all the Trojans, and of Priam's sons beyond all.

106So, friend — you die too. Why lament it so?

107Patroclus died, and he was a far better man than you.

108And do you not see what I am — how beautiful, how big?

109My father is a noble man, a goddess was the mother who bore me:

110yet over me too stand death, and strong fate.

111There will be a dawn, or an evening, or a midday,

112when someone takes my life too out of me in the fighting,

113whether he hits me with a spear or an arrow from the string."

114So he spoke; and there the man's knees went slack, and his own heart.7

115He let go of the spear, and sat back, spreading both hands

116wide. And Achilles drew his sharp sword

117and struck at the collarbone, beside the neck, and the whole

118two-edged blade went in; and face-down on the earth

119he lay stretched out, and the black blood ran, and soaked the ground.

120Achilles seized him by the foot and slung him into the river,

121and cried in triumph over him with winged words:

122"Lie there now among the fish: they will lick the blood

123from your wound, uncaring. No mother will lay you out

124on a bed and mourn you: eddying Scamander

125will carry you into the broad gulf of the sea,

126and many a fish, leaping along the wave, will dart up

127under the black ripple to eat the white fat of Lycaon.

128Perish, all of you — till we reach the city of holy Ilion,

129you running, and I cutting you down from behind!

130Not even the river will save you — the fair-flowing,

131silver-eddying river you have honored so long with slaughtered bulls,

132drowning single-hoofed horses alive in his eddies.

133Even so you shall die evil deaths — every one — till all of you

134pay for the killing of Patroclus and the ruin of the Achaeans

135whom you cut down beside the swift ships when I was away."

136So he spoke; and the river's anger rose the higher in his heart,

137and he turned over in his mind how to stop brilliant Achilles

138in his work, and beat destruction away from the Trojans.

139Meanwhile the son of Peleus, with his long-shadowed spear,8

140sprang on Asteropaeus, wild to kill him —

141the son of Pelegon, whom wide-flowing Axius

142begot on Periboea, eldest of the daughters

143of Acessamenus: the deep-eddying river lay with her.

144On him Achilles rushed; and he stood up out of the river

145to face him, holding two spears; and Xanthus put fury

146in his chest, in anger for the young men cut down in the fighting,

147the ones Achilles was butchering down the stream without pity.

148And when they had come close, advancing on each other,

149the first to speak was swift-footed brilliant Achilles:

150"Who are you — of what men — that you dare come against me?

151Unhappy are the fathers whose sons face my fury!"

152And the shining son of Pelegon answered him:

153"Great-hearted son of Peleus, why ask my lineage?

154I am from Paeonia of the deep soil — a far country —

155leading the Paeonian men of the long lances; and this dawn

156is my eleventh now, since I came to Ilion.

157My line is from Axius, the wide-flowing —

158Axius, who sends the most beautiful water over the earth;

159he fathered Pelegon, famed with the spear; and of Pelegon

160they say I was born. Now let us fight, brilliant Achilles."

161So he spoke, threatening; and brilliant Achilles raised9

162the Pelian ash. But the hero Asteropaeus threw

163both spears at once — for he was a two-handed fighter.

164With the one spear he hit the shield, but did not

165break the shield: the gold held it, the god's gift;

166with the other he grazed the elbow of the right arm,

167and the dark blood shot out; and the spear, passing over him,

168stuck fast in the earth, still craving its fill of flesh.

169Second in turn Achilles let fly his straight-flying ash

170at Asteropaeus, wild to kill him —

171and missed the man, and hit the high bank,

172and drove the ash spear half its length into the bank.

173Then the son of Peleus, drawing the sharp sword from his thigh,

174leapt on him, furious; and the man could not wrench

175Achilles' ash out of the bluff with his thick hand.

176Three times he shook it, straining to pull it free,

177and three times his strength gave out; the fourth time his heart wished

178to bend and snap the ash spear of Aeacus' grandson —

179but before that Achilles closed, and took his life with the sword.

180He struck him in the belly beside the navel, and all

181his bowels gushed out on the ground; and darkness covered his eyes

182as he gasped. And Achilles, springing on his chest,

183stripped his armor, and spoke his word of triumph:

184"Lie there. It is a hard thing to strive with the children10

185of Cronos's mighty son — even for one born of a river.

186You said your birth was from a wide-flowing river;

187I claim the lineage of great Zeus.

188The man who fathered me is lord of many Myrmidons —

189Peleus, Aeacus' son; and Aeacus was from Zeus.

190As Zeus is stronger than the rivers that run murmuring to the sea,

191so the line of Zeus is made stronger than a river's.

192Why — here is a great river at your side, if he can help

193at all. But there is no fighting Zeus, Cronos's son:

194not even Achelous the king is his equal,

195not even the great strength of deep-flowing Ocean,

196from whom all rivers flow and every sea,

197and all the springs, and the deep wells:

198even he fears the thunderbolt of great Zeus

199and the terrible thunder, when it crashes out of heaven."

200He spoke, and pulled the bronze spear out of the bluff,11

201and left the man there, where he had taken his dear life,

202lying in the sands; and the dark water lapped him.

203Around him the eels and the fish were busy,

204tearing at the fat of his kidneys, plucking it away.

205And Achilles went on after the chariot-fighting Paeonians,

206who were still running in terror along the eddying river,

207since they had seen their best man in the strong encounter

208broken by force under the hands and sword of the son of Peleus.

209There he took Thersilochus and Mydon and Astypylus

210and Mnesus and Thrasius and Aenius and Ophelestes —

211and now swift Achilles would have killed still more Paeonians,

212had not the deep-eddying river called to him in anger,12

213likened to a man, and sent his voice out of a deep eddy:

214"O Achilles — beyond men you are strong, and beyond men you do

215outrage — and always the gods themselves are your defenders.

216If the son of Cronos has granted you to destroy all the Trojans,

217at least drive them out of me and do your grim work on the plain:

218my lovely streams are crammed full of corpses,

219and I can no longer pour my current through to the bright sea,

220choked with the dead — and you kill, and kill.

221Come, let it be. Horror takes me, leader of the people."

222Then in answer spoke to him swift-footed Achilles:

223"It shall be so, Scamander, nurtured of Zeus, as you command.

224But the arrogant Trojans — I will not stop cutting them down

225before I pen them in their city, and stand against Hector

226face to face, whether he breaks me or I break him."

227So speaking he swept on the Trojans like more than man.

240And terrible around Achilles the churning water towered,

241and the current beat down on his shield and shoved him; nor could

242his feet find purchase. He grasped an elm with both hands —

243full-grown, majestic — and it tore out by the roots,

244brought the whole overhang down with it, dammed the beautiful

245streams with its dense branches, and bridged the river itself,

246falling in whole. And he sprang up out of the eddy

247and set off flying across the plain on his quick feet —

248terrified. And the great god did not let up, but rose behind him,

250Achilles, and beat destruction away from the Trojans.

251And the son of Peleus bounded away a spear-cast's length,

252with the swoop of the black eagle, the hunter,

253strongest of winged things at once, and swiftest:

255clashed terribly; and swerving out from under it

257As when a ditch-tending man leads a stream of water14

258from a dark spring down through his plantings and gardens,

259mattock in hand, knocking the clogs from the channel,

264fast as he was: gods are stronger than men.

265And as often as swift-footed brilliant Achilles turned

268so often the great wave of the sky-fed river

269battered down on his shoulders; and he sprang high with his feet,

270heartsick; and the river was breaking his knees from under him,

271raging slantwise beneath, eating the dust from under his feet.

272And the son of Peleus cried aloud, looking into the wide heaven:15

273"Father Zeus! — that no god undertakes to save me,

274pitiful as I am, from the river! Afterward let anything happen.

275No other of the sons of Heaven is so much to blame

276as my own mother, who beguiled me with lies:

277she said I would die under the wall of the armored Trojans

278by the swift arrows of Apollo.

279I wish Hector had killed me — the best man reared here:

280then a good man would have done the killing, and stripped a good man.

281Now it is my portion to be caught by a wretched death,

282hemmed in a great river, like a swineherd's boy

283swept away by a gully he was crossing in a storm."

284So he spoke; and very quickly Poseidon and Athena

285came and stood beside him, likened to men in body,

286and taking his hand in their hands they pledged him with words.

288"Son of Peleus — do not tremble so hard, do not be afraid.

289Such are we two of the gods who stand your helpers —

290Zeus approving — I and Pallas Athena.

291It is not your portion to be broken by a river:

292this one will soon give over — you will see it yourself.

293And we will give you close counsel, if you will heed it:

294do not rest your hands from the leveling war

295before you pen the Trojan army inside the famed walls

296of Ilion — whoever escapes. And when you have taken Hector's life,

297come back to the ships. We give you the glory to carry off."

300into the plain. And the whole of it was filled with flood-water,

304hold him now, wide as it flowed: Athena put great strength in him.

306his rage at Peleus's son grew; and he crested the wave of his current,

308"Dear brother — the strength of this man — let the two of us

309hold it together, since very soon he will sack the great city

310of lord Priam; and the Trojans will not stand in the chaos.

311Help me, and quickly: fill your streams with water

312from the springs, and rouse up every gully;

313raise a great wave, and stir a huge clamor

314of timbers and stones — so we stop the wild man

315who is winning now, and straining like the gods themselves.

316For I say his strength will help him nothing, nor his beauty,

320past counting; and the Achaeans will not know where to gather

321his bones, so deep in silt shall I bury him.

401the terrible aegis that not even Zeus's thunderbolt breaks:

402there blood-stained Ares stabbed her with his long spear.

403But she, giving ground, caught up a stone in her thick hand —

404a stone that lay on the plain, black, jagged, and huge,

405which men of former times had set to be a field's boundary-marker.

406With it she hit rushing Ares in the neck, and unstrung his limbs.

407Seven acres he covered, falling; and his hair was fouled with dust,

408and his armor clattered around him. And Pallas Athena laughed,

409and triumphing over him she spoke winged words:

410"Baby fool — you have never yet reckoned how much stronger

411I claim to be, that you match your fury against mine.

412So pay in full your mother's curses — the Erinyes

413she wishes on you in her anger, because you deserted

414the Achaeans and defend the arrogant Trojans."

415So speaking, she turned her shining eyes away.

416And Aphrodite, daughter of Zeus, took Ares by the hand22

417and led him off, groaning thick and fast, barely gathering his spirit.

418But when the goddess white-armed Hera noticed her,

419at once she spoke winged words to Athena:

420"Shame! Unwearied one, child of aegis-bearing Zeus —

421there goes the dog-fly again, leading Ares, bane of mortals,

422out of the deadly war through the chaos. After her!"

423So she spoke; and Athena sped after, glad at heart,

424and rushing on Aphrodite she struck her on the breast

425with her thick hand; and there her knees went slack, and her own heart.

426So the two of them lay on the earth that feeds many,

427and Athena, triumphing, spoke winged words:

428"Such may they all be now — all the helpers of the Trojans

429when they fight the armored Argives:

430just so bold, and just so hardy, as Aphrodite

431coming to second Ares, and facing my fury.

432Then long ago we should have rested from the war,

433with the well-built citadel of Ilion sacked."

434So she spoke; and the goddess white-armed Hera smiled.

435And the lord earth-shaker addressed Apollo:23

436"Phoebus — why do we two stand apart? It is not fitting,

437when the others have begun. The greater shame if we go back

438fightless to Olympus, to the bronze-floored house of Zeus.

439Begin: you are the younger born. It would not be right

440for me, since I was born first and know more things.

441Baby fool — how mindless a heart you have! Do you not even

442remember all we two suffered around Ilion,

443alone of the gods, when we came from Zeus and drudged

444a whole year for haughty Laomedon at a stated wage,

445and he gave the orders, and we served?

446I built the Trojans their wall around the city —

447wide, and very beautiful, so the city might be unbreakable —

448while you, Phoebus, herded the shambling twist-horned cattle

449in the spurs of wooded, many-folded Ida.

450But when the glad seasons brought around the term

451of our hire, then he robbed us of all our wage by force —

452terrifying Laomedon — and sent us off with threats.

453He threatened to bind our feet, and our hands above them,

454and ship us for sale to far-off islands;

455and he vowed to lop the ears from both of us with bronze.

456So back we went, the two of us, with rancor in our hearts,

457in anger for the wage he promised and did not pay.

458And it is his people you now favor — instead of trying,

459with us, to make the arrogant Trojans perish

460root and branch, evilly, with their children and honored wives!"

461Then in turn the lord Apollo, the worker-from-afar, answered him:24

462"Earth-shaker — you would say I was unsound of mind

463if I fought against you for the sake of mortals —

464pitiful things, who like the leaves at one time

465flourish in their blaze, eating the fruit of the plowland,

466and at another wither heartless away. No — quickly,

467let us rest from battle. Let them do their own fighting."

468So speaking he turned away: for he shrank in awe

469from trading blows with his father's brother.

470But his sister rebuked him bitterly — the lady of wild beasts,25

471Artemis of the wilds — and flung the word of insult:

472"So you run, worker-from-afar! — and hand Poseidon the whole

473victory, and give him glory for nothing!

474Baby fool — why do you carry that bow, useless as wind?

475Never again let me hear you in our father's halls

476boasting, as before among the immortal gods,

477that you would fight Poseidon face to face."

478So she spoke; and Apollo the worker-from-afar answered nothing;

479but the honored wife of Zeus, in anger,

480reviled the shooter of arrows with words of insult:

481"And how is it you are eager, shameless bitch, to stand

482against me? I am a hard match for your fury,

483bow-carrier though you are — you whom Zeus made a lion

484to women, and gave you leave to kill whichever you choose.

485Better, surely, to slaughter beasts on the mountains,

486and wild deer, than to fight by force with your betters.

487But if you want a lesson in war — so you may know well

488how much stronger I am, when you match your fury against mine —"

489She spoke, and with her left hand caught both the other's hands

490at the wrist, and with her right stripped the bow from her shoulders,

491and with the bow itself, smiling, she boxed her about the ears

492as she twisted this way and that; and the swift arrows spilled out.

493And the goddess fled from under her, weeping, like a dove

494that flies from a hawk into a hollow rock,

495a cleft — for it was not her portion to be caught:

496so she fled weeping, and left her bow and arrows where they lay.

497And to Leto spoke the courier, the slayer of Argus:26

498"Leto, I will not fight you — no: it is a hard thing

499to trade blows with the wives of cloud-gatherer Zeus.

500With all my heart's goodwill: boast among the immortal gods

501that you overcame me with your strong force."

502So he spoke; and Leto gathered up the curved bow

503and the arrows, fallen this way and that in the swirl of dust,

504and taking her daughter's bow and arrows she went back.

505And the girl came to Olympus, to the bronze-floored house of Zeus,27

506and sat down weeping on her father's knees,

507the ambrosial robe trembling around her; and her father

508the son of Cronos drew her to him, and asked, laughing sweetly:

509"Who of the sons of Heaven, dear child, has treated you so —

510for nothing — as if you had been caught doing wrong in the open?"

511And the fair-crowned goddess of the clamorous hunt answered him:

512"Your wife battered me, father — white-armed Hera,

513from whom strife and quarrel cling to the immortals."

514So the two of them spoke such things to one another.28

515And Phoebus Apollo went down into holy Ilion:

516for the wall of the well-built city was on his mind —

517lest the Danaans sack it that day, beyond its fate.

518The other everlasting gods went to Olympus,

519some in anger, and some in high triumph,

520and sat down beside the father of the black cloud. But Achilles

521went on killing the Trojans — the men and their single-hoofed horses.

522As when smoke rises and reaches the wide heaven

523from a burning city — the wrath of the gods has sent it up,

524and it lays labor on all, and grief on many:

525so Achilles laid labor and grief on the Trojans.

526And old Priam stood on the god-built tower,

527and made out gigantic Achilles: and there before him

528the Trojans were being driven pell-mell in rout, and no defense

529was left. He cried out, and came down from the tower,

530to rouse the famed gate-keepers along the wall:

531"Hold the gates spread open in your hands, until the army

532comes into the city in its rout: for here is Achilles

533close by, driving them. Now, I think, comes disaster.

534But the moment they are gathered inside the wall and breathing,

535close the tight-fitted double doors again:

536I dread that ruinous man — that he may leap inside the wall."

537So he spoke; and they loosed the gates and thrust back the bars,

538and the gates, spread wide, made a light of safety. And Apollo

539sprang out to meet the rout, to beat destruction from the Trojans.

540And they, straight for the city and the high wall,

541parched with thirst and caked with dust, came fleeing

542out of the plain; and he pressed hard behind them with the spear:

543raging madness held his heart, and he strained for glory.

544Then the sons of the Achaeans would have taken high-gated Troy,29

545had not Phoebus Apollo stirred up brilliant Agenor —

546Antenor's son, a man blameless and strong.

547Into his heart he threw courage, and himself stood

548beside him, to beat away the heavy hands of death,

549leaning on the oak tree, wrapped in thick mist.

550Now when Agenor caught sight of Achilles, sacker of cities,

551he stood fast; but the heart in him churned dark as he waited,

552and troubled, he spoke to his own great-hearted spirit:

553"Ah me! If I run from strong Achilles

554the way the rest are driven, panicked, in rout —

555he will catch me all the same, and butcher me for the coward I am.

556But if I leave these men to be driven before him —

557before Achilles, Peleus's son — and run on my own feet

558another way, off from the wall, toward the Ileian plain,

559until I reach the spurs of Ida and get into the brush —

560then at evening, when I have bathed in the river

561and cooled the sweat from me, I could come back to Ilion...

562But why does my own heart argue these things with me?

563What if he sees me swinging wide of the city toward the plain,

564and comes after at a sprint, and runs me down on his quick feet?

565Then there will be no more escaping death and the death-spirits:

566he is too strong — strong beyond all mankind.

567But what if, in front of the city, I stand and face him?

568His flesh too, surely, can be cut by the sharp bronze;

569in him is one life, no more; and mortal, men say

570he is — though Zeus, Cronos's son, keeps handing him the glory."

571So speaking, braced and gathered, he waited for Achilles,30

572and the brave heart in him strained toward war and battle.

573As a leopard comes out of deep brush

574to face the huntsman, and her heart neither

575fears nor thinks of flight when she hears the baying —

576for even if he is quicker, and stabs or hits her with a cast,

577still, run through on the very spear, she does not leave off

578her valor, until she closes with him or goes down:

579so the son of proud Antenor, brilliant Agenor,

580refused to run before he had put Achilles to the test.

581He held the balanced round shield out before him,

582aimed at him with his spear, and shouted loud:

583"No doubt you hoped in your heart, shining Achilles,

584to sack the city of the proud Trojans this very day.

585Baby fool — there is grief yet to be suffered over her:

586inside her we are many, and men of valor,

587who will guard Ilion, standing in front of our dear parents

588and wives and sons. It is you who will meet your doom here —

589terrifying as you are, and so daring a fighter."

590He spoke, and let the sharp javelin fly from his heavy hand,

591and hit him on the shin below the knee — no miss:

592and around it the greave of new-forged tin

593screamed terribly; and the bronze sprang back

594from where it struck, and did not pass: the god's gifts held it.

595And the son of Peleus went for godlike Agenor

596in his turn — but Apollo would not let him win the glory:

597he snatched Agenor away, and hid him in thick mist,

598and sent him quietly out of the war, to go his way.

599Then by a trick he kept the son of Peleus from the army:31

600likened in every point to Agenor himself, the worker-from-afar

601stood before his feet — and Achilles ran hot after him.

602And while he chased him across the wheat-bearing plain,

603turning him along the deep-eddying river Scamander

604as he ran just a little ahead — for by that cunning Apollo

605kept him beguiled, always hoping to catch him on his quick feet —

606meanwhile the rest of the routed Trojans, in a mass,

607came glad to the city, and the town was filled with their crowding.

608Nor did they dare wait for one another any longer

609outside the city and the wall, to learn who had escaped

610and who had died in the fighting: in they poured, headlong,

611every man whose feet and knees could save him.

Notes

1 lines 1–16 — The opening repeats 14.433-434 exactly (our English kept): "the ford of the fair-flowing river, eddying Xanthus, whom immortal Zeus begot" — the crossing where the stunned Hector was laid and revived in Book 14. The river of Trojan refuge becomes the killing-pen: Achilles splits the rout, Hera walls one half behind mist, and the other half goes into the water. The locust simile knows what is coming three hundred lines early: men driven into a river by fire is precisely how the book will end for the river himself. ↩

2 lines 17–33 — The spear left leaning on the tamarisks: sword work — butcher's work — is what the moment wants. The dolphin simile makes the water itself predatory ground. Then the poem's coldest logistics: twelve young men taken alive "blood-price for the death of Patroclus" — the vow of 18.336-337 collected on schedule, the victims led out "dazed, like fawns," bound with the belts of their own tunics. They will be paid out at 23.175-176. Between vow and payment the poem never lets them speak. ↩

3 lines 34–48 — Lycaon's dossier — the fullest biography any minor victim receives, given precisely so we know the whole weight of what is about to be refused: the night raid, the fig-shoots cut for chariot-rails (a boy doing farm-work in an orchard war), the sale on Lemnos to Euneus son of Jason (the fleet's wine-merchant, 7.467-469), the ransom by Eëtion of Imbros, Arisbe, the escape home. "Eleven days he delighted his heart among his dear ones; and on the twelfth the god threw him once more into the hands of Achilles." The symmetry is exact and awful: eleven days of resurrection, and then the machinery resumes. ↩

4 lines 49–63 — Achilles' soliloquy over the unarmed man plays the return as ghost-story: "the great-hearted Trojans I have killed will rise again out of the murky dark." The joke — spear as experiment: "so I may see... whether he will come back equally from down there" — is the aristeia's humor: death-dealing grown so routine it wants variety. Under it, the poem's physics: the sea did not hold him; "the life-giving earth... holds down even the strong" will. ↩

5 lines 64–96 — The supplication tableau: one hand on the knees, one gripping the spear itself — clinging to the weapon to keep it out of his own body. Lycaon's claims are, in order: the suppliant-bond; table-fellowship ("at your table first I ate the grain of Demeter" — the captive fed by his captor becomes his guest); the ransom record (a hundred oxen paid, three hundred to free him); the fate-arithmetic of the twelfth dawn; and last, the desperate genealogical wedge — born of Laothoe, not Hecuba: "I am not from the same womb as Hector." He offers every category the heroic world honors. The answer voids the categories. ↩

6 lines 97–113 — The refusal and the consolation, the poem's ethical summit. "Fool — offer me no ransom": the economy that ran Books 1-9 (and Lycaon's own first capture — "before Patroclus met his day of destiny, sparing Trojans was dearer to my heart") is formally closed. Then the turn from wrath to metaphysics — "So, friend, you die too" (ἀλλὰ φίλος θάνε καὶ σύ) — and the terms of the consolation: Patroclus was better, and died; "do you not see what I am — how beautiful, how big?" — and I die too: "a dawn, or an evening, or a midday." The killer comforts his victim with their equality before death, and means it; φίλος is not sarcasm but the only fellowship left to a man who has accepted his own sentence. No passage of the poem was more quoted in antiquity as the summary of the human condition. ↩

7 lines 114–135 — Lycaon's assent: knees slack, spear released, both hands spread — a posture between surrender and embrace — and the sword at the collarbone. Then the anti-funeral: slung into the river with a taunt that negates each rite in turn — no mother's laying-out, no lament; instead fish licking the wound "uncaring" and darting up "under the black ripple to eat the white fat of Lycaon" (the body already imagined as carcass-meat). The closing insult is cultic: the river you honored "so long with slaughtered bulls, drowning single-hoofed horses alive in his eddies" (live-horse sacrifice to rivers is real archaic practice) will not save you. Aristophanes athetized 130-135 — and Didymus suspected Aristarchus agreed — but the lines are the hinge: this speech is what the river cannot forgive, and the next six hundred lines grow out of it. ↩

8 lines 136–160 — Asteropaeus: grandson of the river Axius, eleven days at Troy (to Lycaon's twelve — the book counts its doomed newcomers), with Xanthus himself putting fury in his chest: the river's first counter-move is a champion of river-blood. The challenge-exchange is a diptych with Book 20's: where Aeneas gave sixteen generations, the Paeonian gives a river and a spear — "Axius, who sends the most beautiful water over the earth." ↩

9 lines 161–183 — The double cast — "for he was a two-handed fighter" (περιδέξιος): one spear into the shield's gold, the other grazing Achilles' right elbow — the only blood Achilles sheds in the entire poem, and a river's grandson draws it. The duel then turns on the ash spear stuck in the bluff: three heaves, and on the fourth attempt — trying to snap the spear of Aeacus' grandson — the sword takes him. He dies at the belly like Polydorus, bowels on the ground, "gasping"; the register's darkness-formula closes his eyes. ↩

10 lines 184–199 — The victory-boast is a theology lesson: "It is a hard thing to strive with the children of Cronos's mighty son — even for the son of a river." Zeus's line beats water's line; and the ladder runs up: "not even Achelous the king is his equal, not even the great strength of deep-flowing Ocean, from whom all rivers flow and every sea, and all the springs, and the deep wells." Zenodotus and Megaclides omitted line 195 (Pausanias saw such texts), which makes Achelous — not Ocean — the greatest of waters: the two readings preserve the oldest Greek dispute about the primal water. The vulgate keeps Ocean as source of all things and Achelous as king of rivers; this translation keeps the vulgate. ↩

11 lines 200–210 — The corpse in the shallows: "around him the eels and the fish were busy, tearing at the fat of his kidneys" — Lycaon's threatened fate enacted on the next body within eighty lines. Then seven Paeonians in a line — the plain catalogue-deaths that mark an aristeia running at full mechanical speed — until the river speaks. ↩

12 lines 211–232 — The river's protest is the book's ecological center: "my lovely streams are crammed full of corpses, and I can no longer pour my current through to the bright sea... and you kill, and kill." The god of the landscape pleads not for Troy but for his own nature — a river's function is to flow, and slaughter has clogged it. Achilles' answer grants the venue-change and nothing else, and states the poem's remaining program in one line: "stand against Hector face to face, whether he breaks me or I break him." The river then rebukes Apollo with Zeus's sunset commission — the day-clause that has governed the poem since 11.194 — as if to say: someone must hold the line till dark, and the archer is idle. ↩

13 lines 233–271 — The attack. The river fights as water fights: not blows but volume — the surge that shoves corpses ashore ("bellowing like a bull") while hiding the living in his eddies (the river as shepherd of his people, doing with water what Apollo does with mist); the wave on the shield; the uprooted elm that takes the whole bank with it and bridges the stream. Achilles — for the first time since Book 1 — runs. The eagle simile keeps his dignity ("strongest of winged things at once, and swiftest"); the irrigation simile takes it away magnificently: the cosmic terror measured by a gardener whose channel outruns him, mattock in hand — the most domestic image in the Iliad set against its most elemental combat, closed by the flattest of maxims: "gods are stronger than men." ↩

14 lines 272–283 — The despair-prayer. Achilles wails (ᾤμωξεν) — the verb of the wounded — that no god will save him, and that "my own mother beguiled me with lies": the promised death "under the wall... by the swift arrows of Apollo" (exactly the death he will in fact receive, 22.359-360 — the complaint certifies the prophecy). The horror is genre-specific: to drown is to die without a victor, without a grave-mound, without kleos — "like a swineherd's boy swept away by a gully he was crossing in a storm." The greatest of heroes measures his death against the smallest imaginable one; Odysseus, drowning, makes the same calculation (Od. 5.306-312). ↩

15 lines 284–297 — The rescue is pure dramaturgy: Poseidon and Athena "likened to men," taking his hand — the pledge- gesture — and dictating the remainder of the epic: not fated to die by water; pen the Trojans; kill Hector; return to the ships; "we give you the glory to carry off." (Seleucus athetized 290-292, and the Cretan edition omitted them.) The plot of Books 21-22 is handed over in a handclasp. ↩

16 lines 298–327 — Scamander summons Simoïs — "dear brother" — for the war of the waters: springs opened, gullies roused, "a huge clamor of timbers and stones." The threat-speech is the book's dark masterpiece: armor "deep beneath the mere... wrapped in slime," the man rolled in sand under "shingle past counting," and the perfect inversion of epic burial: "the Achaeans will not know where to gather his bones... There his burial-mound will stand ready-made: he will need no heaping of a tomb." Book 23 will build Patroclus (and eventually Achilles) the greatest funeral in poetry; the river offers its exact negation — burial as disappearance, the tomb no one can find. For a hero, that is not death but erasure. ↩

17 lines 328–341 — Hera's counter-stroke calls the match-card of 20.73-74 by name: "it was against you we always reckoned eddying Xanthus matched in battle." Her plan is a coordinated burn: Hephaestus on the trees and the river, herself rousing Zephyrus and the white South Wind from the sea to drive the flame onto the Trojans. Her warning — "do not let him turn you back at all with soft words or with threats" — repeats Apollo's coaching of Aeneas (20.108-109): even gods need to be told not to listen. ↩

18 lines 342–355 — Fire eats the book's own scenery. First the corpses — the dead of Achilles' killing get from Hephaestus the cremation the river denied them: the aristeia's litter burned off the plain. The drying simile answers the irrigation simile: there, water outran its gardener; here, the North Wind dries the new-watered orchard "and the man who tends it is glad." Then the riverside flora burns by name — elms, willows, tamarisks (where the spear leaned at line 18), lotus, rushes, galingale — and the eels and fish that ate Asteropaeus tumble in the boil. The book's justice is elemental and complete. ↩

19 lines 356–382 — The surrender: "no one of the gods can stand against you... What are strife and rescue to me?" — the god of the landscape resigning the war effort mid-sentence. The cauldron simile is the poem at its most audaciously domestic: a god's agony rendered as kitchen-work, "melting down the lard of a fatted hog... with dry split wood beneath." And the oath he offers Hera is her own: 374-376 repeat, word for word, the Troy-burning clause she recited at 20.315-317 (renderings kept identical) — the river buys his life by swearing the destruction of the city he rose to save. Hera's stand-down order states the Theomachy's official rule — "not seemly to batter an immortal god for the sake of mortals" — which every god then proceeds to break. ↩

20 lines 383–390 — The gods fall on each other, "the wide earth rang, and the great heaven trumpeted" — and the audience is named: "Zeus heard, sitting on Olympus; and his own heart laughed for joy, when he saw the gods coming together in strife." This is the show he commissioned at 20.23 ("where I can watch and delight my mind"), now delivered. The gods cannot die; therefore their war is comedy, and the poem plays it as such — the laughter of Zeus is the genre-marker for the next hundred lines, while beneath it the mortal war goes on being fatal. ↩

21 lines 391–415 — Ares vs Athena, the grudge-match of Book 5: his "dog-fly" and the memory of Diomedes' guided spear; her weapon a boundary-stone "which men of former times had set to be a field's boundary-marker" — the goddess of civic order felling the war-god with the marker of agreed limits (what Ares tramples, in every sense). Seven acres of fallen god, dusty hair, a laugh, and the taunt: pay "your mother's curses — the Erinyes she wishes on you" for changing sides. The register's formulas — "blood-stained Ares," "unstrung his limbs" — do their fixed work. ↩

22 lines 416–434 — Aphrodite leading Ares off by the hand is Book 5 again (she led him wounded then too), and Hera's "there goes the dog-fly again" hands Athena her second knockdown: one blow to the breast, and the pair lie together "on the earth that feeds many." Athena's boast — had all Troy's helpers been so brave, "long ago we should have rested from the war, with the well-built citadel of Ilion sacked" — is the counterfactual that exposes the whole Theomachy: divine partisanship is what keeps the war long. Hera smiles; the smile is the scene's verdict. ↩

23 lines 435–460 — The one serious challenge: Poseidon to Apollo, elder to younger ("Begin: you are the younger born" — dueling etiquette among immortals), grounded in the poem's deepest Trojan backstory: the year of bonded labor under Laomedon, Poseidon building the wall "wide, and very beautiful, so the city might be unbreakable" (the very fortification this war beats against), Apollo herding cattle on Ida; the wage refused, the threats — binding, sale to far islands, ears lopped with bronze. Troy's royal house cheated two gods and threatened them with mutilation and slavery: the city's doom has a contract-law history, and Poseidon's indignation — "it is his people you now favor!" — is the poem's last rehearsal of it. ↩

24 lines 461–469 — Apollo's refusal is the great verdict on mankind from above: fighting a fellow-god "for the sake of mortals — pitiful things, who like the leaves at one time flourish in their blaze, eating the fruit of the plowland, and at another wither heartless away." This is Glaucus's leaf-simile (6.146-149) — there, a man's hard-won consolation ("as the generation of leaves, so the generations of men"); here, a god's reason for indifference. The same image carries humility from below and contempt from above: no two lines in the poem measure the distance between gods and men more exactly. He turns away out of respect — for his uncle, not for us. (471 and 475-477 were athetized by Aristarchus.) ↩

25 lines 470–496 — Artemis pays for her brother's prudence: her taunt ("why do you carry that bow, useless as wind?") draws Hera — and the queen of heaven disciplines the huntress like an unruly girl: wrists caught in one hand, the bow stripped and used to box her ears, "smiling" as she does it, the arrows spilling. Hera's speech first defines her — "Zeus made you a lion to women, and gave you leave to kill whichever you choose" (Artemis as the sender of women's sudden deaths) — then demotes her: "better to slaughter beasts on the mountains than to fight by force with your betters." The dove-into-the-rock-cleft simile turns the archeress into her own quarry; the bow lies where it fell. ↩

26 lines 497–504 — Hermes to Leto — the Theomachy's shortest match and its wittiest: an immediate, courteous forfeit ("it is a hard thing to trade blows with the wives of cloud-gatherer Zeus") with the winnings handed over in advance: "boast among the immortal gods that you overcame me with your strong force." Leto, unbloodied, gathers her daughter's scattered arrows — the one maternal gesture in a battle of gods. ↩

27 lines 505–513 — The end of the divine war: Artemis in Zeus's lap, the ambrosial robe trembling, and the father "laughing sweetly" as he asks who hurt her "as if you had been caught doing wrong in the open." Her answer indicts the household: "Your wife battered me... from whom strife and quarrel cling to the immortals." The Theomachy that began with a cosmic quake ends as a family scene in the living room — which is the poem's point: for the deathless, war can only ever be domestic comedy. Below, meanwhile, it is Books 22-24. ↩

28 lines 514–543 — Apollo alone stays at his post, entering Troy "lest the Danaans sack it that day, beyond its fate" — the god now guarding the same limit Zeus feared for at 20.30. The burning-city simile makes Achilles into the thing he is about to cause ("the wrath of the gods has sent it up" — he is that wrath); and Priam appears in his end-game role for the first time: the old man on the tower, ordering the gates held open in men's hands until the rout is in, then barred — "I dread that ruinous man — that he may leap inside the wall." The gates "spread wide, made a light of safety" (φάος — the register's word for rescue): the same doors that will stand between Hector and his life in Book 22. ↩

29 lines 544–570 — Agenor — one man, stiffened by Apollo, between Achilles and the open gates. His soliloquy in the registered frame ("troubled, he spoke to his own great-hearted spirit") is the infantryman's complete decision-tree: flight with the crowd (caught and butchered "for the coward I am"); solo flight to Ida's brush — with the poem's most humane detail, the fugitive's daydream of an evening bath in the river, "cooled the sweat from me" — rejected on pure footspeed-arithmetic; then the discovery on which the rest of the poem stands: "His flesh too, surely, can be cut by the sharp bronze; in him is one life, no more; and mortal, men say he is." Every man who chooses to stand against Achilles must first say this catechism; Hector will say it, and its refutations, at 22.99-130. ↩

30 lines 571–598 — The leopard simile — she comes out of deep brush at the huntsman and "run through on the very spear, she does not leave off her valor, until she closes with him or goes down": doomed courage as the poem's highest animal image, spent here on a secondary hero to set the standard Hector will be measured against. Agenor's cast hits the shin — "the greave of new-forged tin screamed terribly" — and Hephaestus's armor turns its first blow in combat exactly where Hephaestus promised marvel rather than protection (18.466-467): it gives both. Apollo snatches Agenor into mist before the counterstroke — the third man so saved from Achilles in two books. ↩

31 lines 599–611 — The decoy: Apollo "likened in every point to Agenor" runs, and the fastest man alive chases a god across the wheat-bearing plain, "always hoping to catch him on his quick feet" — Achilles' one weapon against Hector, his speed, played against him to bleed the pursuit of its object. The trade is a city's remnant for one man's glory: while he chases nothing, Troy pours inside, "glad" — and the book's last lines are the rout's arithmetic, without a single name: no one dared wait "to learn who had escaped and who had died in the fighting: in they poured, headlong, every man whose feet and knees could save him." One man is not inside. Book 22 opens with Hector standing in front of the gates. ↩
