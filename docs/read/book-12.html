Book XII

The battle at the wall: the Trojans storm it on foot, Sarpedon states the heroic contract, Hector overrules the omen, and the gate goes down.

Translator’s note on Book 12

The shortest book of the Iliad so far translated is also its most tightly built: a single military action — the storming of the Achaean wall — organized as a sequence of thresholds: ditch, gates, breastwork, bar; and at each threshold, a debate. Polydamas counsels leaving the chariots, and Hector accepts; Asius refuses, and the narrator files his death-notice in advance (note 7); Polydamas reads the eagle-and-snake omen and counsels retreat, and Hector answers with the most quoted line of the poem — "One bird-sign is best: to fight for your fatherland" (243) — an answer that is magnificent, politically immortal, and wrong (note 12). The book's crown is Sarpedon's speech to Glaucus (310-328), the classic statement of why kings fight — honor received obliges honor paid, and since death comes anyway, the only choice left is the manner — the exact contract whose repudiation by Achilles in Book 9 the poem has already staged (note 19).

The opening is unlike anything else in Homer: the narrator steps out of the story's time to tell how, after the war, Poseidon and Apollo turned eight rivers against the wall and erased it from the beach (3-35) — epic covering its own tracks, since no trace of the wall survived to be seen; the passage contains the poem's only "race of half-god men," the heroes seen for once from the far side of the gulf that separates them from the audience (note 2). Equally unusual is the narrator's cry of overload at 176 — "hard for me to tell it all through, as though I were a god" — which ancient critics athetized, perhaps for its very humanity (note 10).

Throughout, the poet measures the widest war by the smallest peace: stones fall like snow muffling the works of men (note 16); the deadlock is two farmers with measuring-rods disputing a boundary (note 27); the balance of battle is an honest wool-worker's scale, held level "to win a pitiful wage for her children" (note 28). Then Hector lifts the stone two men of today could not shift, breaks the double gate, and leaps through "in his face the very likeness of swift night" — and the book ends inside the breach, the Danaans in rout among their ships, nothing resolved except the door.

3Argives and Trojans in their masses; nor was the ditch

4to hold much longer, nor the wide wall above it

10So long as Hector was alive, and Achilles in his wrath,

11and the city of lord Priam unsacked,

13But when all the best of the Trojans had died,

14and of the Argives many were broken, though some were left,

15and Priam's city was sacked in the tenth year,

16and the Argives had gone in their ships to their dear fatherland,

17then at last Poseidon and Apollo took counsel

18to wash the wall away, turning against it the fury of rivers,

20Rhesus and Heptaporus, Caresus and Rhodius,

21Granicus and Aesepus, and brilliant Scamander,

23had fallen in the dust, and the race of half-god men:

24the mouths of all these Phoebus Apollo turned together,

26without ceasing, to drown the wall in sea the sooner.

27And the earth-shaker himself, trident in hand,

28led them, and sent out on the waves all the foundations

29of beams and stones the Achaeans had laid with labor,

30and made all smooth along the strong-flowing Hellespont,

31and covered the great beach over again with sand,

32the wall washed away; and he turned the rivers back to run

33in their channels, where before they had poured their fair streams.2

35hereafter. But now battle and clamor blazed

36around the well-built wall, and the beams of the towers rang

37under the blows. The Argives, broken under the whip of Zeus,

38were penned by the hollow ships and held there,

39dreading Hector, the strong machine of rout;

40while he, as before, fought on like a whirlwind.

41As when among hounds and hunting men

42a boar or lion wheels, glorying in his strength,

43and they form themselves into a tower-line

44and stand to face him, hurling from their hands

45spearheads thick and fast; but his glorious heart

46knows no fear and no flight — and his courage kills him —3

47and again and again he wheels, testing the ranks of men,

48and wherever he charges, there the ranks of men give way:

49so Hector went through the crowd and begged his companions,

50urging them to cross the ditch. But his swift-footed horses

51did not dare it; loudly they neighed, standing

52at the very lip, for the wide ditch frightened them off —

53not easy to leap from close up, nor to drive across,

54for overhanging banks stood all around it

55on both sides, and above it was fitted with pointed

56stakes, planted by the sons of the Achaeans,

57close-set and great, a defense against enemy men.

58A horse straining at a well-wheeled chariot could not easily

59get in; but the foot-soldiers were mad to try it.

60Then Polydamas came and stood by daring Hector:

61"Hector, and you other leaders of Trojans and allies,

62it is folly to drive our swift horses across the ditch.

63It is very hard to cross: sharp stakes

64stand in it, and right up against them is the Achaeans' wall.

65There is no way down there, no way to fight

66for men in chariots: the space is narrow, and I think we would be hurt.

67For if Zeus who thunders on high, with evil in mind,

68is wiping out the Achaeans utterly, eager to help the Trojans,

69then I too would wish it done this very instant —

70the Achaeans perishing here, nameless, far from Argos.

71But if they turn on us, and we are driven back

72from the ships, and get ourselves tangled in the dug ditch,

73then not even a messenger, I think, will make it back

74to the city, once the Achaeans have wheeled on us.

75Come then — as I say it, let us all obey:

76let the aides hold the horses at the ditch,

77while we ourselves, on foot, armed in our gear,

78follow Hector in one mass; and the Achaeans

79will not hold, if the cables of destruction are fastened on them."

80So spoke Polydamas, and the prudent word pleased Hector,4

81and at once he leapt with his weapons from the chariot to the ground.

108Then the other Trojans and their far-famed allies

116Before that, fate of the hateful name closed round him

117through the spear of Idomeneus, Deucalion's illustrious son.7

118For he steered left of the ships, where the Achaeans

119came back out of the plain with horses and chariots;

120there he drove his horses and car, and at the gates

121he found the doors not shut, the long bar not in place:

122men were holding them thrown open, hoping to save

123any companion in flight from the battle, and bring him to the ships.

124Straight for that point, deliberate, he held his horses, and his men

125followed, screaming sharply, certain the Achaeans

126would hold no longer, but fall upon the black ships —

127the fools: at the gates they found two men of the bravest,

128high-hearted sons of Lapith spearmen,

129one the son of Peirithous, strong Polypoetes,

130the other Leonteus, the equal of Ares bane of mortals.

131These two, in front of the high gates,

132stood like high-crowned oaks in the mountains

133that abide the wind and rain through all their days,

134fast-fixed by their great roots running deep —

135so the two of them, trusting their hands and their strength,

136abode the coming of great Asius, and did not run.

137But the Trojans, holding their dried-oxhide shields high,

138came straight for the well-built wall with a great war-shout

139around lord Asius, and Iamenus, and Orestes,

140and Asius's son Adamas, and Thoon, and Oenomaus.

141And for a while the two, staying inside, were urging

142the strong-greaved Achaeans to defend the ships;

143but when they saw the Trojans storming at the wall,

144and shouting and panic broke out among the Danaans,

145the two burst out and fought in front of the gates

146like a pair of wild boars in the mountains

147that stand the oncoming rabble of men and dogs,

148and charging slantwise shatter the timber around them,

149shearing it off at the root, and from under comes the noise

150of gnashing tusks, until a man's cast takes their life:

151so the shining bronze on their chests rang under the blows

152struck face to face; for they fought hard indeed,

153trusting the troops above them and their own strength.8

154For the men above hurled stones from the well-built towers,

155defending themselves, their huts,

156and the swift-faring ships. Like snowflakes the stones fell earthward,

157flakes a blustering wind, driving the shadowed clouds,

158pours thick and fast on the earth that feeds so many:

159so the missiles streamed from their hands, from Achaeans

160and Trojans alike; and helmets and bossed shields

161rang harsh and dry, pounded with millstone rocks.

162Then Asius, Hyrtacus' son, groaned aloud and struck

163both his thighs, and spoke in outrage:

164"Father Zeus, so even you, it turns out, are an utter

165lover of lies! I did not think the Achaean heroes

166would stand our fury and our invincible hands —

167and they, like wasps of the nimble waist, or bees

168that make their homes beside a rocky road

169and will not abandon their hollow house, but stay

170and fight off the raiding men for their children's sake,

171so these men will not give back from the gates, two though they are,

172until they kill or are taken."9

173So he spoke, but speaking did not persuade the mind of Zeus:

174his heart willed the glory for Hector.

175And others fought the fight at other gates —

176hard for me to tell it all through, as though I were a god:10

177for everywhere around the wall a prodigious fire had risen,

178a fire of stone; and the Argives, grieved to the heart,

179fought on for the ships by necessity; and the gods were downcast,

180all of them who were the Danaans' helpers in the fighting.

181And the Lapiths clashed into war and combat.

182There the son of Peirithous, strong Polypoetes,

183hit Damasus with the spear through the bronze-cheeked helmet:

184the bronze helmet did not hold; straight on through

185the bronze point broke the bone, and the brain

186was all spattered inside it. He broke him in mid-charge.

187And after him he stripped the life from Pylon and Ormenus.

188And Leonteus, scion of Ares, hit Antimachus's son

189Hippomachus with the spear, catching him at the war-belt;

190then drawing his sharp sword out of its sheath

191he sprang through the crowd and struck Antiphates first,

192close-in, hand to hand; he crashed on his back on the ground;

193and after him Menon, and Iamenus, and Orestes —

194all these, one on another, he brought down to the earth that feeds so many.

195While they were stripping the glittering armor from these,

196the young men who followed Polydamas and Hector,

197who were the most and the best, and were most determined

198to break the wall and set fire to the ships,

199still stood at the ditch's edge and were divided in mind:

200for a bird had come upon them as they strained to cross:

201an eagle, flying high, herding the army to the left,

202carrying in its talons a monstrous blood-red snake,

203alive, still writhing — and it had not forgotten its war-craft,

204for it struck the bird that held it, on the chest beside the neck,

205coiling itself backward; and the eagle flung it from him earthward,

206stung with the pain, and let it fall in the middle of the crowd,

207and with a scream flew off down the gusts of the wind.

208And the Trojans shuddered when they saw the writhing snake

209lying in their midst: a portent of aegis-bearing Zeus.11

210Then Polydamas came and stood by daring Hector:

211"Hector, always somehow you rebuke me in the assemblies

212though I counsel well — since it is never fitting, it seems,

213for a man of the people to speak against you, in council

214or ever in war, but always to swell your power.

215Yet now once more I will say what seems to me best.

216Let us not go forward to fight the Danaans for the ships.

217For this, I think, is how it will end — if truly

218this bird came for the Trojans as they strained to cross:

219an eagle, flying high, herding the army to the left,

220carrying in its talons a monstrous blood-red snake,

221alive — and it dropped it before it reached its own nest,

222and never finished carrying it home to give its young.

223So we: even if we break the gates and the wall

224of the Achaeans by main strength, and the Achaeans give way,

225we will come back from the ships in no good order by the same road;

226for we will leave many Trojans behind, whom the Achaeans

227will cut down with the bronze, defending their ships.

228So a reader of gods' signs would interpret, one whose heart

229truly knew the portents, and the people would believe him."

230Glaring at him darkly, Hector of the flashing helmet answered:

231"Polydamas, this talk of yours pleases me no longer;

232you know how to think another word, better than this one.

233But if you truly say this thing in earnest,

234then the gods themselves have destroyed your wits —

235you who tell me to forget the counsels of loud-thundering

236Zeus, which he himself promised me, and bowed his head;

237you who tell me to put my faith in birds

238with wings outstretched. I take no thought of them, no care

239whether they go to the right, toward the dawn and the sun,

240or to the left, toward the misty dark.

241Let us put our faith in the counsel of great Zeus,

242who is king over all mortals and immortals.

243One bird-sign is best: to fight for your fatherland.12

244Why are you so afraid of war and combat?

245For even if the rest of us are killed to a man

246beside the Argive ships, you need not fear to perish:

247your heart is not battle-staunch, not a fighter's heart.

248But if you hang back from the fighting, or talk any other man

249around with your words and turn him away from war,

250struck down at once under my spear you will lose your life."

251So speaking he led the way, and they followed him

252with a prodigious roar; and Zeus who delights in thunder

253roused out of the mountains of Ida a squall of wind

254that carried the dust straight at the ships; and it bewitched

255the mind of the Achaeans, and handed glory to the Trojans and Hector.13

256Trusting his portents, then, and their own strength,

257they set to trying to break the great wall of the Achaeans.

258They dragged at the towers' galleries, pulled down the breastworks,

259and levered up the jutting buttress-piles the Achaeans

260had set first in the earth to shore the towers:14

261these they tried to pull out, hoping to break the wall

262of the Achaeans. But the Danaans did not yet yield the path:

263they fenced the breastworks with oxhide shields

264and from behind them hurled at the enemy coming under the wall.

265And the two Ajaxes, marshaling on the towers,

266ranged everywhere, urging on the fury of the Achaeans;

267one man with honeyed words, another with hard ones

268they would lash, whomever they saw hanging wholly back from the fight:

269"Friends — you who are excellent among the Argives, you who are middling,

270you who are lesser — since men are in no way all equal

271in war — now there is work for every man of you,15

272and you know it yourselves. Let no one turn back

273toward the ships now he has heard the man who drives you;

274press forward instead, and call each other on,

275in hope that Olympian Zeus, lord of lightning, may grant us

276to beat back the assault and drive our enemies to their city."

277So the two, shouting ahead, stirred up the Achaeans' battle.

278And as flakes of snow fall thick and fast on a winter's day,

279when Zeus the counselor has bestirred himself to snow

280on mankind, and shows them what his missiles are,

281and lulling the winds he pours it down steadily, until he has covered

282the peaks of the high mountains, and the jutting headlands,

283and the clover meadows, and men's rich worked fields;

284and it is poured out over the harbors and shores of the grey sea,

285and only the wave, breaking on it, keeps it off; but all things else

286are wrapped from above, when the storm of Zeus drives down upon it:16

287so from both sides their stones flew thick and fast,

288some onto the Trojans, some from Trojans onto Achaeans,

289as they pelted each other; and over the whole wall the din rose.

290Yet not even then would the Trojans and glorious Hector

291have broken the gates of the wall and the long bar,

292had not Zeus the counselor roused his own son Sarpedon

293against the Argives, like a lion on twist-horned cattle.17

294At once he held before him his shield, balanced every way,

295fair, of hammered bronze, which the smith had hammered out,

296stitching inside it oxhides thick and close

297with golden rods running unbroken round the rim.18

298Holding this before him, shaking his two spears,

299he went his way like a mountain-bred lion who has long

300gone hungry for meat, and his proud spirit drives him

301to make trial of the flocks, to go even into the close-built fold;

302and even if he finds the herdsmen there beside them,

303watching over the sheep with dogs and spears,

304he will not be driven from the steading without his try:

305either he leaps in and seizes one, or himself

306is hit among the foremost by a javelin from a quick hand:

307so then his spirit drove godlike Sarpedon

308to rush the wall and break through the breastworks.

309And at once he spoke to Glaucus, Hippolochus's son:

310"Glaucus, why is it that we two are honored beyond all

311with the seat of honor, and the cuts of meat, and full cups,

312in Lycia, and all men look on us like gods,

313and we are granted a great estate on the banks of Xanthus,

314fine land, orchard and wheat-bearing plowland?

315For that, it is our duty now to stand among the first

316of the Lycians, and to meet the blaze of battle,

317so that many a Lycian in his close armor may say:

318'No, they are not inglorious, these kings of ours

319who rule in Lycia, who eat the fat sheep

320and drink the choice honey-sweet wine: their strength too

321is noble, since they fight among the first of the Lycians.'

322Old friend, if the two of us could escape this war

323and live forever, ageless and immortal,

324I would not fight among the foremost myself,

325nor would I send you into the battle where men win glory.

326But now — since the death-spirits stand over us

327in their thousands, and no mortal can flee them or slip past —

328let us go: we will give the triumph to some man, or he to us."19

329So he spoke; and Glaucus did not turn away or disobey,

330and the two went straight on, leading the great Lycian nation.

331And Menestheus, Peteos' son, shuddered to see them,

332for it was toward his tower they came, carrying ruin.

333He peered along the Achaean wall, hoping to see

334some captain who would ward off harm from his companions,

335and made out the two Ajaxes, never sated with war,

336standing there, and Teucer, just come from his hut,

337close by; but there was no way to shout and be heard,

338so great was the din — the clamor went up to heaven —

339of battered shields and horsehair-crested helmets

340and of the gates: for all had been shut, and the Trojans

341stood against them, trying to break them by force and get in.

342At once he sent the herald Thootes to Ajax:

343"Go, brilliant Thootes — run, and call Ajax;

344or rather both of them; that would be best of all,

345since sheer destruction will soon be upon us here.

346The leaders of the Lycians bear down so — men who have always

347come storming into the strong encounters.

348But if toil and struggle have risen for them there as well,

349then at least let stout Telamonian Ajax come alone,

350and let Teucer follow with him, who knows the bow well."20

351So he spoke; and the herald heard, and did not disobey,

352and set off running along the wall of the bronze-shirted Achaeans,

353and came and stood by the Ajaxes, and spoke at once:

354"You two Ajaxes, leaders of the bronze-shirted Argives:

355the dear son of Zeus-nurtured Peteos bids you

356come there, to share the toil at least a little while —

357or rather both of you; that would be best of all,

358since sheer destruction will soon be upon them there.

359The leaders of the Lycians bear down so — men who have always

360come storming into the strong encounters.

361But if here too war and struggle have risen,

362then at least let stout Telamonian Ajax come alone,

363and let Teucer follow with him, who knows the bow well."

364So he spoke, and great Telamonian Ajax did not disobey.

365At once he spoke winged words to Oïleus' son:21

366"Ajax — you and strong Lycomedes stay here,

367stand fast, and urge the Danaans to fight their hardest.

368I will go over there and take my share of the fighting,

369and come back quickly, when I have given them good defense."

370So speaking, Telamonian Ajax went off,

371and with him went Teucer, his brother by one father,

372and with them Pandion carried Teucer's curved bow.

373And when they reached the tower of great-hearted Menestheus,

374passing inside the wall — they came to men hard pressed,

375for up onto the breastworks like a dark squall

376came the powerful leaders and lords of the Lycians;

377and they clashed together in combat, and the war-cry rose.

378Telamonian Ajax was first to kill his man:

379great-hearted Epicles, Sarpedon's companion,

380striking him with a jagged block of marble that lay

381inside the wall, huge, topmost by the breastwork. Not easily

382could a man hold it in both hands, not in his youth's best,

383such as mortals are now; but he heaved it high and threw it,22

384and crushed the four-bossed helmet, and smashed the bones

385of the head all in together; and like a diver

386he dropped from the high tower, and the life left his bones.

387And Teucer hit Glaucus, Hippolochus's strong son,

388with an arrow as he came storming up the high wall,

389just where he saw the arm bared, and stopped his fighting.

390He sprang back down from the wall, keeping hidden, so that no Achaean

391might see that he was hit and glory over him with words.23

392And grief came over Sarpedon at Glaucus's going,

393the moment he knew it; but he did not forget his war-craft:

394he caught Alcmaon, Thestor's son, with a lunge of the spear

395and stabbed, then wrenched the spear out; and the man, following the spear,

396fell on his face, and his armor, intricate with bronze, clashed about him.

397And Sarpedon, gripping the breastwork with his massive hands,

398pulled — and the whole length of it followed; and above,

399the wall was laid naked; and he had made a road for many.24

400Ajax and Teucer answered him together: Teucer's arrow

401struck the shining strap that held the man-covering shield

402about his chest; but Zeus beat the death-spirits away

403from his son — he was not to be broken by the ships' sterns —25

404and Ajax, leaping in, stabbed at the shield; the spear

405did not drive through, but staggered him in mid-fury.

406He gave back a little space from the breastwork, but wholly

407he did not yield, for his heart still hoped to seize glory.

408Wheeling round, he called to the godlike Lycians:

409"Lycians, why do you slacken your rushing courage so?

410It is hard for me, powerful as I am,

411to break through alone and make a path to the ships.

412Follow me in! More hands make better work."26

413So he spoke; and they, in dread of their king's rebuke,

414pressed on harder around their counselor-king.

415And the Argives on the other side strengthened their battle-lines

416inside the wall; and the work before them showed very great:

417for neither could the powerful Lycians break the wall

418of the Danaans and make a path to the ships,

419nor could the Danaan spearmen ever push back

420the Lycians from the wall, once they had come close against it.

421But as two men quarrel over boundary-stones,

422measuring-rods in hand, in a common field,

423who in a little strip of ground contend over the fair share:27

424so the breastworks held them apart; and over them

425they hacked at one another, at the chests, at the oxhide

426shields, round and well-made, and the fluttering bucklers.

427Many were wounded in the flesh by the pitiless bronze,

428whenever a man turned and his back was left bare

429in the fighting, and many straight through the shield itself.

430Everywhere the towers and breastworks were spattered

431with the blood of men, from both sides, Trojan and Achaean.

432But even so they could not turn the Achaeans to flight:

433the lines held — as an honest working woman holds the scales,

434who with the weight in one pan and the wool in the other

435draws them level, to win a pitiful wage for her children:28

436so their battle and their war were drawn level and taut,

437until the moment Zeus handed the higher glory to Hector,

438Priam's son, who was first to leap inside the wall of the Achaeans.

439He cried a piercing cry, to carry to the Trojans:

440"Rise up, horse-taming Trojans! Break the wall

441of the Argives, and hurl prodigious fire among the ships!"

442So he spoke, spurring them on; and every ear heard him,

443and they charged the wall in one mass; and then they

444began mounting the galleries, sharpened spears in hand,

445while Hector snatched up a stone and carried it — it stood

446before the gates, thick at the base and sharp

447on top; the two best men of a district could not easily

448lever it off the ground and onto a wagon,

449such as mortals are now; but he swung it easily, even alone,29

450for the son of crooked-counseled Cronos made it light for him.

451As when a shepherd carries the fleece of a ram

452easily in one hand, and its weight is little burden,

453so Hector lifted the stone and carried it straight

455double doors, high, with two crossed bars

Notes

1 lines 1–2 — The book opens by holding the camera on Patroclus, still in the hut healing Eurypylus — where 11.848 left him, and where he stays until 15.390-404. The whole battle at the wall is thus bracketed inside Patroclus's delayed errand: the poem keeps its catastrophe waiting in a doorway. ↩

2 lines 3–35 — Unique in Homer: the narrator leaves the story's time entirely to relate the wall's future annihilation — after the sack, Poseidon and Apollo turn the eight rivers of Ida against it for nine days, Zeus rains, the trident-bearer levers out the foundations and re-beds the beach in sand. The passage explains to the poet's own audience why no trace of so great a work survived to be visited: epic erasing its own footprint (already at 7.443-464 Poseidon complained the wall would out-fame the one he built for Laomedon; Zeus there licensed this very deletion). Line 23 contains the poem's only "race of half-god men" (ἡμιθέων γένος ἀνδρῶν): elsewhere the heroes are simply men; only here, looking back from the audience's diminished present, does the narrator name the gulf. Aristotle admired the maneuver (Strabo 13.1.36 preserves the remark): "the poet who made the wall also made it disappear." ↩

3 lines 41–48 — The boar-or-lion at bay, wheeling on the closing ring: "his glorious heart knows no fear and no flight — and his courage kills him" (ἀγηνορίη δέ μιν ἔκτα, 46). The aside is proverb-grade and is the book's thesis about Hector — spoken inside a simile about him, six books before 22.99-130 proves it. ↩

4 lines 60–80 — Polydamas's first counsel, accepted. He appears here for the first time speaking (named 11.57): born the same night as Hector (18.251), the poem's designated counter-voice — his four speeches (here; 211; 13.726; 18.254) run accepted, rejected, accepted, rejected, and the last rejection kills Hector's army. The pattern begins with success: chariots left at the ditch, the wall attacked on foot. ↩

5 lines 96–97 — Asius's entry repeats his Catalogue lines (2.838-839) verbatim — our wording kept: the muster-roll recalled at the moment the muster begins to die (cf. the Merops sons, 11.329). ↩

6 lines 87–104 — The five battalions — the Trojan order of battle, with two debuts that matter: Alcathous (dead at 13.427-444) and Asteropaeus the Paeonian, "warlike," chosen by Sarpedon as third — the man whose duel with Achilles in the river (21.139-204) will be the last full combat before Hector's. Sarpedon "stood out even among all": the poem promotes him steadily toward Book 16. ↩

7 lines 108–117 — Asius refuses the plan — "the fool" (νήπιος), and the narrator files the death-notice in advance, naming the killer: Idomeneus, at 13.384-393. The prolepsis-with-obituary is the poem's standard seal on disobedience (2.38, 16.46, 20.466); "fate of the hateful name" (μοῖρα δυσώνυμος) belongs to the small class of fate-phrases where the word itself winces. ↩

8 lines 127–153 — The Lapith aristeia: Polypoetes and Leonteus keep their Catalogue credentials (2.740-746). The similes stack — oaks with roots running deep (131-134), then wild boars shattering the brush (146-150). The apparatus preserves Hephaestion's observation that 131-140 and 141-153 read like alternative versions of the same scene, only one of which was meant to stand — a rare window onto the poem's own workshop; the printed text, and this translation, keep both. ↩

9 lines 162–172 — Asius's outrage: "Father Zeus, so even you, it turns out, are an utter lover of lies!" (φιλοψευδής — nowhere else does a mortal call Zeus a liar to his face). The wasps- and-bees simile he builds — nest defenders who "will not abandon their hollow house... for their children's sake" — is finer than he knows: he has conceded that the two Lapiths are the ones fighting for something. The narrator's dry audit follows: Zeus was not persuaded; the glory was Hector's. ↩

10 lines 175–181 — "hard for me to tell it all through, as though I were a god" — the narrator's only confession of overload in the Iliad, athetized by Aristophanes and Aristarchus and omitted by Zenodotus, perhaps precisely because it lets the human singer show through the Muse's voice. The "prodigious fire... of stone" (θεσπιδαὲς πῦρ λάϊνον) is a famous compression: the stone barrage burns like fire along the whole wall — the enjambed adjective detonates the metaphor. ↩

11 lines 200–209 — The omen: an eagle on the left with a great snake in its talons; the snake, "not forgetting its war-craft," strikes the eagle's chest, is dropped in the crowd, and the eagle screams away empty. Polydamas will read it exactly (218-227): you may take the prey, but you will not carry it home. The scene is the poem's fullest bird-sign, and the interpretation is never refuted — only overruled. ↩

12 lines 230–250 — Hector's answer, culminating in the most quoted hexameter of the Iliad: "One bird-sign is best: to fight for your fatherland" (243). Cited from classical Athens onward as the motto of patriotic defense — and in context it is a king dismissing accurate intelligence on the strength of a private promise from Zeus (236) that expires at sundown (11.192-194, which Hector was not told). The rhetoric is immortal; the generalship is the disaster Polydamas predicted, and both men will say so later — Polydamas at 18.254-283, Hector to himself at 22.99-107. The closing threat (248-250) to kill any Trojan who hangs back completes the inversion: the omen-scene ends with the patriot promising to spear his own men. ↩

13 lines 251–255 — Zeus's dust-squall off Ida "bewitched the mind of the Achaeans": the day's tilt made weather. θέλγε — the verb of enchantment (Circe's verb in the Odyssey) — for what a dust storm does to a defending army's judgment. ↩

14 lines 258–264 — Siege engineering, precisely itemized: the galleries (κρόσσαι), the breastworks, the jutting buttress- piles set as footings for the towers — pulled, pried, and levered by hand. The Iliad's only sustained description of fortification assault, and the vocabulary is a hapax-cluster to match the singular event. ↩

15 lines 265–276 — The Ajaxes' exhortation sorts the army into "excellent... middling... lesser — since men are in no way all equal in war — now there is work for every man": the clearest statement in Homer of the mass army's ethic, where even the worse man's labor counts — a note closer to the hoplite line than to the duel of champions, and fittingly spoken by the defensive hero par excellence. ↩

16 lines 278–289 — The great snow simile: Zeus "shows them what his missiles are," lulls the winds, and covers peaks, headlands, clover meadows, "men's rich worked fields," harbors and shores — only the breaking wave keeps it off. The barrage of stones is rendered as the silencing of the whole worked world under steady snow: din translated into hush, war into weather, and for four lines the poem simply looks at peace. Among the most admired similes in ancient and modern criticism alike. ↩

17 lines 290–293 — "Yet not even then would the Trojans... have broken the gates, had not Zeus roused his own son Sarpedon": the breach requires a demigod, and the poet says so in the counterfactual frame he reserves for real turning-points. The lion simile that follows (299-306) is the poem's fullest: the hungry lion who will either seize his prey or "himself be hit among the foremost" — the exact two outcomes Sarpedon offers Glaucus at 328. ↩

18 lines 294–297 — Sarpedon's shield, described from the smith's bench: hammered bronze face, oxhides stitched inside with golden rods running full circle. The poem's craftsmanship- passages dignify what is about to be risked; compare the cup of Nestor (11.632-637) and Hector's stone below. ↩

19 lines 310–328 — The Sarpedon speech — the classic statement of the heroic contract, in two movements. First, honor as obligation: the seat, the cuts, the full cups, the riverside estate — all are wages, and the Lycian in the ranks must be able to say his kings earn them ("not inglorious... their strength too is noble"). Second, the deeper clause (322-328): if escaping this battle meant being "ageless and immortal forever," fighting would be senseless — I would neither fight nor send you; it is because death comes anyway, in ten thousand forms no mortal dodges, that courage is rational: "let us go: we will give the triumph to some man, or he to us." Mortality is not the objection to heroism but its premise. This is the contract Achilles has just repudiated in Book 9 (life outweighs all Troy's wealth, 9.401-409), and the poem has placed its finest defense in the mouth of the man Zeus's own son — whom Zeus will watch die in Book 16, weeping blood-rain. Quoted and imitated from antiquity forward; the whole aristocratic ethics of the West descends through these nineteen lines. ↩

20 lines 331–363 — The Menestheus episode: the Athenian captain — praised in the Catalogue as a marshal, silent ever since — peers for help and sends Thootes. The herald delivers the message nearly word for word (354-363 = 343-350 with only the deixis shifted: "here" to "there"), our wording kept: messenger- fidelity as verbal discipline. Ancient critics athetized the Teucer line in both copies (350 = 363) as needless; it is the request's whole point — the bowman is wanted against Sarpedon. ↩

21 line 365 — "Oïleus' son" — the lesser Ajax. Zenodotus read a different form of the patronymic here (Ἰλιάδην), and — a marvel of textual history — his reading appears on a vase of the sixth century BC (published J.H.S. 1898): a pre- Alexandrian variant caught in paint before any surviving manuscript. The printed text follows Aristarchus. ↩

22 lines 378–386 — Epicles goes down under the jagged marble block — "not easily could a man hold it in both hands... such as mortals are now" (the fixed diminished-present formula, 5.304) — and falls "like a diver... from the high tower." The diver- image for a death-plunge recurs at 16.742 (Cebriones, with Patroclus's terrible joke about oysters): grace at the instant of obliteration. ↩

23 lines 387–391 — Teucer's arrow finds Glaucus's bared arm, and Glaucus drops back off the wall hiding the wound, "so that no Achaean might see that he was hit and glory over him." The concealment is heroic bookkeeping — deny the enemy the boast — and the wound is a plot-load: at 16.508-531 it is this arm Glaucus begs Apollo to heal so he can defend Sarpedon's corpse. ↩

24 lines 397–399 — Sarpedon pulls down the breastwork with his bare hands: "the wall was laid naked; and he had made a road for many." He does not enter — that honor is reserved (note [30]); he opens. Made a road for many: the plain half-line has the whole tragedy of Book 16 folded in it — the road is for Hector, and for Patroclus coming the other way. ↩

25 lines 400–403 — Teucer's arrow finds only the shield-strap: "Zeus beat the death-spirits away from his son — he was not to be broken by the ships' sterns." Not saved: rescheduled. The clause quietly fixes the terms of 16.433-461, where Zeus will weigh saving him and be talked out of it by Hera. Every rescue in this poem is a date moved. ↩

26 lines 409–412 — After the grandest speech in the book, Sarpedon's working voice: I cannot break through alone — "Follow me in! More hands make better work." The proverb (πλεόνων δέ τι ἔργον ἄμεινον) is the plain-prose twin of Diomedes' "when two go together" (10.224-226): heroism, at the wall as in the night, is cooperative labor. ↩

27 lines 421–426 — The deadlock as a boundary dispute: two men with measuring-rods in a common field, quarreling "in a little strip of ground over the fair share." The war for Troy reduced to village litigation over inches — and with the surveyor's precision comes the bitter joke: this, too, is a war about property. The breastwork is the boundary-stone; the poem's great space annihilates to a farmer's furrow. ↩

28 lines 432–436 — The second scales of the poem: not Zeus's golden balance (8.69) but a wage-worker's — "an honest working woman" (γυνὴ χερνῆτις ἀληθής) holding weight against wool, drawing them level "to win a pitiful wage for her children." The adjective ἀληθής — "true, honest" — is given to the poorest person in the Iliad, and the whole stalemated battle hangs from her steady hand. The pathos is in the economics: she balances so carefully because the wage is pitiful and the children are hers. No simile in the poem reaches further down the social order, or carries more weight per line. ↩

29 lines 445–450 — Hector's stone: thick-butted, sharp-topped — a natural battering ram — "the two best men of a district could not easily lever it off the ground and onto a wagon, such as mortals are now; but he swung it easily even alone." The diminished-present formula again (its second use in the book, the fifth in the poem), and then the theological asterisk: "the son of crooked-counseled Cronos made it light for him." Line 450 was omitted by Zenodotus and athetized by Aristophanes and Aristarchus — deleting the asterisk credits Hector; keeping it credits Zeus; the printed text keeps it, and the shepherd- and-fleece simile (451-452) plays the weight both ways. ↩

30 lines 457–471 — The breach, in carpenter's detail: double doors, two crossed bars, one bolt; the stance ("legs braced wide, to give the blow its force"), both hinges sheared, the stone falling inside "by its own weight," the gates bellowing. Then the poem's great darkness-image: Hector leaps in "in his face the very likeness of swift night" — νυκτὶ θοῇ ἀτάλαντος — man as nightfall, the bronze blazing about the dark (Zenodotus read "wrapped in night," and the vulgate's phrase has passed into proverb as "sudden night"). "No one who met him could have held him back — none but the gods... and his eyes flared with fire." The book ends at the exact instant of penetration — Trojans over the wall and through the gates, Danaans in rout among the ships — the only book of the poem that closes inside an unresolved catastrophe: the door broken, and everything still to lose. ↩
