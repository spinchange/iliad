FOR FURTHER READING

The Iliad's ancient company: a guide


FOREWORD

The Iliad was never alone. It stands at the head of a crowd of
ancient works that continue it, contradict it, dramatize its
aftermath, parody it, argue about it, and forge eyewitness
rebuttals to it — a conversation that ran for well over a
thousand years before the poem reached us, and whose voices are
already present in this edition's notes (Aristarchus athetizing,
Zenodotus emending, Plutarch preserving four suppressed lines,
Strabo reporting the variant Rome preferred). This list gathers
that company. It is arranged not by date but by relationship to
the Iliad — the companion, the sequels, the stage, the parody,
the quarrel, the answer, the hoax, the scholars — because that is
how the works themselves understood their positions. Each entry
notes what survives and in what form; the losses are part of the
story, and are reported as plainly as the survivals. Dates are
approximate throughout; almost every early date in this field is
argued about.

A note on access: nearly everything here exists in a Loeb
Classical Library volume (Greek or Latin with facing English),
and most of it in public-domain translations available through
Perseus or the Internet Archive. Specific standard editions are
named where they matter.


I. THE COMPANION

THE ODYSSEY · attributed to Homer · complete, 12,109 lines
  The other survivor. Composed in the same formulaic tradition,
  plausibly within a generation or two of the Iliad, and built to
  fit around it with astonishing tact: the Odyssey retells almost
  nothing the Iliad narrates, and instead slots its Trojan
  flashbacks — the horse, the sack, the quarrel of Odysseus and
  Achilles, the ghosts of Achilles and Ajax — into the gaps the
  older poem left. Where the Iliad asks what a short glorious
  life is worth, the Odyssey asks what a long unglorious survival
  is worth; the shade of Achilles, telling Odysseus in the
  underworld that he would rather be a poor man's field-hand
  among the living than king of all the dead, is the one poem
  answering the other. A companion translation on the same
  principles as this one, sharing its formula register where the
  poems share formulas, sits beside this project.


II. THE REST OF THE STORY: THE EPIC CYCLE

Antiquity possessed the whole war in verse: six more epics,
collectively the "Epic Cycle," wrapped around the Iliad and
Odyssey like the lost panels of an altarpiece. All six perished.
What survives is a set of terse plot summaries made by one
Proclus (perhaps 2nd c. AD) for his Chrestomathy — themselves
preserved only in Photius and in the margins of Iliad
manuscripts — plus a few dozen quoted fragments. The standard
collection is M. L. West's Loeb, Greek Epic Fragments (2003).
Aristotle (Poetics 23) already saw the difference in kind: the
cyclic poets narrated everything in order, one thing after
another, while Homer took a single action and made everything
else radiate from it. The Cycle is the everything; reading even
its skeletons shows what the Iliad chose not to be.

THE CYPRIA · attributed to Stasinus · 11 books, lost; summary
and ~50 fragments
  The prequel: Zeus's plan to thin mankind, the wedding of Peleus
  and Thetis, the Judgment of Paris, the abduction of Helen, the
  gathering at Aulis, Iphigenia, Philoctetes abandoned, the first
  nine years — everything the Iliad presupposes in its opening
  line. Herodotus (2.117) already argued it could not be Homer's,
  because it contradicts the Iliad's account of Paris's voyage:
  the oldest surviving act of Homeric source-criticism.

THE AETHIOPIS · attributed to Arctinus of Miletus · 5 books,
lost; summary and fragments
  The direct sequel — it opened where our Book 24 closes, with a
  line ancient readers knew as an alternative ending to the
  Iliad itself ("So they busied themselves with the burial of
  Hector; and an Amazon came..."). Penthesileia the Amazon queen,
  killed by Achilles as they fall in love across the spear;
  Memnon the Aethiopian, son of the Dawn, killed for killing
  Antilochus; and then Achilles' own death at the Scaean Gates by
  the arrow of Paris and Apollo — the event the Iliad prophesies
  five times and refuses to narrate.

THE LITTLE ILIAD · attributed to Lesches · 4 books, lost;
summary and fragments
  The Judgment of the Arms (Achilles' armor awarded to Odysseus;
  Ajax's madness and suicide), Philoctetes fetched, Neoptolemus
  brought from Scyros — the poem behind the son Achilles mourns
  for in our Book 19 — and the building of the horse.

THE SACK OF ILION (Iliou Persis) · attributed to Arctinus ·
2 books, lost; summary and fragments
  The horse debated, Laocoon, the night of the fall: Priam killed
  at the altar of Zeus, Astyanax thrown from the wall — the death
  Andromache foresees for him at our 24.734-738 — Polyxena
  sacrificed at Achilles' tomb. The atrocities that made the
  "sack of a city" the ancient shorthand for ultimate suffering
  all lived here.

THE RETURNS (Nostoi) · attributed to Agias of Troezen · 5 books,
lost; summary
  The costly homecomings: the wreck of Ajax son of Oileus,
  Menelaus blown to Egypt, and Agamemnon walking into his own
  murder — the story the Odyssey uses as its dark counter-example
  and the tragedians made their own.

THE TELEGONY · attributed to Eugammon of Cyrene · 2 books, lost;
summary
  The Cycle's strange coda: Odysseus's last wanderings and his
  death at the hands of Telegonus, his son by Circe — killed by a
  spear barbed with a sting-ray's spine, the sea reaching inland
  at last.


III. THE SEQUEL THAT SURVIVES

QUINTUS OF SMYRNA · Posthomerica · probably 3rd c. AD · complete,
14 books
  Late antiquity, missing its Cycle, rewrote the gap: Quintus
  begins at Hector's funeral and marches in Homeric hexameters
  all the way to the sack and the storm over the returning fleet
  — Penthesileia, Memnon, the death of Achilles, the Judgment of
  the Arms, Philoctetes, the horse. Homer's manner without
  Homer's inwardness, but the only continuous ancient narrative
  of the war's end, and often better than its reputation.

TRIPHIODORUS · The Sack of Troy · 3rd-4th c. AD · complete,
691 lines
  A single night in a single sitting: a compact, theatrical
  telling of the horse and the fall.

COLLUTHUS · The Rape of Helen · c. 500 AD · complete, ~390 lines
  The last ancient Troy poem: a small, ornamental epyllion on the
  Judgment of Paris and the abduction — the war's first evening,
  written at the tradition's last.


IV. THE TRAGIC STAGE

Aeschylus is said to have called his plays "slices from the
great banquets of Homer" (Athenaeus 8.347e), and the phrase is
exact: fifth-century Athens treated the Trojan matter — mostly
the Cycle's panels rather than the Iliad's own plot — as its
common story-hoard. This is the ancient fan fiction, written by
masters, and it consistently does what fan fiction does: it goes
looking for the people the epic wronged.

AESCHYLUS · Oresteia (Agamemnon, Libation Bearers, Eumenides) ·
458 BC · complete — the only surviving trilogy
  The homecoming as catastrophe: Agamemnon walks the crimson
  cloth into the bath, and three plays work the blood out of the
  house. The Iliad's king of men becomes a study in what the war
  cost the victors; the herald's speech on the rains and frosts
  at Troy is the veteran's Iliad in twenty lines. His lost
  Achilles trilogy (Myrmidons, Nereids, Phrygians) dramatized our
  Books 9-24 directly — the surviving fragments of Achilles'
  lament over Patroclus were famous in antiquity, and their loss
  is among the tradition's heaviest.

SOPHOCLES · Ajax · c. 440s BC · complete
  What happens to the Iliad's second-best fighter when the poem's
  economy of honor fails him: cheated of Achilles' arms, mad
  among the cattle, and dead by the sword Hector gave him at the
  end of our Book 7 — the guest-gift completing its arc. The
  quiet debate over his burial replays the Iliad's final question
  (what is owed a dead enemy?) in a Greek camp without an
  Achilles to answer it.

SOPHOCLES · Philoctetes · 409 BC · complete
  The man the expedition abandoned, the bow the war requires, and
  the young son of Achilles caught between honesty and Odysseus:
  the epic's margins turned into the sharpest ethics on the
  Athenian stage.

EURIPIDES · Trojan Women · 415 BC · complete
  The sack from beneath: Hecuba, Cassandra, Andromache, one day
  on the beach among the allotted captives, and the body of
  Astyanax brought in on his father's shield — the deliberate
  answering echo of every Hector-and-Andromache scene in the
  Iliad. Produced months after Athens massacred Melos; the oldest
  surviving anti-war play, made entirely of Homer's widows.

EURIPIDES · Hecuba · c. 424 BC · complete
  Polyxena sacrificed to Achilles' ghost, Polydorus murdered by a
  guest-friend for gold — a different death from the one Achilles
  gives him at our 20.407-418; the tradition never agreed on its
  youngest victim — and the old queen's revenge: grief curdling
  into atrocity.

EURIPIDES · Andromache · c. 425 BC · complete
  The afterlife of Hector's wife as the war-prize of Achilles'
  son: the sequel to our 6.450-465, where Hector foresaw exactly
  this.

EURIPIDES · Iphigenia at Aulis · 405 BC · complete
  The war's first death, bought before the fleet could sail: the
  Cypria's story done as domestic tragedy, with Agamemnon
  drafting and redrafting the letter — the ancestor of every
  study in the cowardice of command.

EURIPIDES · Helen · 412 BC · complete
  Built on Stesichorus's recantation (below): the real Helen sat
  out the war in Egypt, and Troy burned for a phantom. The Iliad
  weighed against a hypothesis: what if it was for nothing —
  literally nothing?

RHESUS · transmitted under Euripides' name; authorship disputed ·
complete
  The one surviving tragedy that dramatizes an actual episode of
  the Iliad: our Book 10, the night raid, seen this time from the
  Trojan watch-fires. Whoever wrote it, it is the only chance to
  watch the fifth century stage a scene we also have in Homer.

Lost, and worth mourning by name: Aeschylus's Myrmidons (above);
Sophocles' Polyxena and his Lovers of Achilles; Euripides'
Alexandros (Paris exposed and recognized — partially recovered
from papyrus), Palamedes, and Philoctetes (the versions that
competed with the survivors). The tragic Troy we have is perhaps
a tenth of the tragic Troy there was.


V. LYRIC VOICES

SAPPHO · fragment 44 ("The Wedding of Hector and Andromache") ·
c. 600 BC
  The marriage the Iliad only mourns, sung whole: Hector and
  Andromache brought over the salt sea to Troy amid myrrh and
  cassia and the sound of pipes, "like to the gods." Whether it
  ends in shadow is invisible — the fragment breaks off at the
  height of the joy, which is somehow the most Iliadic thing
  about it.

STESICHORUS · Palinode · 6th c. BC · fragments
  The poet struck blind, tradition said, for blaming Helen — and
  cured on recanting: "The story is not true. You never went in
  the well-benched ships; you never came to the citadel of Troy."
  The first recorded retraction in Western literature, and the
  seed of Euripides' Helen.

PINDAR · Nemean 7, Nemean 8, Isthmian 8, and passim · early 5th
c. BC · complete odes
  The Iliad's heroes as the currency of praise: Nemean 8 on Ajax
  destroyed by the secret ballot ("envy fastens always on the
  good"); Nemean 7 on Neoptolemus dead at Delphi, with Pindar's
  startling charge that Odysseus's reputation outruns his deeds
  because sweet-worded Homer inflated it; Isthmian 8 on Themis's
  prophecy that Thetis's son would outdo his father — the divine
  near-miss behind the whole poem. Pindar reads Homer the way the
  fifth century did: as scripture to be corrected.


VI. THE PARODY

BATRACHOMYOMACHIA ("The Battle of Frogs and Mice") · Hellenistic,
though antiquity credited it to Homer · complete, ~300 lines
  A mouse drowns; the mice arm for war; Zeus convenes the gods to
  watch; crabs settle it. Every convention of the Iliad — the
  arming scene, the aristeia, the divine council, the pathetic
  obituary — miniaturized onto a pond. The ancients thought
  Homer wrote it as a joke on himself, which is false as
  scholarship and perfect as criticism.

LUCIAN · True History; Dialogues of the Dead · 2nd c. AD ·
complete
  In the True History Lucian sails to the Isle of the Blest and
  interviews Homer, settling the scholarly questions directly:
  asked about the lines the critics bracket as spurious, Homer
  says he wrote every one — a joke aimed precisely at the
  atheteses recorded throughout this edition's notes. The
  Dialogues of the Dead send Achilles, Ajax, and Helen's skull
  down to Hades to be needled by Menippus: the underworld of
  Odyssey 11 replayed as satire.


VII. THE PHILOSOPHERS' QUARREL

Homer was ancient Greece's schoolbook, scripture, and moral
authority — which is why the philosophers could not leave him
alone.

XENOPHANES and HERACLITUS · 6th-5th c. BC · fragments
  The opening shots: Xenophanes — "Homer and Hesiod attributed to
  the gods everything that is shame and reproach among men";
  Heraclitus — Homer "deserves to be thrown out of the contests
  and beaten with a staff." The scandal of the Iliad's gods,
  stated at maximum volume, within two centuries of the poem.

PLATO · Ion; Republic, books 2-3 and 10 · 4th c. BC · complete
  The Ion teases a Homeric performer who knows everything about
  the poems and nothing about anything; the Republic, with
  visible reluctance and repeated professions of love, banishes
  Homer from the ideal city for making gods lie and good men wail
  — citing the Iliad's grieving Achilles by name. "There is an
  ancient quarrel between philosophy and poetry": this is where
  it is declared, and the Iliad is the battlefield.

ARISTOTLE · Poetics · 4th c. BC · complete (the surviving book)
  The defense. Homer is "divine beyond the rest" precisely for
  what this edition's introduction calls the poem's shape: he
  took one action — not the whole war — and made a unity of it,
  and he knew, alone among epic poets, when to shut up and let
  his people speak. Aristotle's few pages set the terms of epic
  criticism permanently.

LONGINUS · On the Sublime · 1st c. AD · complete (with gaps)
  The most quotable of ancient critics on the Iliad's grandeur
  (the gods' battle, the horses' leap — our 5.770-772 — measured
  against the space of the world), and author of the famous
  verdict that in the Odyssey Homer is the setting sun: the
  greatness remains, without the intensity.

PLUTARCH · How the Young Man Should Study Poetry · 1st-2nd c. AD
· complete
  The ancient world's reading manual for Homer — how to profit
  from a poet who shows bad behavior — and, for this project, a
  benefactor: his quotation is the sole preserver of the four
  suppressed lines of Phoenix's confession (our 9.458-461 gap,
  translated in the note there from his text).

DIO CHRYSOSTOM · Oration 11, "The Trojan Discourse" · c. 100 AD ·
complete
  A sophist's tour de force arguing, with a straight face, that
  Homer lied and Troy won the war. Silly and serious at once: the
  earliest full display of how much play there is in a story
  everyone knows from one contested source.


VIII. ROME'S ANSWER

VIRGIL · Aeneid · 19 BC · complete, 12 books
  The losers' sequel and the Iliad's greatest reply. Virgil takes
  the one Trojan the Iliad conspicuously saves — Aeneas, rescued
  by Poseidon in our Book 20 "so that the race of Dardanus not
  perish seedless," with the note there recording the variant
  Strabo knew, in which his line shall rule "over all" — and
  builds Rome on the prophecy. Books 1-6 rewrite the Odyssey,
  books 7-12 the Iliad, and the poem ends on a deliberate,
  troubling inversion of our Book 24: where Achilles yields to a
  suppliant, Aeneas, remembering a dead friend, does not.

OVID · Metamorphoses, books 12-13; Heroides 3 · c. 8 AD ·
complete
  Metamorphoses 12-13 run the war past at Ovid's speed —
  invulnerable Cygnus, the centaurs' brawl told by Nestor grown
  even older, and the Judgment of the Arms staged as a full
  courtroom debate between Ajax's deeds and Ulysses' words (words
  win; Ajax becomes a flower). Heroides 3 is the letter of
  Briseis to Achilles: the woman over whom the Iliad's quarrel
  broke, given at last a voice and a viewpoint — the ancient poem
  this project's Briseis passages most want beside them.

CATULLUS · Poem 64 · c. 55 BC · complete
  The wedding of Peleus and Thetis in miniature epic, with the
  Fates singing the unborn Achilles as a prophecy of glorious
  slaughter — the Iliad's future tense made a lullaby, and meant
  to chill.

SENECA · Trojan Women · 1st c. AD · complete
  Euripides' beach re-staged in the imperial manner: Astyanax and
  Polyxena, rhetoric at the cliff's edge. The Troy play as Rome's
  meditation on its own capacity for atrocity.

ILIAS LATINA · attributed to Baebius Italicus · 1st c. AD ·
complete, ~1,070 lines
  The Iliad compressed into a Latin school text — and, after the
  West lost its Greek, the thread by which the poem itself (as
  opposed to the Troy story) stayed faintly alive in Europe for
  a thousand years.


IX. THE HOAX

DICTYS CRETENSIS · Journal of the Trojan War · Greek original
c. 1st-2nd c. AD (papyrus-confirmed), Latin version 4th c. ·
complete in Latin
DARES PHRYGIUS · The Fall of Troy: A History · Latin, 5th-6th c. ·
complete
  The fake eyewitnesses: "Dictys" claims to be a Cretan soldier's
  war diary dug up from his tomb, "Dares" a Trojan priest's
  account (he is borrowed from our 5.9, where a Dares serves as
  priest of Hephaestus) — both "predating" Homer, both correcting
  him, both stripping out the gods, and both written centuries
  after him. The Middle Ages, preferring eyewitnesses to poets,
  took them as history: through Benoît de Sainte-Maure and Guido
  delle Colonne they begat the medieval Troy, and thence
  Boccaccio, Chaucer's Troilus and Criseyde, and Shakespeare's
  Troilus and Cressida — a whole tradition descended from two
  forgeries and a minor character. (Troilus himself gets one line
  in the Iliad, among the dead sons Priam counts at 24.257.)

PHILOSTRATUS · Heroicus ("On Heroes") · early 3rd c. AD ·
complete
  A Phoenician traveler and a vinedresser talk in a vineyard
  haunted by the ghost of Protesilaus — the first Achaean to die
  at Troy, dead before the Iliad opens — who corrects Homer on
  points of detail from personal experience. The gentlest of the
  rebuttal-fictions, and the one closest to this project's next
  undertaking: it is, in its way, an interview with the fallen.


X. THE SCHOLARS

THE ALEXANDRIANS · Zenodotus, Aristophanes of Byzantium,
Aristarchus · 3rd-2nd c. BC · surviving only through the scholia
  The founders of the text: the librarians of Alexandria who
  collated the manuscripts, invented the critical signs, bracketed
  what they doubted, and fixed the vulgate this translation
  renders. They appear throughout this edition's notes as living
  interlocutors — Zenodotus making Agamemnon stand, Aristarchus
  doubting the divine singer and the Judgment of Paris — because
  the poem we have is, in part, their argument.

THE SCHOLIA · especially the Venetus A manuscript (10th c. AD) ·
edited by Erbse (1969-88)
  The margins of the great Byzantine Iliad manuscripts preserve,
  in compressed centuries-deep layers, the wreckage of ancient
  Homeric scholarship — Aristarchus's judgments, rejected
  variants, allegorists, grammarians, the lot. The Venetus A,
  whose margins carry the richest layer, is the single most
  important physical object in the poem's transmission; its
  apparatus is the ancestor of the one extracted for this
  project. High-resolution images of the manuscript itself are
  freely available online through the Homer Multitext project.

PORPHYRY · Homeric Questions · 3rd c. AD · partially preserved
  The problem-and-solution tradition: apparent contradictions in
  Homer posed and resolved, some brilliantly, some by main force.
  The ancestor of every "but in Book 5 he was wounded" argument
  readers have with epics to this day.

EUSTATHIUS OF THESSALONICA · Commentaries on the Iliad · 12th c.
AD · complete, enormous
  A Byzantine archbishop's line-by-line commentary that folds in
  everything he could still read of the older scholarship — much
  of it now surviving nowhere else. The last ancient reader, in
  effect, writing at the door of the modern ones.

THE CONTEST OF HOMER AND HESIOD (Certamen) · compiled 2nd c. AD
from older material · complete
  A fiction in which the two founding poets compete before a
  king: Hesiod asks the trick questions, Homer answers in
  effortless hexameters, and the prize goes to Hesiod — because
  the judge prefers the poet of peace and farming to the poet of
  slaughter. The tradition's own verdict on the Iliad's
  greatness, and its own joke about what greatness is for.


A CLOSING NOTE

The shortest true history of ancient literature is that it is a
long argument with the Iliad. The Odyssey answers it; the Cycle
completes it; the tragedians prosecute it on behalf of its
victims; the philosophers put it on trial; Alexandria edits it;
Rome appropriates it; the forgers depose it; the satirists
interview it; and the scholiasts annotate every round of the
fight in the margins. Any door in this list opens back into the
poem. Begin anywhere.
