# The Iliad — index of names and places

Every named person, god, people, and place in the poem, with pronunciation, epithets, aliases, kin, and line citations. Generated from `index/entries/` by `tools/merge_index.py`; see `index/canon.md` for the editorial method and `index/pron-key.md` for the pronunciation scheme.

**Abantes** (Ἄβαντες) · PEOPLE — *Say:* uh-BAN-teez
The Euboean contingent of the Catalogue of Ships, holding Chalcis, Eretria,
Histiaea, Cerinthus, Dios, Carystus and Styra, and led by Elephenor son of
Chalcodon. The poem marks them twice over: they wear their hair long behind and
shorn in front, and they are spearmen who close to break corslets rather than
fight at range.
*Kin:* led by Elephenor, Chalcodon's son.
**Refs:** 2.536, 2.541, 2.542, 4.464. Books 2, 4.
*See also:* Achaeans.

---

**Abydos** (Ἄβυδος) · PLACE — *Say:* uh-BY-dos
A town on the Asian shore of the Hellespont, held with Sestus and Arisbe by
Asius' contingent of Trojan allies. It is named twice more for the men who come
from it: Democoon, Priam's bastard son, who kept the fast mares there, and the
Phaenops whose shape Apollo borrows to shame Hector.
**Refs:** 2.836, 4.500, 17.584. Books 2, 4, 17.
*See also:* Asius, Arisbe, Hellespont.

---

**Acamas** (Ἀκάμας) · MORTAL — *Say:* AK-uh-mas
Two men of the name fight on the Trojan side, and the poem keeps them apart. The
first is a son of Antenor, named with his brother Archelochus in the Catalogue
and again among Hector's captains at 11.60; in Book 14 he stands over
Archelochus' body and kills Promachus in vengeance, vaunting over him, and
Meriones runs him down as he mounts his chariot at 16.342. The second is a
Thracian leader, Eussorus' son, "the man who was best among the Thracians," whom
Telamonian Ajax kills at 6.8; Ares had worn his likeness to rally the Trojans a
book earlier (5.462).
*Kin:* the Trojan Acamas is Antenor's son and Archelochus' brother; the Thracian
is Eussorus' son.
*Kills:* Promachus (14.476–477). *Killed by:* Meriones (16.342); the Thracian
Acamas by Ajax (6.8).
**Refs:** 2.823, 2.844, 5.462, 6.8, 11.60, 12.100, 14.476, 14.478, 14.488,
16.342. Books 2, 5, 6, 11, 12, 14, 16.
*See also:* Antenor, Archelochus, Peiroos, Thracians, Meriones.

**Achaea** (Ἀχαιΐς) · PLACE — *Say:* uh-KEE-uh
The Achaeans' homeland, named in the formula "horse-pasturing Argos and Achaea of
the beautiful women" that closes the terms of the duel in Book 3, and turned
against the army as a taunt in Menelaus' reproach — "Women of Achaea, men of
Achaea no longer" (7.96).
**Refs:** 3.75, 3.258, 7.96, 11.770. Books 3, 7, 11.
*See also:* Achaeans, Argos.

---

**Achaeans** (Ἀχαιοί) · PEOPLE — *Say:* uh-KEE-unz
The poem's default name for the Greeks besieging Troy, used interchangeably with
Argives and Danaans for the same host. After Zeus and the Trojans it is the
commonest name in the Iliad: the army is the standing audience before which honor
is won and lost, the assembly that must be persuaded, and the collective whose
griefs the second line of the poem announces (1.2).
*Epithets:* "the long-haired Achaeans" (κάρη κομόωντες Ἀχαιοί, 2.11); "the
strong-greaved Achaeans" (ἐϋκνήμιδες Ἀχαιοί, 1.17); "the bronze-shirted Achaeans"
(χαλκοχίτωνες Ἀχαιοί, 1.371).
*Also called:* Argives; Danaans — metrically convenient synonyms for the same
host, not distinct peoples. The Myrmidons and Hellenes are Achilles' own
contingent and are not synonyms for the whole army.
**Refs:** 1.2, 1.17, 1.371, 2.11, 2.51 …and throughout.
Books 1–24.
*See also:* Argives, Danaans, Myrmidons, Hellenes, Achaea, Agamemnon.

---

**Achilles** (Ἀχιλλεύς) · MORTAL — *Say:* uh-KIL-eez
The poem's subject, greatest of the Achaeans, whose wrath is its announced theme
and its first word. Agamemnon's seizure of Briseis drives him from the fighting;
his refusal of the embassy in Book 9 hardens the quarrel past mending; and
Patroclus' death in his own armor brings him back not to win the war but to kill
Hector. The Iliad ends not with that vengeance but with him giving the body up to
Priam, having been argued out of his rage by an old man who kisses his hands.
*Epithets:* "swift-footed" (πόδας ὠκύς / ποδάρκης, 1.58); "brilliant" (δῖος, 1.7);
"Zeus-sprung" (διογενής, 1.489).
*Also called:* Peleus' son; Aeacus' grandson; Thetis' son.
*Kin:* son of Peleus and the sea-nymph Thetis; grandson of Aeacus; father of
Neoptolemus, reared on Scyros. Companion of Patroclus; foster-son of Phoenix.
**Refs:** 1.1, 1.58, 1.348, 9.199, 16.2, 18.22, 22.330, 24.596 …and throughout.
Books 1–2, 4–24.
*See also:* Patroclus, Hector, Agamemnon, Thetis, Peleus, Aeacus, Briseis, Priam,
Phoenix.

**Actor** (Ἄκτωρ) · MORTAL — *Say:* AK-tor
A name borne by more than one man of the older generation, all of them fathers
rather than fighters. The poem's Actors include Azeus' son, in whose house
Astyoche bore Ares' twin sons Ascalaphus and Ialmenus; the father of Menoetius,
and so Patroclus' grandfather (11.785, 16.14); the father of Echecles (16.189);
and the father of the twins Cteatus and Eurytus, called both "Actor's line" and
"the two sons of Actor," who crowd past Nestor's horses in his reminiscence of
the Epeian games.
*Kin:* variously father of Menoetius, of Echecles, and of the Molione twins.
**Refs:** 2.513, 2.621, 11.750, 11.785, 13.186, 16.14, 16.189, 23.638.
Books 2, 11, 13, 16, 23.
*See also:* Menoetius, Patroclus, Amarynceus.

**Adamas** (Ἀδάμας) · MORTAL — *Say:* AD-uh-mas
Son of Asius, one of the Trojan captains who storm the wall in Book 12. In the
fighting at the ships he stabs Antilochus' shield and slips back into the ranks
"dodging doom," but Meriones catches him with a spear-cast in the groin and he
dies writhing like a roped bull. Deiphobus names him among the dead Trojan
leaders at 13.771.
*Kin:* son of Asius, Hyrtacus' son.
*Killed by:* Meriones (13.567–575).
**Refs:** 12.140, 13.561, 13.566, 13.760, 13.771. Books 12, 13.
*See also:* Asius, Meriones, Antilochus.

---

**Admetus** (Ἄδμητος) · MORTAL — *Say:* ad-MEE-tus
Father of Eumelus and husband of Alcestis, "brilliant among women, loveliest of
the daughters of Pelias." He does not come to Troy: the name is a patronymic
throughout, marking his son, who brings eleven ships from Pherae and drives the
best horses in the Achaean host.
*Kin:* husband of Alcestis; father of Eumelus.
**Refs:** 2.713, 2.714, 23.289, 23.391, 23.532. Books 2, 23.
*See also:* Eumelus, Alcestis.

---

**Adrastus** (Ἄδρηστος) · MORTAL — *Say:* uh-DRAS-tus
King of Sicyon in the older generation — "Sicyon, where Adrastus first was king"
— and owner of Arion, the swift horse "whose line was from the gods," against
which Antilochus is told even a better driver could not have won. His daughters
marry into the poem's Argive houses: Aegialeia is named as Diomedes' wife at
5.412, and Tydeus married a daughter of his after settling at Argos (14.121). The
translation spells him *Adrastus* and the Trojan-side leader *Adrestus*, keeping
two men distinct whom one spelling would merge; see `canon.md`.
*Kin:* father of Aegialeia; father-in-law of Tydeus.
**Refs:** 2.572, 5.412, 14.121, 23.347. Books 2, 5, 14, 23.
*See also:* Adrestus, Arion, Diomedes, Sicyon.

---

**Adrestus** (Ἄδρηστος) · MORTAL — *Say:* uh-DRES-tus
Three Trojan-side men bear this spelling. One leads the contingent from Adresteia
and Apaesus with his brother Amphius, sons of Merops of Percote, who foresaw their
deaths and could not keep them home. The second is the suppliant of Book 6, thrown
from a bolting chariot, who clasps Menelaus' knees and offers ransom. Menelaus is
on the point of granting it when Agamemnon runs up and shames him — "Soft-hearted
Menelaus!" — so that Menelaus pushes the man back with his hand and Agamemnon
does the killing (6.62–65). A third falls to Patroclus. The translation reserves
*Adrastus* for the Sicyon king.
*Kin:* the Percotian Adrestus is Merops' son and Amphius' brother.
*Killed by:* Agamemnon (6.63–65); the third by Patroclus (16.694).
**Refs:** 2.830, 6.37, 6.45, 6.63, 16.694. Books 2, 6, 16.
*See also:* Adrastus, Amphius, Merops, Agamemnon, Menelaus.

---

**Aeacus** (Αἰακός) · MORTAL — *Say:* EE-uh-kus
Peleus' father and Achilles' grandfather, himself sprung from Zeus (21.189). He
never appears: in all its occurrences the name is a patronymic, and the
translation keeps the generations apart by a fixed rule — "Aeacus' son" is
**Peleus**, "Aeacus' grandson" is **Achilles**, except inside the fixed epithet
formulas, where "the swift-footed son of Aeacus" and "Aeacus' blameless son" mean
Achilles. See `translation/CONVENTIONS.md`.
*Kin:* son of Zeus; father of Peleus; grandfather of Achilles.
**Refs:** 2.860, 2.874, 9.184, 16.15, 16.140, 16.854, 21.178, 21.189 …and
throughout. Books 2, 9–11, 16–18, 21, 23.
*See also:* Achilles, Peleus.

**Aeneas** (Αἰνείας) · MORTAL — *Say:* ee-NEE-us *or* uh-NEE-us
Leader of the Dardanians and, after Hector, Troy's best fighter. Son of Anchises
and Aphrodite, he is twice snatched from death by gods who have plans for him —
by his mother and Apollo when Diomedes crushes his hip with a boulder (5.311ff),
and by Poseidon when Achilles has him beaten, in the scene that gives the poem its
one glance past its own ending: Aeneas is destined to escape, "so that the race of
Dardanus not perish seedless," and his line will rule the Trojans hereafter
(20.302–308).
*Also called:* Anchises' son.
*Kin:* son of Anchises and Aphrodite; of the line of Dardanus through Assaracus.
**Refs:** 2.820, 5.166, 5.311, 5.445, 13.459, 16.620, 17.323, 20.302 …and
throughout. Books 2, 5–6, 8, 11–17, 20, 23.
*See also:* Anchises, Aphrodite, Dardanus, Dardanians, Achilles, Diomedes.

---

**Aesepus** (Αἴσηπος) · MORTAL — *Say:* ee-SEE-pus
A Trojan twin, killed with his brother Pedasus by Euryalus in the general rout of
Book 6. The poem gives the pair a full genealogy at the moment of their death:
the water-nymph Abarbarea bore them to Bucolion, Laomedon's eldest son, got in
secret while he was shepherding. The name is also a river of the Troad, one of
those the gods turn against the Achaean wall (12.21).
*Kin:* son of Bucolion and the nymph Abarbarea; twin of Pedasus.
*Killed by:* Euryalus (6.20–28).
**Refs:** 2.825, 4.91, 6.21, 12.21. Books 2, 4, 6, 12.
*See also:* Pedasus, Bucolion, Euryalus, Laomedon.

---

**Aethe** (Αἴθη) · OTHER — *Say:* EE-thee
Agamemnon's mare, lent to Menelaus for the chariot race at Patroclus' funeral —
a gift to Agamemnon from Echepolus son of Anchises, made so that he might stay
home from the war. Nestor's son Antilochus is warned that she will "pour shame"
on his horses, and her fury nearly carries Menelaus past him at the finish.
*Kin:* yoked with Menelaus' own horse Podargus.
**Refs:** 23.295, 23.408, 23.524. Book 23.
*See also:* Podargus, Menelaus, Antilochus, Echepolus.

---

**Aetolians** (Αἰτωλοί) · PEOPLE — *Say:* ee-TOH-lee-unz
The contingent from Pleuron, Olenus, Pylene, Chalcis and Calydon, led to Troy by
Thoas son of Andraemon with forty ships. The Catalogue explains the odd
succession: Oeneus' sons were dead and Meleager with them, so the whole rule fell
to Thoas. Their older war against the Curetes over Calydon is the setting of
Phoenix's Meleager story in Book 9.
*Kin:* led by Thoas, Andraemon's son.
**Refs:** 2.638, 2.643, 4.527, 9.529, 9.531, 13.218, 23.633. Books 2, 4–5, 9,
13, 15, 23.
*See also:* Thoas, Meleager, Oeneus, Calydon, Curetes.

---

**Agamemnon** (Ἀγαμέμνων) · MORTAL — *Say:* ag-uh-MEM-non
Commander of the whole Achaean expedition and Achilles' antagonist in the quarrel
that starts the poem. His refusal to give Chryseis back brings the plague, and his
taking of Briseis in compensation drives Achilles from the fighting; the disaster
that follows is his to answer for, and in Book 19 he answers for it by blaming
Blindness sent from Zeus. His authority rests on the sceptre whose descent
Hephaestus to Zeus to Hermes to Pelops to Atreus to Thyestes to himself the poem
traces at 2.101–108: he rules "many islands and all of Argos," and he is
consistently a worse fighter than the men he commands.
*Epithets:* "lord of men" (ἄναξ ἀνδρῶν, 1.7); "wide-ruling" (εὐρὺ κρείων, 1.102);
"shepherd of the people" (ποιμὴν λαῶν, of Agamemnon at 2.243) — the last is a
general royal epithet, used of Atreus and others too.
*Also called:* Atreus' son — in the singular this means Agamemnon unless the scene
is Menelaus'; see `canon.md`.
*Kin:* son of Atreus, grandson of Pelops; brother of Menelaus; husband of
Clytemnestra.
**Refs:** 1.24, 1.130, 2.100, 2.107, 4.148, 9.114, 11.91, 19.86 …and throughout.
Books 1–11, 13–14, 16–19, 23–24.
*See also:* Achilles, Menelaus, Atreus, Briseis, Chryseis, Blindness.

---

**Agenor** (Ἀγήνωρ) · MORTAL — *Say:* uh-JEE-nor
Antenor's son, "a man blameless and strong," and the last Trojan to stand in
Achilles' path before Hector. Apollo puts courage in him at 21.545 and he turns to
face the charge, breaking off his own debate with the formula the poem gives its
cornered men — "But why does my own heart argue these things with me?" (21.562,
as at 11.407, 17.97, 22.122, 22.385). He casts, and Apollo snatches him away in
mist, then wears his shape to decoy Achilles from the walls: the trick that lets
the routed Trojans get inside the gates.
*Kin:* son of Antenor; father of Echeclus (20.474).
**Refs:** 4.467, 11.59, 12.93, 13.490, 15.340, 20.474, 21.545, 21.595, 21.600.
Books 4, 11–16, 20–21.
*See also:* Antenor, Achilles, Apollo, Hector.

**Ajax** (Αἴας) · MORTAL — *Say:* AY-jaks
Telamon's son, from Salamis with twelve ships: the biggest man in the army and its
best fighter after Achilles, whom Helen picks out from the wall as "gigantic Ajax,
wall of the Achaeans." He carries a tower-like shield of seven oxhides and an
eighth layer of bronze, made by Tychius of Hyle. The lot chooses him to duel
Hector in Book 7, and they part at nightfall exchanging gifts; he is one of the
three sent to Achilles in Book 9, where his short blunt speech comes nearest to
moving him; and in Book 15 he holds the last deck alone against the fire, giving
ground plank by plank. Unqualified "Ajax" means him unless the line marks the
other.
*Epithets:* "wall of the Achaeans" (ἕρκος Ἀχαιῶν, 3.229).
*Also called:* Telamonian Ajax; Telamon's son; the great Ajax.
*Kin:* son of Telamon; half-brother of Teucer.
**Refs:** 2.557, 3.229, 7.183, 7.219, 9.622, 15.727, 17.132 …and throughout.
Books 1–18, 23.
*See also:* Ajax son of Oïleus, Ajaxes, Telamon, Teucer, Hector, Achilles.

---

**Ajaxes** (Αἴαντε) · MORTAL — *Say:* AY-jak-seez
The dual — Telamonian Ajax and Ajax son of Oïleus fighting as a single unit, and
the poem's standing image of a defensive line that holds. They are always found
where the wall or the ships are about to break: marshaling the towers in Book 12,
addressed first by Poseidon when he comes to rally the army in Book 13, carrying
Imbrius' body off like two lions with a goat, and holding the line over Patroclus'
corpse in Book 17. The Book 4 simile of a black storm-cloud coming on before the
West Wind is given to them together.
*Also called:* the two Ajaxes.
**Refs:** 2.406, 4.273, 4.280, 12.265, 13.46, 13.197, 17.507, 17.732, 18.157.
Books 2, 4–8, 10, 12–13, 16–18.
*See also:* Ajax, Ajax son of Oïleus, Poseidon, Patroclus.

**Alastor** (Ἀλάστωρ) · MORTAL — *Say:* uh-LAS-tor
At least three men. One is a Pylian, named among Nestor's captains at 4.295 and
twice paired with Mecisteus son of Echius as a stretcher-bearer, carrying the
wounded off to the ships (8.333, 13.422). Another is a Trojan-side victim in
Odysseus' rally at 5.677. The third is named only as a father: Tros, "Alastor's
son," is the suppliant who reaches for Achilles' knees and is killed anyway at
20.463.
**Refs:** 4.295, 5.677, 8.333, 13.422, 20.463. Books 4–5, 8, 13, 20.
*See also:* Nestor, Odysseus, Tros.

---

**Alcathous** (Ἀλκάθοος) · MORTAL — *Say:* al-KATH-oh-us
A Trojan captain, Aesyetes' son, married to Anchises' eldest daughter Hippodameia.
Idomeneus kills him in one of the poem's strangest deaths: Poseidon charms his eyes
and shackles his limbs so that he stands unmoving "like a grave-pillar or a tree in
high leaf" and cannot dodge, and his heart goes on beating hard enough to shake the
spear standing in it. Deiphobus calls Aeneas to avenge the man who reared him.
*Kin:* son of Aesyetes; husband of Hippodameia, Anchises' eldest daughter;
brother-in-law and foster-father to Aeneas.
*Killed by:* Idomeneus (13.427–444).
**Refs:** 12.93, 13.428, 13.465, 13.496. Books 12, 13.
*See also:* Idomeneus, Aeneas, Anchises, Poseidon.

---

**Alcimedon** (Ἀλκιμέδων) · MORTAL — *Say:* al-SIM-uh-don
Son of Laerces and grandson of Haemon, one of the five company-commanders Achilles
sets over the Myrmidons when Patroclus goes out (16.197). In Book 17 he finds
Automedon fighting alone from Achilles' chariot with Patroclus dead and Hector
wearing the armor, tells him he has lost his wits, and takes the reins so that
Automedon can fight on foot.
*Kin:* son of Laerces, grandson of Haemon.
**Refs:** 16.197, 17.467, 17.475, 17.481, 17.500, 17.501. Books 16, 17.
*See also:* Automedon, Achilles, Myrmidons, Patroclus.

---

**Alpheius** (Ἀλφειός) · PLACE — *Say:* al-FEE-us
The great river of the western Peloponnese, "which flows wide through the
Pylians' land." It marks Nestor's country in his reminiscences — the cattle-raid
that reached its sacred stream by midday, the ford named in the Catalogue — and
as a river-god it heads a Pylian genealogy: Alpheius begot Ortilochus, who
fathered Diocles, whose twin sons Aeneas kills at 5.541–560.
**Refs:** 2.592, 5.545, 11.712, 11.726, 11.728. Books 2, 5, 11.
*See also:* Nestor, Pylos, Elis.

---

**Altes** (Ἄλτης) · MORTAL — *Say:* AL-teez
Lord of the Lelegians, "holding steep Pedasus on the Satnioeis," and father of
Laothoe, one of Priam's wives. He is named only through his grandsons: Lycaon
recites the descent while begging Achilles for his life at 21.85, and Priam names
him again mourning Lycaon and Polydorus at 22.51, recalling the dowry "the famous
name" gave with his daughter.
*Kin:* father of Laothoe; grandfather of Lycaon and Polydorus.
**Refs:** 21.85, 21.86, 22.51. Books 21, 22.
*See also:* Laothoe, Lycaon, Polydorus, Priam, Pedasus.

---

**Amarynceus** (Ἀμαρυγκεύς) · MORTAL — *Say:* am-uh-RIN-syoos
An Epeian lord of the older generation, father of Diores. Nestor's last long
reminiscence is of the funeral games his sons held for him at Buprasium, where
Nestor beat every man there at boxing, wrestling, running and the spear, and lost
only the chariot race to the two sons of Actor — the memory he offers Achilles as
he accepts a prize he no longer has to compete for.
*Kin:* father of Diores.
**Refs:** 2.622, 4.517, 23.630. Books 2, 4, 23.
*See also:* Diores, Nestor, Actor, Epeians, Buprasium.

---

**Amazons** (Ἀμαζόνες) · PEOPLE — *Say:* AM-uh-zonz
A people of women who fight, named only in the back-story and only twice: Priam
recalls campaigning against them in Phrygia in his youth, and Bellerophon is set
to fight them as the third of the impossible tasks he survives.
*Epithets:* "the match of men" (ἀντιάνειραι, 3.189).
**Refs:** 3.189, 6.186. Books 3, 6.
*See also:* Priam, Bellerophon.

---

**Amphidamas** (Ἀμφιδάμας) · MORTAL — *Say:* am-FID-uh-mas
Two men. One is of Cythera, a link in the descent of the boar's-tusk helmet
Odysseus wears on the night raid: Autolycus stole it, gave it to Amphidamas at
Scandeia, who gave it to Molus, whose son Meriones lends it on. The other is of
Opoeis, whose son Patroclus killed as a boy in a quarrel over dice — "a child's
act; I did not mean it" — the killing that brought him to Peleus' house.
*Kin:* the Cytheran is a guest-friend of Molus; the Opoeisian is father of the boy
Patroclus killed.
**Refs:** 10.268, 10.269, 23.87. Books 10, 23.
*See also:* Meriones, Molus, Patroclus, Opoeis.

---

**Amphimachus** (Ἀμφίμαχος) · MORTAL — *Say:* am-FIM-uh-kus
Two men, both leaders in the Catalogue. The Epeian is a son of Cteatus, of Actor's
line, one of four captains from Elis; Hector spears him in the chest as he comes
into the fighting at the ships and is reaching to strip his helmet when Ajax
drives him back, and the lesser Ajax kills Imbrius in wrath for him. The other is
a Carian, son of Nomion and brother of Nastes, one of the pair who "went to war
decked in gold, like a girl."
*Kin:* the Epeian is Cteatus' son, of Actor's line; the Carian is Nomion's son and
Nastes' brother.
*Killed by:* Hector (13.185–187).
**Refs:** 2.620, 2.870, 2.871, 13.185, 13.189, 13.195, 13.203. Books 2, 13.
*See also:* Nastes, Actor, Cteatus, Hector, Ajax.

---

**Anchises** (Ἀγχίσης) · MORTAL — *Say:* an-KY-seez
Aeneas' father by Aphrodite, who "conceived him to Anchises herding cattle" on the
spurs of Ida. He does not fight: he belongs to the genealogy Aeneas recites to
Achilles at 20.208–241, the Dardanian line running Dardanus — Erichthonius — Tros
— Assaracus — Capys — Anchises — Aeneas, the branch that survives when Priam's does
not. A different Anchises, of Sicyon, is the father of the Echepolus who bribed
Agamemnon with a mare to be let off the war (23.296).
*Kin:* son of Capys; father of Aeneas by Aphrodite; father-in-law of Alcathous.
**Refs:** 2.819, 5.247, 5.313, 13.428, 17.491, 20.208, 20.240, 23.296.
Books 2, 5, 12–13, 17, 20, 23.
*See also:* Aeneas, Aphrodite, Dardanus, Capys, Alcathous, Echepolus.

---

**Andromache** (Ἀνδρομάχη) · MORTAL — *Say:* an-DROM-uh-kee
Hector's wife, daughter of Eëtion of Thebe-under-Placus, whose father and seven
brothers Achilles killed in the sack of that city — so that, as she tells Hector,
he is now father and mother and brother to her as well as husband. She meets him
at the Scaean Gates in Book 6 with their son in her arms and begs him to fight
from the wall; he refuses, and the scene ends with the child frightened by his own
father's helmet. She is at her loom, ordering a bath for him, when the wailing
tells her he is dead (22.437–515), and she leads the lament over his body in the
poem's last scene.
*Epithets:* "white-armed" (λευκώλενος, 6.371).
*Kin:* daughter of Eëtion; wife of Hector; mother of Astyanax.

**Antenor** (Ἀντήνωρ) · MORTAL — *Say:* an-TEE-nor
The wisest of the Trojan elders and the one who says plainly what the war is
about. He had hosted Menelaus and Odysseus on the embassy that first asked for
Helen back, and he identifies them for Priam from the wall; in Book 7 he tells the
assembly to give Helen and her goods to the Atreidae, since "it is with sworn
oaths made false that we fight," and Paris refuses. Many of his sons die in the
poem — Archelochus, Acamas, Iphidamas, Coon, Polybus — and Agenor is the last to
face Achilles.
*Kin:* husband of Theano, Athena's priestess in Troy; father of Archelochus,
Acamas, Agenor, Iphidamas, Coon, Polybus, and others.
**Refs:** 2.822, 3.148, 3.203, 3.262, 6.299, 7.347, 11.221, 14.463, 21.546.
Books 2–7, 11–12, 14–15, 19–21.
*See also:* Theano, Agenor, Acamas, Archelochus, Iphidamas, Coon, Helen, Paris.

**Antilochus** (Ἀντίλοχος) · MORTAL — *Say:* an-TIL-oh-kus
Nestor's son, the youngest of the Achaean captains and among the fastest. He kills
the first Trojan of the general fighting (4.457) and fights hard through the middle
books; but his two great scenes are late. It falls to him to carry the news of
Patroclus' death to Achilles, and he does it holding Achilles' hands, afraid he
will cut his own throat (18.32). In the funeral games he takes second place in the
chariot race by forcing Menelaus into a narrow gully — his father having coached
him at length in exactly such guile — and then gives the mare back when Menelaus
challenges him on oath, in a scene of tact that ends the quarrel.
*Also called:* Nestor's son.
*Kin:* son of Nestor; brother of Thrasymedes.
**Refs:** 4.457, 5.565, 13.396, 15.585, 16.317, 17.685, 18.2, 18.32, 23.514,
23.541 …and throughout. Books 4–6, 13–18, 23.
*See also:* Nestor, Menelaus, Achilles, Patroclus, Thrasymedes.

**Antimachus** (Ἀντίμαχος) · MORTAL — *Say:* an-TIM-uh-kus
The Trojan who took Paris' gold and argued in the assembly against giving Helen
back — and who urged that Menelaus be killed on the spot when he came as an envoy.
Agamemnon kills two of his sons, Peisander and Hippolochus, in Book 11 and refuses
their ransom on the strength of it: "now you will pay for your father's foul
outrage." A third son falls to Leonteus at 12.188.
*Kin:* father of Peisander, Hippolochus, and Hippomachus.
**Refs:** 11.123, 11.132, 11.138, 12.188. Books 11, 12.
*See also:* Agamemnon, Menelaus, Paris, Helen, Leonteus.

**Antiphus** (Ἄντιφος) · MORTAL — *Say:* AN-tih-fus
Three men. On the Achaean side, a son of Thessalus and grandson of Heracles, who
leads thirty ships with his brother Pheidippus from Cos and the Calydnian islands.
On the Trojan side, a son of Talaemenes who leads the Maeonians with Mesthles, and

half-brother Isus riding one chariot, both of them men Achilles had once caught

*Kin:* variously son of Thessalus; son of Talaemenes; son of Priam.

**Aphareus** (Ἀφαρεύς) · MORTAL — *Say:* AF-uh-ryoos
Son of Caletor, one of the younger Achaean captains who keep company with Meriones
and Deïpyrus on guard duty in Book 9 and in the line Idomeneus calls to in Book 13.

**Aphrodite** (Ἀφροδίτη) · GOD — *Say:* af-roh-DY-tee
Paris' patron and the cause, through the judgment implied at 24.29–30, of the war
itself. She rescues Paris out of the duel in a mist and carries him to Helen's
bedchamber, then bullies Helen into following — the poem's coldest scene between
goddess and woman. In Book 5 she comes to save Aeneas, her son, and Diomedes
wounds her hand; she flees weeping to Olympus, where Dione comforts her and Zeus
tells her to keep to the works of marriage and leave war alone. She lends Hera the
embroidered strap of desire in Book 14, and in the theomachy Athena knocks her
flat with a blow to the breast.
*Epithets:* "laughter-loving" (φιλομειδὴς Ἀφροδίτη, 3.424).
*Also called:* Cypris — used in Book 5 only.
*Kin:* daughter of Zeus and Dione; mother of Aeneas by Anchises.
**Refs:** 3.374, 3.389, 3.424, 5.131, 5.312, 5.330, 5.375, 14.188, 21.416, 24.699.
Books 2–5, 9, 14, 19–24.
*See also:* Paris, Helen, Aeneas, Anchises, Dione, Diomedes, Hera, Athena.

**Apisaon** (Ἀπισάων) · MORTAL — *Say:* ap-ih-SAY-on
Two Trojan-side men, killed in the same words. Phausius' son falls to Eurypylus at
11.578, struck "in the liver below the midriff," and Paris shoots Eurypylus in the
thigh as he strips the body. Hippasus' son, a Paeonian and "their best in battle
after Asteropaeus," falls to Lycomedes at 17.348 in a line repeated verbatim from
the first.
*Kin:* one is Phausius' son; the other Hippasus' son.
*Killed by:* Eurypylus (11.578–579); Lycomedes (17.348–349).
**Refs:** 11.578, 11.582, 17.348. Books 11, 17.
*See also:* Eurypylus, Lycomedes, Asteropaeus, Paris.

**Apollo** (Ἀπόλλων) · GOD — *Say:* uh-POL-oh
Troy's chief defender among the gods and the poem's first mover: Chryses' prayer
brings him down from Olympus "like nightfall," and his arrows loose the plague
that starts the quarrel (1.43–53). He fights on the Trojan side throughout —
snatching Aeneas and Hector out of danger, standing off Diomedes at 5.437–444,
levelling the Achaean wall — and his decisive act is to strike Patroclus from
behind with the flat of his hand, knocking off helmet and corslet and leaving him
dazed for Euphorbus and Hector (16.788–806). He puts strength in Hector one last
time in Book 22 and then leaves him when Zeus's scales sink. In Book 24 he keeps
Hector's body from disfigurement and shames the gods into ransoming it.
*Epithets:* "who strikes from afar," standalone "the far-striker" (ἑκηβόλος /
ἕκατος, 1.15 / 1.385); "the worker-from-afar" (ἑκάεργος, 1.147); "god of the
silver bow" (ἀργυρότοξος, 1.37); "the Lycian-born" (Λυκηγενής, 4.101); "famed for
the bow" (κλυτότοξος, 4.101); "of the golden sword" (χρυσάορος, 5.509).
*Also called:* Phoebus; Smintheus — the cult title of Chryses' prayer (1.39).
*Kin:* son of Zeus and Leto; brother of Artemis.
**Refs:** 1.36, 1.43, 1.370, 1.479, 4.101, 5.437, 16.788, 21.545, 22.213, 24.18
…and throughout. Books 1–2, 4–5, 7–13, 15–18, 20–24.
*See also:* Phoebus, Chryses, Artemis, Leto, Patroclus, Hector, Aeneas.

**Archelochus** (Ἀρχέλοχος) · MORTAL — *Say:* ar-KEL-oh-kus
One of Antenor's sons, named in the Catalogue and again among the captains of the
Trojan storming-parties at the wall, always paired with his brother Acamas as
"skilled in all fighting." Telamonian Ajax kills him in Book 14 with a cast meant
for Polydamas: the spear takes him where head and neck join and shears both
tendons, so that his head strikes the ground before his knees do. Ajax then calls
across to Polydamas to say whether the man is a fair exchange for Prothoenor.
*Kin:* son of Antenor; brother of Acamas.
*Killed by:* Ajax (14.463–468).
**Refs:** 2.823, 12.100, 14.463. Books 2, 12, 14.
*See also:* Antenor, Acamas, Ajax, Polydamas, Prothoenor.

**Areithous** (Ἀρηΐθοος) · MORTAL — *Say:* uh-REE-thoh-us
Three men. The first is the fighter of Nestor's Book 7 reminiscence, called by his
surname the Club-man because he fought with neither bow nor long spear but smashed
companies apart with a club of iron; Lycurgus killed him by craft in a narrow lane
where the club was useless, and stripped the armor that passed to Ereuthalion.
The same man is named at 7.8–10 as the father of Menesthius of Arne, whom Paris
kills, by ox-eyed Phylomedusa. A third Areithous is a Thracian charioteer,
Rhigmus' aide, whom Achilles spears in the back at 20.487.
*Kin:* the Club-man is husband of Phylomedusa and father of Menesthius.
*Killed by:* the Club-man by Lycurgus (7.142–145); the Thracian aide by Achilles
(20.487–489).
**Refs:** 7.8, 7.10, 7.137, 7.138, 20.487. Books 7, 20.
*See also:* Menesthius, Lycurgus, Ereuthalion, Nestor, Rhigmus, Achilles.

**Ares** (Ἄρης) · GOD — *Say:* AIR-eez
The war-god, who fights on the Trojan side and is the least dignified of the
Olympians in the poem's handling of him. Athena leads him off the field by the
hand in Book 5 and then, at Diomedes' shoulder, rams the mortal's spear into his
belly, so that he bellows like ten thousand men and flees to Zeus, who tells him
he is the most hateful of the gods. In Book 21 Athena knocks him flat with a
boundary-stone and he covers seven acres falling. His son Ascalaphus is killed at
Troy (13.518) and he is barred from avenging him by Zeus's designs.
*Epithets:* "bane of mortals" (βροτολοιγός, 5.31); "blood-stained" (μιαιφόνος,
5.31); "stormer of walls" (τειχεσιπλήτης, 5.31); "rushing" (θοῦρος, 5.30);
"insatiate of war" (ἆτος πολέμοιο, 5.388); "brazen Ares" (χάλκεος Ἄρης, 5.704);
"massive" (ὄβριμος, 5.845); "turncoat" (ἀλλοπρόσαλλος, 5.831).
*Also called:* Enyalius — chiefly in the formula "the equal of Enyalius, slayer of
men" for Meriones (2.651).
*Kin:* son of Zeus and Hera; father of Ascalaphus and Ialmenus by Astyoche.
**Refs:** 5.30, 5.31, 5.385, 5.859, 13.521, 15.113, 21.391, 21.406 …and
throughout. Books 2–13, 15–24.
*See also:* Enyalius, Athena, Diomedes, Ascalaphus, Aphrodite, Zeus.

**Argives** (Ἀργεῖοι) · PEOPLE — *Say:* AR-jyvz *or* AR-gyvz
One of the poem's three interchangeable names for the whole Greek host, taken from
Argos and used without distinction from Achaeans and Danaans — the choice among
them is metrical, not ethnic. Helen is "Argive Helen" (2.161); the plague and the
grief are the Argives' as often as the Achaeans'; and the assemblies, the funeral
games, and the fighting at the ships are all narrated with the three names in
rotation.
*Also called:* Achaeans; Danaans — the same host under three names. The Myrmidons
and Hellenes are Achilles' own contingent and are not synonyms for the army.
**Refs:** 1.79, 1.119, 2.109, 2.159, 5.3, 7.350, 9.22 …and throughout.
Books 1–19, 21–24.
*See also:* Achaeans, Danaans, Argos, Helen.

**Argos** (Ἄργος) · PLACE — *Say:* AR-gos
Agamemnon's realm — the sceptre makes him "lord over many islands and all of
Argos" (2.108) — and, loosely, the whole Greek mainland the army came from and
hopes to go home to: "horse-pasturing Argos," "Achaean Argos, udder of the
plowland." The Catalogue gives a narrower Argos, held with Tiryns by Diomedes
(2.559); Hera names it first of the three cities dearest to her, with Sparta and
Mycenae, and offers all three for Troy's destruction (4.52–53). Bellerophon's
Ephyra lies "in a fold of horse-pasturing Argos" (6.152).
**Refs:** 1.30, 2.108, 2.559, 4.52, 6.152, 9.22, 9.141, 19.115, 24.437.
Books 1–7, 9, 12–15, 19, 24.
*See also:* Argives, Agamemnon, Diomedes, Mycenae, Hera.

**Arion** (Ἀρίων) · OTHER — *Say:* uh-RY-on

**Arisbe** (Ἀρίσβη) · PLACE — *Say:* uh-RIZ-bee
A town on the Trojan side, held with Sestus and Abydos by Asius son of Hyrtacus,
whose tall fire-colored horses came from there, from the river Selleïs — a detail
the poem repeats verbatim when he storms the wall (2.838 = 12.96). Axylus, killed
by Diomedes in Book 6, lived there and kept an open house by the road; and it is

**Artemis** (Ἄρτεμις) · GOD — *Say:* AR-tuh-mis
Apollo's sister, huntress and killer of women, who takes the Trojan side in the
theomachy and is humiliated for it: Hera catches both her wrists in one hand,
strips the bow from her shoulders, boxes her about the ears with it until the
arrows spill, and she flees weeping to Zeus's knees like a dove from a hawk
(21.479–513). Elsewhere she works chiefly in the back-stories, as the god whose
anger kills — Andromache's mother, Bellerophon's daughter, Niobe's six daughters,
and the boar sent against Calydon when Oeneus forgot her offering.
*Epithets:* "who showers arrows" (ἰοχέαιρα, 5.53); "of the golden reins"
(χρυσήνιος, 6.205).
*Kin:* daughter of Zeus and Leto; sister of Apollo.

**Ascalaphus** (Ἀσκάλαφος) · MORTAL — *Say:* as-KAL-uh-fus
A son of Ares, who with his brother Ialmenus leads thirty ships from Aspledon and
Minyan Orchomenus; Astyoche bore the two of them in Actor's house, having gone up
to her upper chamber where the god lay with her in secret. He stands the night
watch in Book 9 and dies in Book 13, when Deiphobus' cast — aimed elsewhere —
takes him through the shoulder. Ares knows nothing of it, penned on Olympus by
Zeus's designs, until Hera tells him at 15.110; then he beats his thighs and calls
for his armor.
*Kin:* son of Ares and Astyoche; brother of Ialmenus.
*Killed by:* Deiphobus (13.518–520).
**Refs:** 2.512, 9.82, 13.478, 13.518, 13.526, 15.112. Books 2, 9, 13, 15.

**Asclepius** (Ἀσκληπιός) · GOD — *Say:* as-KLEE-pee-us
The healer, father of the army's two physicians, Podaleirius and Machaon, who lead
thirty ships from Tricca and Ithome. He does not appear: the name is a patronymic
throughout, and the poem calls him "the blameless healer" whenever Machaon is
fetched to a wound. His own healing knowledge came from Chiron, who "once gave his
father" the soothing drugs Machaon uses on Menelaus (4.219).
*Kin:* father of Machaon and Podaleirius; taught by Chiron.
**Refs:** 2.731, 4.194, 4.204, 11.518, 11.614, 14.2. Books 2, 4, 11, 14.

**Asius** (Ἄσιος) · MORTAL — *Say:* AY-see-us
Three men bear the name. The chief one is Hyrtacus' son, leader of the contingent
from Percote, Sestus, Abydos and Arisbe, "whom horses carried from Arisbe, tall
and fire-colored, from the river Selleïs" — a couplet repeated verbatim in Book 12
when he alone of the Trojan captains refuses Polydamas' advice to dismount and
drives his chariot at the gate. Idomeneus spears him under the chin, and he falls
like an oak cut for ship-timber, bellowing and clawing at the bloody dust. A
second Asius is Hecuba's brother, son of Dymas, whose likeness Apollo borrows to
urge Hector on (16.717); a third is the father of Phaenops of Abydos (17.583).
*Kin:* the leader is son of Hyrtacus and father of Adamas; the second is son of
Dymas and brother of Hecuba.
*Killed by:* Idomeneus (13.387–393).
**Refs:** 2.837, 2.838, 12.96, 12.110, 12.140, 13.384, 13.771, 16.717, 17.583.

*See also:* Adamas, Idomeneus, Polydamas, Arisbe, Hecuba, Phaenops.

**Asteropaeus** (Ἀστεροπαῖος) · MORTAL — *Say:* as-ter-oh-PEE-us
Leader of the Paeonians of the long lances, at Troy eleven days when he dies, and
picked out by Sarpedon with Glaucus as the best of the allies after himself. In
Book 21 he stands up out of the Scamander to face Achilles, throws both spears at
once — "for he was a two-handed fighter" — grazes Achilles' elbow with one and
pins the other's shield with the first cast to draw the blood of a god's gift.
Achilles kills him with the sword and strips him; the corslet and the Thracian
sword become prizes at Patroclus' games.
*Kin:* son of Pelegon; grandson of the river Axius by Periboea, daughter of

*Killed by:* Achilles (21.179–182).

**Astyanax** (Ἀστυάναξ) · MORTAL — *Say:* as-TY-uh-naks
Hector's infant son, carried to the Scaean Gates in his nurse's arms and
frightened by his own father's helmet. The poem glosses the name twice and both
times the gloss is the child's doom: his father called him Scamandrius, "but the
others Astyanax — for Hector alone was the saving of Ilion" (6.402–403); and
Andromache, keening over the body, repeats it — "Astyanax, 'the lord of the city,'
as the Trojans call him by name: for you alone guarded their gates and their long
walls" (22.506–507). She foresees him pushed from other men's tables and
fatherless.
*Also called:* Scamandrius — Hector's own name for him.
*Kin:* son of Hector and Andromache; grandson of Priam.
**Refs:** 6.403, 22.500, 22.506. Books 6, 22.
*See also:* Hector, Andromache, Scamandrius, Priam.

**Athena** (Ἀθήνη) · GOD — *Say:* uh-THEE-nuh
The Achaeans' fiercest divine partisan and, with Hera, the implacable enemy of
Troy. She checks Achilles' sword-hand in Book 1, seizing him by the hair and
visible to him alone; she stops the army's flight to the ships in Book 2; and on
Zeus's word — obtained by Hera — she goes down to make the Trojans break the truce
first (4.64–73). In Book 5 she mounts Diomedes' chariot herself and guides his
spear into Ares' belly. Her last work is the cruelest: she takes Deiphobus' shape
to persuade Hector to stand, hands Achilles his spear back, and leaves Hector
calling for a brother who is not there (22.214–247).
*Epithets:* "grey-eyed," standalone "grey-eyes" (γλαυκῶπις, 1.206 / 8.373); "the
driver of the spoil" (ἀγελείη, 4.128); "Unwearied one" (Ἀτρυτώνη, 2.157) —
translated, not transliterated; "she of the mighty father" (ὀβριμοπάτρη, 5.747);
"guardian of the city" (ἐρυσίπτολις, 6.305).
*Also called:* Pallas; Tritogeneia (4.515).
*Kin:* daughter of Zeus.
**Refs:** 1.194, 1.200, 2.156, 4.73, 5.1, 5.856, 21.406, 22.214 …and throughout.
Books 1–11, 13–24.
*See also:* Pallas, Zeus, Hera, Diomedes, Achilles, Hector, Ares.

**Athenians** (Ἀθηναῖοι) · PEOPLE — *Say:* uh-THEE-nee-unz
The contingent from Athens, "the district of great-hearted Erechtheus," under
Menestheus son of Peteos with fifty ships. They are a minor presence in the
fighting: "masters of the war-cry" when Agamemnon comes on them in his review of
the army, and picked men holding the line against Hector's charge at the ships in
Book 13, where Stichius, Pheidas and Bias fight beside Menestheus. Iasus, son of
Sphelus, is set a captain over them, and Aeneas kills him at 15.332.
*Kin:* led by Menestheus, Peteos' son.
**Refs:** 2.550, 4.328, 13.196, 13.689, 15.337. Books 2, 4, 13, 15.

**Atreus** (Ἀτρεύς) · MORTAL — *Say:* AY-tree-us *or* AY-troos
Father of Agamemnon and Menelaus, and the man from whose hand the sceptre passed
to Thyestes and so to Agamemnon in the descent traced at 2.101–108. He is dead
before the poem opens and never acts in it: in nearly all of the name's 181
occurrences it is a patronymic, and "Atreus' son" in the singular means Agamemnon
unless the scene is Menelaus's — the duel of Book 3, the oath-breaking and the
wounding of Book 4, the fight over Patroclus' body. The dual "Atreus' sons" means
the two brothers together. See `canon.md` for the rule.
*Kin:* son of Pelops; brother of Thyestes; father of Agamemnon and Menelaus.
**Refs:** 1.7, 2.105, 2.106, 2.249, 3.271, 9.340, 11.131 …and throughout.
Books 1–11, 13–14, 16–17, 19, 22–24.
*See also:* Agamemnon, Menelaus, Thyestes, Pelops.

**Augeias** (Αὐγείας) · MORTAL — *Say:* aw-JEE-us
An Epeian king of the older generation, "lord of men," named only in genealogy and
in Nestor's long reminiscence of the Pylian cattle-raid. He had kept four
prize-winning horses that came to Elis for the games and sent their driver home
grieving — the debt that provoked Neleus' raid. Nestor's first kill in that fight
was Augeias' son-in-law Mulius, husband of his eldest daughter, tawny-haired
Agamede.
*Kin:* grandfather of Polyxeinus through Agasthenes; father of Agamede;
father-in-law of Mulius.
**Refs:** 2.624, 11.701, 11.739. Books 2, 11.
*See also:* Nestor, Epeians, Elis, Mulius, Agamede, Polyxeinus.

**Automedon** (Αὐτομέδων) · MORTAL — *Say:* aw-TOM-uh-don
Achilles' charioteer, "the man he honored most after Achilles breaker of men, and
trustiest at his post to await the shout of battle" (16.146–147). He yokes Xanthus
and Balius for Patroclus and drives him out; when Patroclus is dead he fights on
alone from the chariot, swooping like a vulture on geese and taking no man,
because one man cannot both hold the immortal horses and use a spear — until
Alcimedon asks who took his wits and takes the reins from him. He drives Achilles
back to the war in Book 19 and helps serve Priam his supper in Book 24.
*Kin:* son of Diores (17.429, 17.474).
**Refs:** 9.209, 16.145, 16.864, 17.429, 17.459, 17.474, 19.396, 24.574.
Books 9, 16–17, 19, 23–24.
*See also:* Achilles, Patroclus, Alcimedon, Alcimus, Xanthus, Balius.

**Axius** (Ἀξιός) · PLACE — *Say:* AK-see-us
The Paeonian river, twice praised in the same phrase — "the wide-flowing Axius,
whose water spreads fairest over the earth" — as the country the Paeonians of the
curved bows come from, with Amydon, under Pyraechmes. As a river-god he lay with
Periboea, eldest daughter of Acessamenus, and begot Pelegon, so that Asteropaeus
recites him as the head of his own line when Achilles asks who he is (21.157–160).
**Refs:** 2.849, 2.850, 16.288, 21.141, 21.157, 21.158. Books 2, 16, 21.
*See also:* Asteropaeus, Pelegon, Paeonians, Pyraechmes, Amydon.

**Bear** (Ἄρκτος) · OTHER
The constellation set on the Shield of Achilles among the Pleiades, the Hyades and
Orion — "the Bear, which men also call by the name of the Wagon, who turns in her
own place, keeping watch on Orion, and alone has no share in the baths of Ocean"
(18.487–489). The one occurrence; the token's other appearances in the poem are
the imperative "Bear up."
*Also called:* the Wagon.
**Refs:** 18.487. Book 18.
*See also:* Orion, Ocean, Shield of Achilles, Hephaestus.

**Bellerophon** (Βελλεροφόντης) · MORTAL — *Say:* buh-LAIR-uh-fon
Glaucus' grandfather, whose story is the long back-piece of the Book 6 meeting
with Diomedes. Proetus, believing his wife Anteia's lie, sent him to Lycia with
"baneful signs" scratched in a folded tablet — the poem's one mention of writing —
and the Lycian king set him three tasks meant to kill him: the Chimaera, the
Solymi, the Amazons. He survived all three and an ambush besides, and was given
the king's daughter and half his kingly honor. He ended hated by the gods,
wandering the Aleian plain alone and eating out his heart, his son killed by Ares
and his daughter by Artemis. His guest-friendship with Oeneus is what stops
Glaucus and Diomedes from fighting.
*Kin:* son of Glaucus, grandson of Sisyphus; father of Isander, Hippolochus and
Laodameia; grandfather of Glaucus and, through Laodameia and Zeus, of Sarpedon.
**Refs:** 6.155, 6.162, 6.190, 6.196, 6.200, 6.217, 6.220. Book 6.
*See also:* Glaucus, Hippolochus, Sarpedon, Proetus, Anteia, Chimaera, Oeneus,
Diomedes.

**Bias** (Βίας) · MORTAL — *Say:* BY-us
Three men. One is a Pylian, "Bias, shepherd of the people," named among the
captains Nestor is arraying at 4.296. Another is an Athenian, "brave Bias," in
Menestheus' company at the ships (13.691). The third is named only as a father:
Achilles kills "Laogonus and Dardanus, the two sons of Bias," tumbling both from
their horses, one with a spear-throw and one with the sword (20.460–462).
*Kin:* the third is father of Laogonus and Dardanus.
**Refs:** 4.296, 13.691, 20.460. Books 4, 13, 20.
*See also:* Nestor, Menestheus, Laogonus, Achilles.

**Blindness** (Ἄτη) · GOD
Ἄτη personified — the ruinous folly that comes over a man before he acts. Phoenix
makes her the runner the limping Prayers can never catch: "Blindness is strong and
sound of foot, and far outruns them all, and gets in first over all the earth
tripping mankind" (9.505–507). Agamemnon builds his whole apology on her in Book
19, calling her "the eldest daughter of Zeus, who blinds all men," whose feet are
soft and walk over men's heads, and telling how she once blinded Zeus himself over
the birth of Heracles, so that he seized her by her shining braids and flung her
out of heaven forever. She also takes Patroclus' mind at 16.805, when Apollo has
struck him.
*Kin:* eldest daughter of Zeus.
**Refs:** 9.504, 9.505, 9.512, 16.805, 19.91, 19.126, 19.129, 19.136.
Books 9, 16, 19.
*See also:* Prayers, Agamemnon, Zeus, Phoenix, Patroclus.

**Boeotians** (Βοιωτοί) · PEOPLE — *Say:* bee-OH-shunz
The first contingent of the Catalogue of Ships, under five leaders — Peneleos,
Leïtus, Arcesilaus, Prothoenor and Clonius — with fifty ships and a hundred and
twenty young men to each. They hold the left of the line at the muster, and turn
up again where the fighting is thickest: Hera watches two of them killing Argives
in Book 5, and Hector cuts down Arcesilaus, "a leader of the bronze-shirted
Boeotians," at 15.330.
*Kin:* led by Peneleos, Leïtus, Arcesilaus, Prothoenor and Clonius.
**Refs:** 2.494, 2.510, 2.526, 5.710, 13.685, 13.700, 15.330. Books 2, 5, 13, 15.
*See also:* Peneleos, Leïtus, Arcesilaus, Prothoenor, Achaeans.

**Boreas** (Βορέης) · GOD — *Say:* BOR-ee-us
The north wind, personified and genealogically active. He is the cold blast behind
the snow-simile of Book 19; he desired Erichthonius' three thousand mares as they
pastured, covered them in the likeness of a dark-maned stallion, and got twelve
foals so light they ran over standing grain without breaking it (20.223–226); and
in Book 23 Iris fetches him with Zephyrus from the feast in Zephyrus' house, at
Achilles' prayer, to make Patroclus' pyre burn.
**Refs:** 19.358, 20.223, 23.195, 23.208. Books 19, 20, 23.
*See also:* Zephyrus, Iris, Erichthonius, Patroclus, Achilles.

**Briseis** (Βρισηΐς) · MORTAL — *Say:* bry-SEE-is
Achilles' prize, taken from him by Agamemnon in compensation for Chryseis, and so
the occasion — not the cause — of the quarrel that is the poem's subject. Achilles
had taken her out of Lyrnessus, where he killed her husband Mynes; she goes from
his hut unwilling, led out by Patroclus. Agamemnon offers her back in Book 9 with
an oath he never touched her, and swears that oath publicly over a boar in Book
19. Her one speech is the lament for Patroclus, who had promised to make her
Achilles' wedded wife in Phthia and who "was gentle, always" — after which the
women wail "for Patroclus in show; but each for her own sorrows." She is restored
to Achilles' side in the poem's last book.
*Epithets:* "fair-cheeked" (καλλιπάρῃος, 1.184).
*Also called:* the daughter of Briseus.
*Kin:* daughter of Briseus; widow of Mynes of Lyrnessus.
**Refs:** 1.184, 1.323, 1.346, 2.689, 9.107, 19.246, 19.282, 24.676.
Books 1–2, 9, 19, 24.
*See also:* Briseus, Achilles, Agamemnon, Patroclus, Chryseis, Mynes, Lyrnessus.

**Briseus** (Βρισεύς) · MORTAL — *Say:* BRY-syoos
Briseis' father, who never appears and is never described. The name occurs only in
the periphrasis "the daughter of Briseus" — Achilles' complaint to Thetis (1.392)
and, twice over, in Agamemnon's list of amends and Odysseus' verbatim relaying of
it (9.132 = 9.274).
*Kin:* father of Briseis.
**Refs:** 1.392, 9.132, 9.274. Books 1, 9.
*See also:* Briseis, Achilles, Agamemnon.

**Cadmeans** (Καδμεῖοι) · PEOPLE — *Say:* kad-MEE-unz
The Thebans of the earlier war, named only in the back-story of Tydeus' embassy.
Sent forward alone from the Asopus, he found them feasting in Eteocles' house,
challenged them to contests and won every one with Athena's help; they set fifty
young men to ambush him on the road home, and he killed all but one. Agamemnon
tells the story to shame Diomedes, and Athena tells it again herself to shame him
further; Diomedes recalls it to her on the night raid.
**Refs:** 4.385, 4.388, 4.391, 5.804, 10.288. Books 4, 5, 10.
*See also:* Tydeus, Diomedes, Athena, Eteocles, Thebes, Maeon.

---

**Calchas** (Κάλχας) · MORTAL — *Say:* KAL-kus
The army's seer, "far the best of bird-readers, who knew what is, what will be,
and what was before," and who had guided the ships to Ilion by the craft Apollo
gave him. He names the plague's cause only after Achilles guarantees his safety,
and Agamemnon answers him as "seer of evils" — the exchange that opens the quarrel.
Odysseus recalls his reading of the serpent and the nine sparrows at Aulis, which
fixed the war at nine years and a tenth. In Book 13 Poseidon takes his shape to
rally the two Ajaxes, and Ajax son of Oïleus knows the god by his footprints going
away — "and that is not Calchas."
*Also called:* Thestor's son.
*Kin:* son of Thestor.
**Refs:** 1.69, 1.86, 1.105, 2.300, 2.322, 13.45, 13.70. Books 1, 2, 13.
*See also:* Apollo, Agamemnon, Achilles, Poseidon, Ajax son of Oïleus, Aulis.

**Calydon** (Καλυδών) · PLACE — *Say:* KAL-ih-don
Oeneus' city in Aetolia, "rocky Calydon" of the Catalogue and "lovely Calydon" of
Phoenix's tale. Artemis raised the war between the Curetes and the Aetolians over
it in anger at Oeneus' forgotten harvest-offering, and it is the city Meleager
would not defend until his wife described what a sacked town looks like — the
exemplum Phoenix presses on Achilles in Book 9. Poseidon takes the shape of Thoas,
who rules "all Pleuron and steep Calydon," to stir Idomeneus; and Diomedes names it
in reciting Portheus' three sons.
**Refs:** 2.640, 9.530, 9.531, 9.577, 13.217, 14.116. Books 2, 9, 13, 14.
*See also:* Meleager, Oeneus, Aetolians, Curetes, Phoenix, Thoas, Artemis.

**Capaneus** (Καπανεύς) · MORTAL — *Say:* KAP-uh-nyoos
One of the Seven against Thebes and father of Sthenelus, Diomedes' charioteer. He
does not appear: the name is a patronymic throughout — "Sthenelus, Capaneus' son"
— and it carries the argument his son makes at 4.404–410, that the sons who took
seven-gated Thebes with a smaller host were better men than the fathers who
perished there by their own recklessness.
*Kin:* father of Sthenelus.
**Refs:** 2.564, 4.367, 4.403, 5.108, 5.109, 5.241, 5.319. Books 2, 4, 5.
*See also:* Sthenelus, Diomedes, Thebes, Tydeus.

**Cassandra** (Κασσάνδρη) · MORTAL — *Say:* kuh-SAN-druh
Priam's daughter, "the loveliest of Priam's daughters," whom Othryoneus asked for
without bride-gifts, promising to drive the Achaeans out of Troy; Priam bowed his
head to it, and Idomeneus killed him for it. She has one living appearance, in the
last book: she has climbed Pergamus and is the first to see her father coming back
with the herald and the body on the bier, and her cry through the city brings all
Troy out to the gates. The poem says nothing of her prophecy.
*Kin:* daughter of Priam and Hecuba.
**Refs:** 13.366, 24.699. Books 13, 24.
*See also:* Priam, Othryoneus, Hector, Idomeneus.

**Cebriones** (Κεβριόνης) · MORTAL — *Say:* seb-RY-oh-neez
Hector's half-brother, "a bastard son of far-famed Priam," who takes the reins
after Archeptolemus is killed and drives for Hector through the rest of the poem.
He is the man Hector left the chariot to when the Trojans went at the wall on foot,
and it is he who tells Hector the Trojans are being driven on the other wing. In
Book 16 Patroclus kills him with a jagged stone that crushes both brows together
and drops his eyes into the dust at his feet, so that he goes over the rail "like a
diver" — and Patroclus mocks him for it before the fight over his body, where he
lies "mighty in his mightiness, his horsemanship all forgotten."
*Kin:* bastard son of Priam; brother of Hector.
*Killed by:* Patroclus (16.737–743).
**Refs:** 8.318, 11.521, 12.91, 13.790, 16.727, 16.738, 16.756, 16.776.
Books 8, 11–13, 16.
*See also:* Hector, Patroclus, Priam, Archeptolemus.

**Chiron** (Χείρων) · MORTAL — *Say:* KY-ron
"The most righteous of the Centaurs," named four times and always as a source of
gifts to the house of Peleus. He taught the healing drugs Achilles knows and passed
on to Patroclus, and which reach Menelaus' arrow-wound through Machaon's hands,
Asclepius having had them from him. And he gave Peleus the Pelian ash from the peak
of Pelion, "to be death for heroes" — the spear no other Achaean could swing, which
Patroclus alone of Achilles' arms leaves behind when he borrows the rest.
**Refs:** 4.219, 11.832, 16.143, 19.390. Books 4, 11, 16, 19.
*See also:* Achilles, Peleus, Patroclus, Machaon, Asclepius, Pelion.

**Chromius** (Χρομίος) · MORTAL — *Say:* KROH-mee-us
At least four men, on both sides. One is a Pylian, in the group Nestor is arraying
at 4.295. A son of Priam of the name is taken with his brother Echemmon, both from
one chariot, when Diomedes falls on them like a lion among cattle (5.160). A
Lycian falls in Odysseus' rally at 5.677, and another Trojan-side man to Teucer's
bow at 8.275. A fifth is alive in Book 17, ranged among the Trojan allies Hector
harangues and then, with Aeneas and Hector, drawing back from Automedon's rescuers
and leaving Aretus dead.
**Refs:** 4.295, 5.160, 5.677, 8.275, 17.218, 17.494, 17.534. Books 4, 5, 8, 17.
*See also:* Nestor, Diomedes, Odysseus, Teucer, Aretus, Automedon.

**Chryse** (Χρύση) · PLACE — *Say:* KRY-see
The town of Apollo's priest Chryses, over which the god "stands guard" with Cilla
and Tenedos in the prayer that brings the plague. It is where the hecatomb has to
go: Odysseus sails there with the girl and the beasts, and the ceremony on its
shore — the prayer, the thigh-bones, the paean sung all afternoon — is the poem's
fullest account of a sacrifice made right.
**Refs:** 1.37, 1.100, 1.390, 1.431, 1.451. Book 1.
*See also:* Chryses, Chryseis, Apollo, Cilla, Tenedos, Odysseus.

**Chryseis** (Χρυσηΐς) · MORTAL — *Say:* kry-SEE-is
Daughter of the priest Chryses, given to Agamemnon out of the plunder of Eëtion's
Thebe. Agamemnon refuses her ransom and says he prefers her to Clytemnestra, "since
she is not her inferior in build or bearing, in mind or in any work of her hands" —
so the god's arrows come, and when he is forced to give her up he takes Briseis
instead. Odysseus commands the ship that carries her home and puts her in her
father's arms at the altar. She never speaks.
*Epithets:* "fair-cheeked" (καλλιπάρῃος, 1.143).
*Kin:* daughter of Chryses.
**Refs:** 1.112, 1.143, 1.182, 1.310, 1.369, 1.439. Book 1.
*See also:* Chryses, Chryse, Agamemnon, Apollo, Briseis, Odysseus.

**Chryses** (Χρύσης) · MORTAL — *Say:* KRY-seez
Apollo's priest, whose daughter Agamemnon holds as a prize. He comes to the ships
with a ransom past counting and the god's wreaths on a golden staff; the army
approves, Agamemnon sends him off harshly, and his prayer walking alone along the
shore brings the plague that begins the poem. When Odysseus returns the girl to
his arms at the altar of Chryse he prays a second time — "Once before you heard me
when I prayed" — and the plague stops. He does not appear again.
*Kin:* father of Chryseis.
**Refs:** 1.11, 1.12, 1.370, 1.442, 1.450. Book 1.
*See also:* Chryseis, Chryse, Apollo, Agamemnon, Odysseus.

**Clytius** (Κλυτίος) · MORTAL — *Say:* KLIT-ee-us
One of Priam's brothers, a son of Laomedon, who sits with Lampus and Hicetaon
among the elders at the Scaean Gates, "finished with war now because of age, but
speakers of quality." He is named in both Trojan genealogies — the elder-list of
Book 3 and Aeneas' recital of the house of Dardanus at 20.238 — and his son
Caletor, carrying fire toward a ship, is killed by Ajax under Hector's eyes; Hector
calls on the Trojans to save "the son of Clytius" and cast at Ajax for him. A
second Clytius is the father of an Achaean Dolops killed by Hector at 11.302.
*Kin:* son of Laomedon; brother of Priam, Tithonus, Lampus and Hicetaon; father of
Caletor.
**Refs:** 3.147, 11.302, 15.419, 15.427, 20.238. Books 3, 11, 15, 20.
*See also:* Priam, Laomedon, Lampus, Hicetaon, Caletor, Dolops.

---

**Coeranus** (Κοίρανος) · MORTAL — *Say:* SEER-uh-nus
Meriones' aide and charioteer, who had followed him from well-founded Lyctus in
Crete. Hector casts at Idomeneus, misses, and hits Coeranus instead — the spear
under the jaw and ear, dashing his teeth out at the roots and cutting the tongue
through the middle, so that he crashes from the chariot and pours the reins to the
ground. The poem stops to say that his driving up had saved Idomeneus, on foot and
in danger: "to him he came as light, and beat off the pitiless day — and lost his
own life." A different Coeranus is a Lycian killed by Odysseus at 5.677.
*Killed by:* Hector (17.610–619).
**Refs:** 5.677, 17.611, 17.614. Books 5, 17.
*See also:* Meriones, Idomeneus, Hector, Lyctus.

**Coon** (Κόων) · MORTAL — *Say:* KOH-on
Antenor's eldest son, who stabs Agamemnon mid-forearm from the side, unseen, after
watching him strip his fallen brother Iphidamas — "strong grief came down over his
eyes." Agamemnon shudders but fights on, spears him as he drags the body away by
the foot, and cuts his head off over Iphidamas. The wound is what drives Agamemnon
from the field, and he is still carrying it in Book 19 when he comes late to the
assembly of reconciliation.
*Kin:* son of Antenor; brother of Iphidamas.
*Killed by:* Agamemnon (11.256–261).
**Refs:** 11.248, 11.256, 11.257, 19.53. Books 11, 19.
*See also:* Antenor, Iphidamas, Agamemnon.

**Cos** (Κόως) · PLACE — *Say:* kos
"Cos, city of Eurypylus," an island of the Catalogue's southeast group, sending
thirty ships under Pheidippus and Antiphus, sons of Thessalus and grandsons of
Heracles. It matters chiefly in the back-story Hera and Zeus each rehearse: after
the sack of Troy, Hera laid Zeus's mind asleep and Sleep roused the winds that
carried Heracles far off course to "well-peopled Cos," for which Zeus hunted the
gods through his house and would have flung Sleep into the sea.
**Refs:** 2.677, 14.255, 15.28. Books 2, 14, 15.
*See also:* Heracles, Sleep, Hera, Zeus.

**Cretans** (Κρῆτες) · PEOPLE — *Say:* KREE-tunz
Idomeneus' contingent, eighty ships from Knossos, Gortyn and hundred-citied Crete,
led by him with Meriones. They are named almost always through their leader, in the
formulas "Idomeneus, leader of the Cretans" and "counselor of the bronze-shirted
Cretans" that carry his long exchange with Meriones in Book 13. Helen points them
out from the wall — Idomeneus standing among them "like a god, and the Cretan
captains gathered around him" — and Agamemnon finds them arming in his review of
the army.
*Kin:* led by Idomeneus and Meriones.

**Crete** (Κρήτη) · PLACE
Idomeneus' island, "hundred-citied Crete" of the Catalogue, whose towns — Knossos,
Gortyn, Lyctus, Miletus, chalk-white Lycastus, Phaestus, Rhytium — the poem lists
in full. Helen recalls that Menelaus was often host to Idomeneus "whenever he came
from Crete." Idomeneus gives its royal line to Othryoneus as a boast just before he
kills him: Zeus begot Minos, Minos begot Deucalion, and Deucalion begot Idomeneus,
"lord over many men in wide Crete."
**Refs:** 2.649, 3.233, 13.450, 13.453. Books 2, 3, 13.
*See also:* Idomeneus, Cretans, Minos, Deucalion, Meriones.

**Cronos** (Κρόνος) · GOD — *Say:* KROH-nos
Zeus's father, deposed and shut away: Zeus "put Cronos down beneath the earth and
the barren sea" (14.203–204), and he sits now at the nethermost limits of earth
and sea with Iapetus, in Tartarus, taking no joy in Hyperion the Sun (8.479–481) —
the gods gathered around him below are the Titans Hera swears by (14.274–279). He
never acts. Of the name's 109 occurrences almost all are the patronymic "Cronos'
son," which means **Zeus**; Hera and Poseidon are also his children, and Poseidon's
claim to equal honor rests on it: "Three brothers are we, whom Rhea bore to Cronos"
(15.187).
*Epithets:* "crooked-counseled Cronos" (ἀγκυλομήτεω Κρόνου, 2.205).
*Kin:* with Rhea, father of Zeus, Poseidon, Hades, Hera and Demeter.
**Refs:** 2.205, 4.59, 5.721, 8.479, 14.203, 14.274, 15.187, 15.225 …and
throughout, chiefly as the patronymic of Zeus. Books 1–9, 11–22, 24.
*See also:* Zeus, Rhea, Poseidon, Hera, Hades, Iapetus, Titans.

**Curetes** (Κουρῆτες) · PEOPLE — *Say:* kyoo-REE-teez
The besiegers of Calydon in Phoenix's tale of Meleager, fighting the Aetolians over
the head and hide of the boar Artemis sent against Oeneus' orchards. While Meleager
fought they could not hold their ground outside the wall, though they were many;
when he withdrew in anger at his mother they came on until the chamber itself was
under bombardment. The exemplum Phoenix presses on Achilles turns on their advance.
**Refs:** 9.529, 9.532, 9.549, 9.551, 9.588. Book 9.
*See also:* Meleager, Aetolians, Calydon, Oeneus, Phoenix, Artemis.

**Danaans** (Δαναοί) · PEOPLE — *Say:* DAN-ay-unz *or* DAN-uh-unz
The third of the poem's interchangeable names for the whole Greek host, alongside
Achaeans and Argives; the choice among the three is metrical, not ethnic. It is the
name in Chryses' curse — "may the Danaans pay for my tears by your arrows" (1.42) —
and the one the Catalogue uses for the leaders it is about to list. Agamemnon's
standing address to the army is "Friends, Danaan heroes, squires of Ares."
*Epithets:* "the Danaans of swift colts" (ταχύπωλοι Δαναοί, 4.232).
*Also called:* Achaeans; Argives — the same host under three names. The Myrmidons
and Hellenes are Achilles' own contingent and are not synonyms for the army.
**Refs:** 1.42, 1.56, 2.110, 2.487, 4.232, 4.257, 7.382, 8.161 …and throughout.
Books 1–17, 19–21, 23–24.
*See also:* Achaeans, Argives, Agamemnon.

**Dardania** (Δαρδανίη) · PLACE — *Say:* dar-DAY-nee-uh
The city Dardanus founded on the lower slopes of many-springed Ida, "for holy Ilion
was not yet built on the plain, a city of mortal men" (20.216–218) — the elder
settlement of the Trojan line, named in Aeneas' genealogy. Elsewhere it is a region
paired with Troy as the home of the women who will mourn: Achilles means to make
"some deep-girdled woman of Troy or Dardania" wipe the tears from her cheeks, and
promises the same women will keen night and day around Patroclus' body.
**Refs:** 18.123, 18.339, 20.216. Books 18, 20.
*See also:* Dardanus, Dardanians, Troy, Ilion, Ida, Aeneas.

**Dardanians** (Δάρδανοι) · PEOPLE — *Say:* dar-DAY-nee-unz
Aeneas' people, Trojan-side allies of the house of Dardanus, led by "the good son of
Anchises" with Antenor's sons Archelochus and Acamas. The name lives chiefly in two
fixed address-formulas that recur across the battle books: "Hear from me, Trojans
and Dardanians and allies," and the rallying line "Trojans and Lycians and
Dardanians who fight at close quarters — be men, my friends; remember your rushing
courage" (8.173–174, repeated verbatim five times). The Dardanian gates of Troy are
named for them, and Euphorbus, who strikes Patroclus first, is introduced simply as
"a Dardanian."
*Kin:* led by Aeneas, Anchises' son.
**Refs:** 2.819, 3.456, 7.348, 8.173, 11.286, 15.425, 16.806, 17.184.
Books 2–3, 5, 7–8, 11, 13, 15–17, 22.
*See also:* Aeneas, Anchises, Dardanus, Trojans, Euphorbus.

---

**Dardanus** (Δάρδανος) · MORTAL — *Say:* DAR-duh-nus
Founder of the Trojan line and its eponym: "Dardanus first was begotten by
cloud-gatherer Zeus, and he founded Dardania" (20.215–216), in the days before
Ilion stood on the plain. Aeneas recites the descent to Achilles, and Poseidon
saves Aeneas so "the race of Dardanus not perish seedless" — Zeus having loved
Dardanus beyond all his mortal children, and having come to hate the line of Priam
(20.302–306). He never acts: outside the two genealogies the name is the patronymic
"Dardanus' descendant," used of Priam throughout and of the ancient Ilus at
11.166–372. A second Dardanus, a son of Bias, is killed with his brother Laogonus
by Achilles at 20.460.
*Kin:* son of Zeus; father of Erichthonius; ancestor of Tros, Ilus, Laomedon,
Priam, and through Assaracus of Aeneas.
**Refs:** 3.303, 5.159, 7.366, 11.166, 11.372, 20.215, 20.303, 21.34, 24.171.
Books 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 20–22, 24.
*See also:* Priam, Aeneas, Dardania, Erichthonius, Ilus, Zeus, Bias.

**Dawn** (Ἠώς) · GOD
Personified daybreak, whose formulas divide the poem into days. She rises from her
bed beside illustrious Tithonus "to bring light to the immortals and to mortal men"
(11.1–2), spreads over all the earth in her saffron robe, and rises out of the
streams of Ocean at 19.1 on the day Achilles arms. Her fixed line — "and when
early-born rosy-fingered Dawn appeared" — opens days from Book 1 to the poem's
close. She acts once in her own right, going up to high Olympus to announce the
light to Zeus and the other gods (2.48); and in Book 8 she is what the Trojans wait
for, sitting all night by their fires.
*Epithets:* "rosy-fingered Dawn" (ῥοδοδάκτυλος Ἠώς, 1.477); "early-born"
(ἠριγένεια, 1.477); "Dawn in her saffron robe" (κροκόπεπλος Ἠώς, 8.1);
"fair-throned Dawn" (ἐΰθρονος Ἠώς, 8.565). Note χρυσόθρονος "of the golden
throne" is **Hera's**, not Dawn's — all four occurrences are hers or Zeus's;
CONVENTIONS.md was corrected accordingly on 2026-07-27.
*Kin:* consort of Tithonus, Laomedon's son.
**Refs:** 1.477, 2.48, 8.1, 8.565, 9.240, 11.1, 19.1, 23.109, 24.695, 24.788.
Books 1–2, 6, 8–9, 11, 19, 23–24.
*See also:* Tithonus, Ocean, Sun, Zeus.

---

**Death** (Θάνατος) · GOD
Personified, and twinned with Sleep: "Sleep, the brother of Death" (14.231), "the
twin brothers Sleep and Death" (16.672 = 16.682). Zeus, unable to keep Sarpedon
alive, has Apollo wash the body in the running river, anoint it with ambrosia and
give it to the two of them as escorts, who set him down in wide Lycia for his
brothers and kinsmen to bury — "for that is the honor-right of the dead." Agamemnon
uses the name unpersonified at 4.270 in the oath-breaking scene: "Death and sorrows
hereafter will be theirs."
*Kin:* brother of Sleep.
**Refs:** 4.270, 14.231, 16.454, 16.672, 16.682. Books 4, 14, 16.
*See also:* Sleep, Sarpedon, Apollo, Zeus, Lycia.

---

**Deiphobus** (Δηΐφοβος) · MORTAL — *Say:* dee-IF-oh-bus
A son of Priam and Hecuba, one of the more active Trojan fighters and the shape
Athena wears to kill Hector. He leads the third storming-party at the wall with
Helenus, and in Book 13 he is everywhere: he grieves for Asius and kills Hypsenor
in vengeance, vaunts over him, then strikes Ascalaphus down with a cast meant for
Idomeneus and is wounded in the arm by Meriones over the body, so that Polites has
to lead him out of the fighting. In Book 22 Athena takes his likeness "in body and
unwearying voice," offers to stand with Hector, and vanishes; Hector calls for a
spear and finds no one there — "he is inside the wall, and Athena has tricked me."
*Kin:* son of Priam and Hecuba; brother of Hector, Paris, Helenus and Polites.
*Kills:* Hypsenor (13.410–412); Ascalaphus (13.516–520).
**Refs:** 12.94, 13.156, 13.402, 13.413, 13.516, 13.534, 22.227, 22.294, 24.251.
Books 12–13, 22, 24.
*See also:* Priam, Hector, Athena, Helenus, Ascalaphus, Idomeneus, Meriones.

**Demeter** (Δημήτηρ) · GOD — *Say:* dih-MEE-tur
The grain-goddess, never present and never speaking. She stands behind the poem's
fixed way of saying "mortal": Ajax will not give way "to any man who is mortal and
eats the grain of Demeter" (13.322), and Lycaon's plea to Achilles begins from the
same phrase — "it was at your table first that I ate the grain of Demeter." She has
a winnowing simile at 5.500, where "tawny-haired Demeter in the driving wind divides
the grain from the husks"; a precinct at Pyrasus in the Catalogue; and a place in
Zeus's list of loves, "the lady Demeter of the lovely braids."
*Kin:* daughter of Cronos and Rhea; sister of Zeus.
**Refs:** 2.696, 5.500, 13.322, 14.326, 21.76. Books 2, 5, 13, 14, 21.
*See also:* Zeus, Cronos, Lycaon, Ajax.

---

**Deucalion** (Δευκαλίων) · MORTAL — *Say:* dyoo-KAY-lee-on
Two men. The first is Idomeneus' father, son of Minos and grandson of Zeus, named
only in the line Idomeneus recites over Othryoneus — "Minos got a son, blameless
Deucalion; and Deucalion fathered me, lord over many men in wide Crete" — and
otherwise carried as the patronymic "Deucalion's son," which the poem uses of
Idomeneus at the moments of his greatest exposure (12.117, 17.608) and Meriones
uses to address him. The second is a Trojan Achilles kills in Book 20, pierced
through the arm where the tendons of the elbow join, so that he stands waiting with
his arm hanging heavy and sees death in front of him.
*Kin:* the Cretan is son of Minos and father of Idomeneus.
*Killed by:* the Trojan by Achilles (20.478–483).
**Refs:** 12.117, 13.307, 13.451, 13.452, 17.608, 20.478. Books 12–13, 17, 20.
*See also:* Idomeneus, Minos, Crete, Meriones, Achilles.

---

**Deïpyrus** (Δηΐπυρος) · MORTAL — *Say:* dee-IP-ih-rus
An Achaean captain of the second rank, one of the seven leaders of the night
sentries in Book 9 and one of the men Poseidon rallies and Idomeneus calls to in
Book 13. Helenus kills him there at close quarters, shearing his helmet away with a
great Thracian sword — the helmet knocked spinning off between the fighters' feet
for some Achaean to pick up — and gloomy night comes down over his eyes. His death
is what stirs Menelaus to the duel with Helenus that follows.
*Killed by:* Helenus (13.576–580).
**Refs:** 9.83, 13.92, 13.478, 13.576. Books 9, 13.
*See also:* Helenus, Menelaus, Idomeneus, Meriones, Aphareus.

---

**Diomedes** (Διομήδης) · MORTAL — *Say:* dy-oh-MEE-deez
Tydeus' son, lord of Argos and Tiryns with eighty ships, and the Achaean whose
aristeia fills Book 5. Athena kindles unwearying fire from his helmet and shield,
takes the mist from his eyes so that he may know god from man, and licenses him to
wound Aphrodite — which he does, drawing the ichor from her hand as she carries
Aeneas off — and then mounts his chariot herself and rams his spear into Ares'
belly. He is the poem's model of the young man who takes rebuke in silence:
Agamemnon rakes him at 4.365 and he does not answer, and pays it back in the
assembly of Book 9. He meets Glaucus between the lines, discovers their
grandfathers' guest-friendship, and exchanges armor rather than blows; he goes with
Odysseus on the night raid and kills Dolon and Rhesus; and he is wounded in the foot
by Paris' arrow at 11.377.
*Epithets:* "good at the war-cry" (βοὴν ἀγαθός, 2.563).
*Also called:* Tydeus' son.
*Kin:* son of Tydeus of the Seven against Thebes; his charioteer is Sthenelus,
Capaneus' son.
**Refs:** 2.563, 4.365, 5.1, 5.114, 5.330, 5.855, 6.122, 6.235, 9.31 …and
throughout. Books 2, 4–11, 14, 16, 21, 23.
*See also:* Tydeus, Sthenelus, Athena, Aphrodite, Ares, Glaucus, Odysseus, Dolon.

**Dione** (Διώνη) · GOD — *Say:* dy-OH-nee
Aphrodite's mother in this poem, named only in the scene after Diomedes wounds her.
Aphrodite falls on her knees; Dione gathers her daughter in her arms, strokes her,
and comforts her with a catalogue of gods who have suffered at mortal hands — Ares
bound in a jar by Otus and Ephialtes, Hera and Hades shot by Heracles — before
warning that a man who fights the immortals does not come home to his children.
*Kin:* mother of Aphrodite by Zeus.
**Refs:** 5.370, 5.371, 5.381. Book 5.
*See also:* Aphrodite, Zeus, Diomedes, Ares, Heracles.

---

**Diores** (Διώρης) · MORTAL — *Say:* dy-OR-eez
Two men. The first is an Epeian leader, Amarynceus' son, captain of the third of the
four Elian companies, whose death closes the general fighting of Book 4: a jagged
stone thrown by Peiroos of the Thracians crushes both tendons and the bones beside
his right ankle, and he falls back in the dust stretching out his hands to his
companions, and Peiroos runs up and spears him beside the navel. The second is
Automedon's father, named only in the patronymic "Diores' son" during the fight
over Patroclus' body.
*Kin:* the Epeian is son of Amarynceus; the second is father of Automedon.
*Killed by:* Peiroos (4.517–526).
**Refs:** 2.622, 4.517, 17.429, 17.474. Books 2, 4, 17.
*See also:* Amarynceus, Peiroos, Epeians, Automedon.

---

**Dog** (Κύων) · OTHER
The star Sirius, "the star men call by name the Dog of Orion" — brightest of all,
rising at harvest-time among many stars in the milking-dark of night, "made an evil
sign, and brings great fever down on wretched mortals." It is what Achilles' bronze
looks like to Priam as he comes across the plain (22.26–32), the simile that opens
the last hunt of Hector. The name's other occurrences in the poem are the insult:
Achilles' "Dog's eyes and a deer's heart" to Agamemnon (1.225), the vaunt "Dog!
Again you have run out from under death" that Diomedes and then Achilles throw at
Hector (11.362 = 20.449), and Achilles' one-word refusal of Hector's dying plea
(22.345).
*Also called:* the Dog of Orion.
**Refs:** 1.225, 11.362, 20.449, 22.29, 22.345. Books 1, 11, 20, 22.
*See also:* Orion, Achilles, Hector, Priam.

---

**Dolon** (Δόλων) · MORTAL — *Say:* DOH-lon
The Trojan scout of Book 10, son of the herald Eumedes, "ugly to look at, but
swift-footed," and an only son among five sisters. He volunteers to spy on the ships
and asks as his price the horses and chariot of Achilles; Odysseus and Diomedes let
him run past them and take him, and he is caught green with fear, his teeth
chattering. He tells them everything they ask — the watch, the disposition of the
allies, the new-come Thracians of Rhesus and their horses — and is begging with his
hand out to Diomedes' chin when Diomedes shears through both tendons of his neck
and "the head of the still-speaking man was mingled with the dust." His spoils go on
Odysseus' stern as an offering for Athena.
*Kin:* son of Eumedes the herald.
*Killed by:* Diomedes (10.454–457).
**Refs:** 10.314, 10.374, 10.390, 10.412, 10.426, 10.447, 10.478, 10.571. Book 10.
*See also:* Eumedes, Odysseus, Diomedes, Rhesus, Hector, Athena.

**Dolops** (Δόλοψ) · MORTAL — *Say:* DOH-lops
Two men. The Achaean is Clytius' son, killed by Hector in the rally of Book 11 and
named only in the list of that killing. The Trojan is Lampus' son and Laomedon's
grandson, "a master of the spear" and "the strongest son that Lampus fathered": he
springs on Meges as he strips Croesmus and stabs his shield, but Phyleus' old
corslet from Ephyra turns it; Meges shears the plume off his helmet, and while the
two fight Menelaus comes up unseen from behind and drives a spear through his chest.
Hector rallies his kinsmen over the armor — "do you not see how they swarm over
Dolops's armor?"
*Kin:* the Achaean is son of Clytius; the Trojan is son of Lampus, grandson of
Laomedon, and Hector's kinsman.
*Killed by:* the Achaean by Hector (11.302); the Trojan by Menelaus (15.540–543).
**Refs:** 11.302, 15.525, 15.536, 15.555. Books 11, 15.
*See also:* Clytius, Lampus, Meges, Menelaus, Hector, Melanippus.

---

**Dream** (Ὄνειρος) · GOD
The messenger Zeus sends to deceive Agamemnon — "baneful Dream" — with the false
promise that he may take Troy now, because the immortals are no longer divided and
Hera has bent them by her begging. It stands above Agamemnon's head in the likeness
of Nestor, "whom Agamemnon honored most of the elders," delivers the word exactly as
charged, and flies away; Agamemnon reports it to the council as "a divine Dream" and
acts on it, and the army's near-flight to the ships follows.
**Refs:** 2.6, 2.8, 2.16, 2.22, 2.56, 2.70. Book 2.
*See also:* Zeus, Agamemnon, Nestor, Blindness.

---

**Earth** (Γαῖα) · GOD
Invoked, never acting: one of the standing witnesses of the poem's oaths. Two lambs
are brought "for Earth and Sun" at the truce of Book 3, and Priam's oath-prayer
calls on "Rivers, and Earth, and you two below who punish the dead." Hera swears by
Earth, wide Heaven and the down-flowing water of Styx that she has not moved
Poseidon; Agamemnon swears by Zeus, Earth, the Sun and the Erinyes that he never
touched Briseis. The related "Earth-shaker" is a fixed epithet of Poseidon, not this
entity.
**Refs:** 3.104, 3.278, 15.36, 19.259, 20.310, 21.462. Books 3, 15, 19–21.
*See also:* Sun, Zeus, Styx, Erinyes, Poseidon, Hera.

**Echius** (Ἐχίος) · MORTAL — *Say:* EK-ee-us
Three men. One is a father only: "Mecisteus, Echius' son," one of the two loyal
companions who twice stoop under a wounded man and carry him groaning to the ships
(8.333 = 13.422). An Achaean of the name is killed by Polites in the rout of Book
15, in the same onset in which Polydamas kills that Mecisteus. A third is a Trojan,
one of nine men Patroclus brings down in heaps at 16.415–418.
*Kin:* the first is father of Mecisteus.
*Killed by:* the Achaean by Polites (15.339); the Trojan by Patroclus (16.416).
**Refs:** 8.333, 13.422, 15.339, 16.416. Books 8, 13, 15, 16.
*See also:* Mecisteus, Polites, Polydamas, Patroclus.

**Elis** (Ἦλις) · PLACE — *Say:* EE-lis
"Brilliant Elis" in the western Peloponnese, the Epeians' country in the Catalogue,
bounded by Hyrmine, Myrsinus at the land's edge, the Olenian rock and Alesium, and
sending forty ships under four leaders; the Echinae islands lie "across the sea
facing Elis." It is the setting of Nestor's long reminiscence in Book 11: the
cattle-raid in which he killed Itymoneus, the debt owed the Pylians "in brilliant
Elis," and Augeias' seizure of the four prize-winning horses that had come there for
the games.
**Refs:** 2.615, 2.626, 11.673, 11.686, 11.698. Books 2, 11.
*See also:* Epeians, Nestor, Augeias, Buprasium, Meges.

**Ennomus** (Ἔννομος) · MORTAL — *Say:* EN-oh-mus
"Ennomus the bird-reader," who leads the Mysians with Chromis in the Catalogue,
where the poem adds the notice that his birds did not save him from black doom: he
was broken under the hands of Achilles in the river, with the other Trojans (2.858–
861). He is still among the allied captains Hector harangues at 17.218, so the
Catalogue's notice runs ahead of the narrative. A second man of the name is killed
by Odysseus with Thoon in Book 11.
*Killed by:* Achilles, in the Scamander (2.860–861); the second by Odysseus
(11.422).
**Refs:** 2.858, 11.422, 17.218. Books 2, 11, 17.
*See also:* Chromis, Mysians, Achilles, Scamander, Odysseus, Thoon.

**Enops** (Ἔνοψ) · MORTAL — *Say:* EE-nops
A name borne by three fathers, none of whom appears. One is the Trojan who herded by
the banks of the Satnioeis, where a blameless naiad nymph bore him Satnius, killed
by Ajax son of Oïleus (14.444–446). Another is father of the Trojan Thestor, whom
Patroclus hooks out of his chariot by the jaw like a fish (16.401). The third is
father of Clytomedes, the boxer Nestor beat at the funeral games of Amarynceus.
*Kin:* father, severally, of Satnius, Thestor and Clytomedes.
**Refs:** 14.444, 14.445, 16.401, 23.634. Books 14, 16, 23.
*See also:* Satnius, Thestor, Clytomedes, Nestor, Patroclus, Satnioeis.

**Epeians** (Ἐπειοί) · PEOPLE — *Say:* eh-PEE-unz
The people of Elis and Buprasium, forty ships under four leaders — Amphimachus and
Thalpius, Diores, and Polyxeinus — and "the shining Epeians" who hold part of the
line at the ships with the Locrians and Phthians under Meges and Amphion. Their
larger role is retrospective: Nestor's Book 11 reminiscence is entirely about them,
the debt they owed the Pylians, the Molione brothers, and the battle by the
Alpheius; and the funeral games they held for lord Amarynceus at Buprasium are the
measure Nestor sets against Patroclus'.
*Kin:* led by Amphimachus, Thalpius, Diores and Polyxeinus.
**Refs:** 2.619, 4.538, 11.688, 11.694, 11.737, 13.686, 15.519, 23.630.
Books 2, 4, 11, 13, 15, 23.
*See also:* Elis, Nestor, Diores, Meges, Amarynceus, Buprasium.

**Epeius** (Ἐπειός) · MORTAL — *Say:* eh-PEE-us
Panopeus' son, "huge and strong, skilled in fist-fighting," who claims the boxing
prize before the fight and gets it: "I claim to be the best. Is it not enough that I
fall short in battle?" He drops Euryalus with one blow to the cheek — the man leaps
at it like a fish under the North Wind and goes down — then sets him upright again
with his own hands. In the weight-throw he takes the lump of pig-iron first, whirls,
lets it fly, and the Achaeans laugh at him.
*Kin:* son of Panopeus.
**Refs:** 23.665, 23.689, 23.694, 23.838, 23.839. Book 23.
*See also:* Panopeus, Euryalus, Achilles, Polypoetes, Leonteus.

**Epistrophus** (Ἐπίστροφος) · MORTAL — *Say:* ee-PIS-troh-fus
Three men, all named in Book 2 and none of them again. Two are Catalogue leaders:
the Phocian, who leads forty ships with his brother Schedius, sons of Iphitus
Naubolus' son; and the Halizonian, who leads with Odius "from far away, from Alybe,
where silver has its birthplace." The third is one of the "spear-mad sons of lord
Euenus" whom Achilles cut down with Mynes at the sack of Lyrnessus, where he took
Briseis.
*Kin:* the Phocian is son of Iphitus, brother of Schedius; the third is son of
Euenus, brother of Mynes.
*Killed by:* the third by Achilles (2.692).
**Refs:** 2.517, 2.692, 2.856. Book 2.
*See also:* Schedius, Odius, Phocians, Halizones, Mynes, Briseis, Lyrnessus.

**Ereuthalion** (Ἐρευθαλίων) · MORTAL — *Say:* er-yoo-THAY-lee-on
The champion young Nestor killed, and the standing proof of what Nestor once was.
He wore the armor Ares gave Areithous the Club-man, which Lycurgus took by craft in
a narrow lane and gave in old age to Ereuthalion, his aide; wearing it he challenged
all the Pylian best by the walls of Pheia, and they trembled and no man dared until
Nestor, youngest-born of all, went out and killed him. "The tallest and the
strongest man I ever killed: a great sprawl of him lay there, this way and that."
Nestor tells it twice, both times to shame men into fighting.
*Killed by:* Nestor (7.153–156).
**Refs:** 4.319, 7.136, 7.149, 7.150. Books 4, 7.
*See also:* Nestor, Areithous, Lycurgus, Arcadians, Hector.

**Erinyes** (Ἐρινύες) · GOD — *Say:* ee-RIN-ee-eez
The avenging powers a curse calls up, and the poem's guarantors of oaths and of due
order. Amyntor's curse on Phoenix summons "the hateful Erinyes"; Althaea, beating
the earth and calling on Hades and Persephone for her son's death, is heard "out of
Erebus" by an Erinys "who walks in mist." Iris tells Poseidon that "the Erinyes
always side with the elder-born," and Agamemnon lays his blindness on Zeus, Fate,
and the Erinys who walks in mist. They are witnesses under the earth in his oath at
19.259, and they stop the mouth of Achilles' speaking horse (19.418).
*Epithets:* "who walks in mist" (ἠεροφοῖτις, 9.571).
**Refs:** 9.454, 9.571, 15.204, 19.87, 19.259, 19.418, 21.412.
Books 9, 15, 19, 21.
*See also:* Phoenix, Amyntor, Althaea, Meleager, Agamemnon, Blindness, Xanthus.

**Euaemon** (Εὐαίμων) · MORTAL — *Say:* yoo-EE-mon
Eurypylus' father, who never appears: in all seven occurrences the name is the
patronymic, and almost always in the fixed phrase "Eurypylus, Euaemon's shining
son" — which carries him through the Catalogue, his killing of Hypsenor, the
volunteering for the duel with Hector, and finally his own wounding, "Euaemon's
Zeus-sprung son, hit in the thigh by the arrow."
*Kin:* father of Eurypylus.
**Refs:** 2.736, 5.76, 5.79, 7.167, 8.265, 11.575, 11.810. Books 2, 5, 7, 8, 11.
*See also:* Eurypylus, Hypsenor, Patroclus.

**Eumedes** (Εὐμήδης) · MORTAL — *Say:* yoo-MEE-deez
Dolon's father, "the sacred herald — rich in gold and rich in bronze," named only to
place his son: the wealth is what makes Dolon's offer of ransom credible when
Odysseus and Diomedes take him. The name appears three times in Book 10 and nowhere
else, twice in the formula "Dolon, Eumedes' son" that introduces the scout's two
truthful answers.
*Kin:* father of Dolon.
**Refs:** 10.314, 10.412, 10.426. Book 10.
*See also:* Dolon, Odysseus, Diomedes.

**Eumelus** (Εὔμηλος) · MORTAL — *Say:* yoo-MEE-lus
Leader of eleven ships from Pherae and Iolcus, and owner of the best horses in the
host: the Muse-question at 2.761–767 answers that the finest were "the mares of
Pheres' line, which Eumelus drove," matched in coat and years, reared by Apollo in
Pereia. He is "supreme in horsemanship" and rises first for the chariot race in
Book 23, but Athena — retaliating for Apollo's sabotage of Diomedes — shatters his
yoke, and he is thrown and comes in last, dragging his chariot. Achilles pities
him and proposes second prize; Antilochus objects, and Eumelus is given
Asteropaeus' corslet instead.
*Also called:* Admetus' son.
*Kin:* son of Admetus and Alcestis, "brilliant among women, loveliest of the
daughters of Pelias" (2.715); grandson of Pheres.
**Refs:** 2.714, 2.764, 23.288, 23.354, 23.381, 23.481, 23.558, 23.565. Books 2,
23.
*See also:* Admetus, Alcestis, Athena, Diomedes, Antilochus, Asteropaeus.

**Euphorbus** (Εὔφορβος) · MORTAL — *Say:* yoo-FOR-bus
The Dardanian who strikes Patroclus first — from behind, between the shoulders,
after Apollo has already dazed him — then pulls his spear free and runs back into
the crowd rather than face even an unarmed man. The poem gives him a resumé in the
same breath: he outshone all men of his age at the spear, at horsemanship and in
the footrace, and had brought down twenty men his first time out with a chariot.
Dying, Patroclus names him second of his three killers, after Apollo. In Book 17
he claims the body from Menelaus and is speared through the throat; his blood-soaked
hair, "hair like the Graces'," braided in gold and silver, gets the poem's olive-sapling
simile.
*Also called:* Panthous' son — the patronymic also serves Polydamas and Hyperenor.
*Kin:* son of Panthous.
*Kills:* wounds Patroclus (16.806–815).
*Killed by:* Menelaus (17.45–60).
**Refs:** 16.808, 16.850, 17.47, 17.59, 17.81. Books 16–17.
*See also:* Patroclus, Menelaus, Panthous, Polydamas, Apollo, Hector, Graces.

---

**Euryalus** (Εὐρύαλος) · MORTAL — *Say:* yoo-RY-uh-lus
Third captain of the Argive contingent under Diomedes, behind Diomedes himself and
Sthenelus, "a godlike man." He kills four Trojans in the Book 6 rout, among them
the twins Aesepus and Pedasus, whom the water-nymph Abarbarea bore to Bucolion. In
Book 23 he alone stands up to Epeius at boxing — Diomedes girds him and talks him
up — and is knocked flat with a blow to the cheek, led away through the gathering
with his feet trailing, spitting thick blood, his wits astray.
*Kin:* son of Mecisteus, son of Talaus. His father once came to Thebes for the
burial of fallen Oedipus and beat all the sons of Cadmus (23.679–680).
*Kills:* Dresus, Opheltius, Aesepus and Pedasus (6.20–28).
**Refs:** 2.565, 6.20, 23.677. Books 2, 6, 23.
*See also:* Diomedes, Sthenelus, Mecisteus, Epeius, Bucolion.

---

**Eurypylus** (Εὐρύπυλος) · MORTAL — *Say:* yoo-RIP-ih-lus
Thessalian leader of forty ships from Ormenion and Hypereia, and one of the nine
who stand up to answer Hector's challenge in Book 7. He kills Hypsenor the
Scamander-priest's son in Book 5 and Melanthius in Book 6, and in Book 11, coming
to the rescue of the hard-pressed Ajax, is shot through the right thigh by Paris.
His wound is the hinge of the plot: Patroclus, sent by Achilles to ask after the
wounded, meets him limping from the field, and though on an errand he will not
abandon him — he cuts the arrow out, washes the wound and lays on a root Chiron
taught Achilles. Patroclus is still sitting in his hut at 15.390, delighting him
with talk, when the Trojans storm the wall; the delay is the last thing between him
and his own death. A different Eurypylus is the hero of Cos (2.677).
*Also called:* Euaemon's son — the recurring line is "Euaemon's shining son."
*Kin:* son of Euaemon.
*Kills:* Hypsenor (5.76–83); Melanthius (6.36); Apisaon (11.578).
**Refs:** 2.736, 5.76, 6.36, 7.167, 11.583, 11.809, 12.2, 15.392, 16.27. Books 2,
5–8, 11–12, 15–16.
*See also:* Patroclus, Paris, Ajax, Machaon, Achilles.

---

**Eurystheus** (Εὐρυσθεύς) · MORTAL — *Say:* yoo-RIS-thyoos
The king Heracles served, present only in the back-story. Athena complains at
8.362–369 that Zeus forgets how often she was sent to rescue his son "worn to
breaking under Eurystheus's labors" — among them the descent to Hades to fetch the
hound. His birth is the point of Agamemnon's Blindness-parable in Book 19: Hera,
trapping Zeus in an oath, hurried this child into the light before his month and
held back Alcmene's labor, so that the rule Zeus meant for Heracles fell to
Eurystheus, and Zeus groaned ever after to see his son "doing shameful labor under
Eurystheus's tasks." His herald Copreus carried the orders (15.639–640).
*Kin:* son of Sthenelus, son of Perseus.
**Refs:** 8.363, 8.367, 15.640, 19.123, 19.133. Books 8, 15, 19.
*See also:* Heracles, Hera, Alcmene, Blindness, Sthenelus, Perseus, Copreus.

---

**Eëtion** (Ἠετίων) · MORTAL — *Say:* ay-EE-tee-on
King of Thebe-under-Placus, lord over the Cilician men, and Andromache's father.
Achilles sacked his city before the poem opens — the raid that also took Chryseis —
and killed him there, but "did not strip him: his heart stood in awe of that,"
burning him in his armor and heaping a mound over which the mountain nymphs planted
elms. Achilles killed all seven of his sons in one day and ransomed his wife back.
The spoils of Thebe run through the poem: the lyre Achilles is singing to when the
embassy arrives, the horse Pedasus, and the lump of self-smelted iron he sets up as
a prize at Patroclus' games, "that the huge strength of Eëtion used to throw." Two
other men bear the name: Eëtion of Imbros, who ransomed Lycaon (21.43), and the
father of Podes, Hector's feast-companion (17.575).
*Kin:* father of Andromache and of seven sons; father-in-law of Hector.
*Killed by:* Achilles (6.414–420).
**Refs:** 1.366, 6.396, 6.416, 8.187, 9.188, 16.153, 21.43, 22.472, 23.827. Books
1, 6, 8–9, 16–17, 21–23.
*See also:* Andromache, Achilles, Hector, Thebe, Placus, Chryseis, Podes.

---

**Fate** (Μοῖρα) · GOD
Personified only where the translation capitalizes her; elsewhere μοῖρα is the
common noun "fate," and the death-line formula μοῖρα κραταιή is rendered "fate the
overpowering." She walks the shield of Achilles beside Strife and Tumult, "deadly
Fate, holding one man fresh-wounded and alive, another unwounded, another dead,
dragging him by the feet through the fighting," her cloak red with the blood of men
(18.535–538). Elsewhere she is a spinner: what she "spun for him with her thread at
his birth" is said of Achilles by Zeus (20.127–128) and of Hector by Hecuba
(24.209–210), in the same wording. Agamemnon names her with Zeus and the Erinys as
the causes of his blindness, and the horse Xanthus names "a great god, and strong
Fate" as the killers of Patroclus.
*See also:* Strife, Blindness, Erinys, Xanthus, Zeus, Hecuba.

---

**Gargaron** (Γάργαρον) · PLACE — *Say:* GAR-guh-ron
The peak of Ida where Zeus's precinct and smoking altar are, and the seat from
which he watches the war: he halts his horses there at 8.48–52, pours mist around
them, and sits "looking out at the Trojans' city and the ships of the Achaeans."
It is where Hera comes to him arrayed in the kestos, and where he sleeps after,
"broken by sleep and love, his wife in his arms," while Sleep runs to Poseidon
with the news; and where Iris and Apollo find him wreathed in a fragrant cloud when
he wakes.
**Refs:** 8.48, 14.292, 14.352, 15.152. Books 8, 14–15.
*See also:* Ida, Zeus, Hera, Sleep, Iris.

---

**Glaucus** (Γλαῦκος) · MORTAL — *Say:* GLAW-kus
Co-leader of the Lycians with Sarpedon, and one of the two men — with Asteropaeus —
whom Sarpedon picks out as best of the allies after himself. In Book 6 he meets
Diomedes between the lines and answers the challenge with the poem's most famous
image, "As the generation of leaves, so is the generation of men," then recites the
Bellerophon story; the two discover their grandfathers' guest-friendship, refuse to
fight, and exchange armor — at which "Zeus took Glaucus's wits away," gold for
bronze, a hundred oxen's worth for nine. Teucer's arrow through his bared arm takes
him out of the fight at the wall; Apollo heals it at his prayer in Book 16 so that
he can rally the Lycians over Sarpedon's body. In Book 17 he rounds on Hector for
letting Sarpedon's corpse go and threatens to take the Lycians home. His
great-grandfather Glaucus, son of Sisyphus, bears the same name (6.154).
*Kin:* son of Hippolochus; grandson of Bellerophon; cousin of Sarpedon through
Laodameia.
*Kills:* Iphinous (7.13–16); Bathycles (16.593–598).
**Refs:** 2.876, 6.119, 6.234, 7.13, 12.309, 12.387, 16.492, 16.530, 17.140.
Books 2, 6–7, 12, 14, 16–17.
*See also:* Sarpedon, Diomedes, Hippolochus, Bellerophon, Teucer, Apollo, Hector.

---

**Graces** (Χάριτες) · GOD
Divine craftswomen and, in Book 14, marriageable persons. They wove the ambrosial
robe Aphrodite is wearing when Diomedes cuts her hand (5.338). Hera buys Sleep's
help against Zeus by offering him "one of the younger Graces" to marry, and he names
his price: Pasithea, "whom I myself have longed for all my days." Their name is also
the poem's measure of male beauty — Euphorbus dies with "hair like the Graces'."
*Also called:* the younger Graces (14.267, 14.275); Pasithea is the one named.
**Refs:** 5.338, 14.267, 14.275, 17.51. Books 5, 14, 17.
*See also:* Aphrodite, Hera, Sleep, Pasithea, Euphorbus.

---

**Hades** (Ἅιδης) · GOD — *Say:* HAY-deez
Lord of the dead, and by metonymy the underworld itself — most of the poem's
forty-six occurrences are the destination in phrases like "went down to the house of
Hades," which is where the wrath of the proem sends "many strong souls of heroes"
in the fourth line. As a person he is the third of Cronos' sons: Poseidon's account
of the lots at 15.187–193 gives Zeus the wide heaven, himself the grey sea, and
"Hades the misty dark," with earth and Olympus common to all three. Heracles shot
him at Pylos "among the dead, and gave him over to pain" (5.395–397); Athena wears
his helmet to hide from Ares (5.845); and in the theomachy he leaps screaming from
his throne, afraid Poseidon will crack the earth open and expose his "terrible,
mouldering houses, which the very gods abhor" (20.61–65). Achilles learns from
Patroclus' ghost that there is a soul and an image in his house "but the wits are
not in it at all."
*Epithets:* "the gate-closer" (πυλάρτης, 8.367); "of the famed colts"
(κλυτόπωλος, 5.654).
*Also called:* Aïdoneus (5.190, 20.61).
*Kin:* son of Cronos and Rhea; brother of Zeus, Poseidon and Hera.
**Refs:** 1.3, 5.395, 5.654, 5.845, 8.367, 9.158, 15.188, 15.191, 16.856, 20.294,
22.213, 23.103 …and throughout. Books 1, 3, 5–9, 11, 13–16, 20–24.
*See also:* Zeus, Poseidon, Persephone, Erebus, Patroclus, Heracles.

---

**Haemon** (Αἵμων) · MORTAL — *Say:* HEE-mon
Three men. The first is a Pylian, "the lord Haemon," one of the five captains
Nestor is arraying his companions around when Agamemnon reaches him in Book 4. The
second is a Cadmean, father of Maeon — one of the two leaders of the fifty-man
ambush the Thebans set for Tydeus, and the only man Tydeus let go home alive. The
third is named once as Alcimedon's grandfather, father of Laerces, among the
Myrmidons.
**Refs:** 4.296, 4.394, 17.467. Books 4, 17.
*See also:* Nestor, Maeon, Tydeus, Alcimedon, Laerces.

---

**Hebe** (Ἥβη) · GOD — *Say:* HEE-bee
Daughter of Zeus and Hera, and the gods' cup-bearer: the poem's first sight of
Olympus at the opening of Book 4 has her pouring nectar among the seated gods while
they look out at the city of the Trojans. She also works as Hera's groom, setting
the bronze eight-spoked wheels on the chariot when her mother goes to war (5.722),
and it is she who bathes Ares and dresses him in graceful clothing after Paiëon has
healed his belly-wound.
*Epithets:* "the lady Hebe" (πότνια Ἥβη, 4.2).
*Kin:* daughter of Zeus and Hera; sister of Ares.
**Refs:** 4.2, 5.722, 5.905. Books 4–5.
*See also:* Hera, Zeus, Ares, Paiëon.

---

**Hector** (Ἕκτωρ) · MORTAL
Priam's eldest son, Troy's champion, and the man on whom the city rests — "for
Hector alone was the saving of Ilion" (6.403). He is the poem's second protagonist:
he duels Ajax to a standstill in Book 7, breaks the Achaean gate with a stone in
Book 12, fires the ships in Book 15, and strips Achilles' armor from Patroclus'
body, the act that brings Achilles back and destroys him. His domestic scene with
Andromache and the infant Astyanax at the Scaean Gates (6.390–496) is set against
his own certainty that Ilion will fall; he twice rejects Polydamas' counsel to
withdraw, the second time fatally. In Book 22 he waits alone outside the wall,
runs three times around the city, is tricked by Athena in Deiphobus' shape, and
takes Achilles' spear through the gullet. Achilles drags his body for twelve days
until Priam ransoms it, and the poem closes on his burial.
*Epithets:* "of the flashing helmet" (κορυθαίολος, 2.816); "glorious" (φαίδιμος,
4.505); "man-slaughtering" (ἀνδροφόνος, 1.242 — given last to his own head at
24.724); "breaker of horses" (ἱππόδαμος, 7.38), rendered "tamer of horses" in the
line that ends the poem (22.161, 24.804).
*Also called:* Priam's son — the default referent of the patronymic, though it also
serves Paris, Helenus, Deiphobus, Polydorus, Lycaon and the Book 24 roll-call.
*Kin:* son of Priam and Hecuba; husband of Andromache, daughter of Eëtion; father
of Astyanax; brother of Paris, Helenus, Deiphobus, Polydorus, Lycaon, Cassandra and
Laodice.
*Kills:* Patroclus (16.818–863), with Apollo and Euphorbus striking before him;
Amphimachus, Schedius, Asaeus, Autonous and many more.
*Killed by:* Achilles (22.326–330).
**Refs:** 1.242, 2.816, 3.38, 6.390, 6.440, 7.38, 12.445, 16.818, 18.284, 22.326,
24.724, 24.804 …and throughout. Books 1–24.
*See also:* Achilles, Priam, Andromache, Astyanax, Polydamas, Patroclus, Ajax,
Apollo, Deiphobus.

**Hecuba** (Ἑκάβη) · MORTAL — *Say:* HEK-yoo-buh
Priam's wife and Hector's mother. She meets Hector at the palace in Book 6 and
offers him wine, then leads the older women to lay the loveliest robe of her Sidonian
store on Athena's knees — a prayer Athena refuses. On the wall at 22.79–89 she bares
her breast and begs him to come inside; she leads the lament over his body twice,
at the wall and at the bier, where she says his flesh is unmarked and "dear to the
gods you were while you lived with me." In Book 24 she opposes Priam's journey to
the ships outright, calling Achilles savage and faithless and wishing she could eat
his liver, then yields and makes her husband pour a libation before he goes.
*Epithets:* "gentle-giving" (ἠπιόδωρος, 6.251).
*Kin:* daughter of Dymas, sister of Asius (16.718); wife of Priam; mother of Hector,
Paris, Deiphobus, Helenus, Laodice, Cassandra and the rest.
**Refs:** 6.293, 6.451, 16.718, 22.234, 22.430, 24.193, 24.283, 24.747. Books 6,
16, 22, 24.
*See also:* Priam, Hector, Andromache, Athena, Achilles, Dymas, Asius.

**Helen** (Ἑλένη) · MORTAL — *Say:* HEL-un
Menelaus' wife, taken to Troy by Paris, and the cause the whole war is fought over —
"Argive Helen, for whose sake so many Achaeans have perished at Troy." The poem
handles her almost entirely through her own speech, and every scene of it is
self-reproach: she names herself "dog-face" to Priam on the wall while identifying
the Achaean captains for him, calls herself "the dog, the evil-scheming horror" to
Hector, and wishes a storm had carried her off the day she was born. Aphrodite
compels her to Paris' bed in Book 3 with a threat, and she goes in silence. She
speaks last of the three women who lament Hector, mourning the only man in Troy who
never gave her a hard word: "there is no one else now in wide Troy gentle to me, or
kind: they all shudder at me."
*Epithets:* "of the trailing robe" (τανύπεπλος, 3.228).
*Kin:* daughter of Zeus (3.418, "Helen sprung of Zeus"); wife of Menelaus; taken by
Paris; she names a daughter left behind at Sparta (3.175).
**Refs:** 2.161, 3.121, 3.171, 3.228, 3.418, 6.343, 7.350, 24.761. Books 2–4, 6–9,
11, 13, 19, 22, 24.
*See also:* Paris, Menelaus, Priam, Hector, Aphrodite, Antenor, Laodice.

**Helenus** (Ἕλενος) · MORTAL — *Say:* HEL-uh-nus
Priam's son, "far the best of bird-readers," whose counsel twice sets the plot
moving: at 6.75–101 he sends Hector back into the city to have Hecuba petition
Athena, which produces the Andromache scene; and at 7.44–53 he hears "the voice of
the gods who are forever," relays that it is not yet Hector's fate to die, and
prompts the challenge that brings on the duel with Ajax. He also fights — he leads
one of the storming-parties at the wall with Deiphobus, kills Deïpyrus with a
Thracian sword, and is shot in the bow-hand by Menelaus and carried out. Priam
curses him by name among the nine surviving sons in Book 24. A different Helenus,
son of Oenops, is one of the Achaeans Hector and Ares kill at 5.707.
*Kin:* son of Priam and Hecuba; brother of Hector, Paris and Deiphobus.
*Kills:* Deïpyrus (13.576–580).
**Refs:** 5.707, 6.75, 7.44, 12.94, 13.576, 13.582, 24.249. Books 5–7, 12–13, 24.
*See also:* Priam, Hector, Deiphobus, Menelaus, Cassandra, Deïpyrus.

**Hellas** (Ἑλλάς) · PLACE — *Say:* HEL-us
Not yet a name for Greece: in this poem it is a district of Phthia, listed in the
Catalogue with Alos, Alope, Trachis and Phthia as the country of Achilles'
contingent, and fixed as "Hellas of the beautiful women." Achilles offers it as his
marriage-market — "There are Achaean girls enough through Hellas and through Phthia"
— and Phoenix fled from it to Peleus after the quarrel with his father Amyntor,
crossing "Hellas of the wide spaces." Bathycles, the richest of the Myrmidons, had
his home there.
**Refs:** 2.683, 9.395, 9.447, 9.478, 16.595. Books 2, 9, 16.
*See also:* Hellenes, Phthia, Achilles, Phoenix, Myrmidons, Bathycles.

---

**Hellenes** (Ἕλληνες) · PEOPLE — *Say:* HEL-eenz
Still a narrow regional term, twice only. At 2.684 it is one of the three names of
Achilles' own contingent — "and they were called Myrmidons, and Hellenes, and
Achaeans" — and at 2.530 the lesser Ajax "with the spear surpassed all Hellenes and
Achaeans," a pairing that keeps the two distinct. It is not a name for the whole
host; that is Achaeans, Argives, Danaans.
**Refs:** 2.530, 2.684. Book 2.
*See also:* Hellas, Myrmidons, Achaeans, Achilles, Ajax son of Oïleus.

**Hellespont** (Ἑλλήσποντος) · PLACE — *Say:* HEL-uh-spont
The strait beside the Achaean camp, always "strong-flowing" or "wide" or
"boundless," and the poem's standing figure for the far edge of the beachhead. Nestor
proposes the barrow of the unnamed dead be heaped "beside the wide Hellespont" so
that men sailing by in later ages will see it; Achilles threatens to load his ships
and be seen sailing it at earliest light; Poseidon's wall-destroying flood in Book
12 smooths the beach "along the strong-flowing Hellespont." It bounds the Thracian
contingent in the Catalogue and, in Priam's mind, the whole extent of his former
wealth.
**Refs:** 2.845, 7.86, 9.360, 12.30, 15.233, 17.432, 18.150, 23.2, 24.346, 24.545.
Books 2, 7, 9, 12, 15, 17–18, 23–24.
*See also:* Troy, Ida, Poseidon, Nestor, Achilles.

**Hephaestus** (Ἥφαιστος) · GOD — *Say:* hih-FES-tus *or* hih-FEE-stus
The smith-god, lamed and comic in Book 1 — where he bustles round the hall pouring
nectar to break up his parents' quarrel, and tells how Zeus once caught him by the
foot and flung him from the threshold of heaven to fall all day and land on Lemnos —
and formidable everywhere after. He made Agamemnon's sceptre and the houses of the
gods; his name stands by metonymy for fire ("the flame of Hephaestus," 2.426). His
great work is the armor and the shield of Achilles, forged at Thetis' asking, with
the earth, heaven, sea, two cities, the plowing and harvest, the vineyard, the herd
and the lions, the dancing-floor and the river of Ocean on it (18.478–608). In Book
21 Hera sets him on the Scamander: he burns the plain, then the river itself, until
the water boils in its channel and the god begs off.
*Epithets:* "crook-footed" (ἀμφιγυήεις, 1.607); "famed for craft" (κλυτότεχνης,
1.571); "famed" (περικλυτός, 1.607).
*Kin:* son of Hera (1.572, 21.330); husband of Charis (18.382).
**Refs:** 1.571, 1.600, 2.101, 2.426, 14.239, 18.137, 18.369, 18.473, 20.36,
21.342, 21.367, 21.381. Books 1–2, 5, 8–9, 14–15, 17–23.
*See also:* Hera, Thetis, Achilles, Charis, Scamander, Zeus, Lemnos.

---

**Hera** (Ἥρη) · GOD — *Say:* HEER-uh *or* HAIR-uh
Zeus's wife and sister, and the Achaeans' most relentless partisan — she will trade
Argos, Sparta and Mycenae, the three cities dearest to her, for the sack of Troy
(4.51–56), and Zeus says she would eat Priam and his sons raw if she could get at
them. She sets the poem going by putting the assembly into Achilles' mind (1.55),
and her Book 14 deception is its central act of divine mischief: she borrows the
kestos from Aphrodite, buys Sleep with the promise of Pasithea, and takes Zeus on
Gargaron so that Poseidon can turn the battle while he sleeps. Zeus's fury when he
wakes — and his reminder of the time he hung her from heaven with anvils on her
feet — does not change her; she extorts his consent for Troy's fall in Book 4 and
again in Book 21, and sends Hephaestus against the river to save Achilles.
*Epithets:* "white-armed" (λευκώλενος, 1.55); "ox-eyed lady Hera" (βοῶπις πότνια
Ἥρη, 1.551); "eldest goddess" (πρέσβα θεά, 5.721); "of the golden throne"
(χρυσόθρονος, 1.611).
*Kin:* daughter of Cronos and Rhea; sister and wife of Zeus; sister of Poseidon and
Hades; mother of Ares, Hebe and Hephaestus.
**Refs:** 1.55, 1.400, 1.536, 4.5, 4.50, 5.721, 8.198, 14.159, 14.292, 15.5, 19.407
…and throughout. Books 1–2, 4–5, 7–11, 13–16, 18–21, 24.
*See also:* Zeus, Athena, Sleep, Aphrodite, Hephaestus, Poseidon, Hebe, Ares.

**Heracles** (Ἡρακλῆς) · MORTAL — *Say:* HER-uh-kleez
Always in the past tense, and always as the measure of a generation stronger than
this one. His Troy is the earlier sack: he came for the horses of Laomedon "with
only six ships and fewer men, and sacked the city of Ilion, and widowed her
streets," and Laomedon cheated him of the horses (5.638–651). Sleep and Hera between
them wrecked his homecoming and blew him to Cos, for which Zeus hurled gods through
the house; Poseidon shows Aeneas the wall the Trojans built him against the
sea-monster. Achilles takes him as the precedent for his own death — "not even the
might of Heracles escaped his doom" — and Agamemnon takes his birth as the great
instance of Blindness, when Hera's trick handed his rule to Eurystheus. He wounded
Hades at Pylos, and he battered Nestor's Pylos so hard that eleven of Neleus' twelve
sons were killed.
*Epithets:* "the might of Heracles" (βίη Ἡρακληείη, 2.658) — the periphrasis is
kept, and is the usual form of the name.
*Kin:* son of Zeus and Alcmene; father of Tlepolemus by Astyocheia and of Thessalus.
**Refs:** 2.658, 2.666, 5.638, 11.690, 14.255, 15.25, 18.117, 19.98, 20.145. Books
2, 5, 11, 14–15, 18–20.
*See also:* Zeus, Hera, Alcmene, Eurystheus, Laomedon, Tlepolemus, Sarpedon, Nestor.

**Hermes** (Ἑρμείας) · GOD — *Say:* HUR-meez
The gods' messenger and escort. He passes the sceptre from Zeus to Pelops in the
Book 2 genealogy, steals Ares out of the bronze jar where the sons of Aloeus had
him bound, fathers Eudorus on Polymele, and is paired against Leto in the theomachy —
where he declines to fight, saying it is a hard thing to trade blows with the wives
of Zeus. His great scene is Book 24: Zeus sends him to bring Priam through the
Achaean camp unseen, and he comes as a young man, takes the reins, drugs the
gate-keepers, opens the gate, identifies himself at Achilles' door, and comes back
before dawn to wake the old man and drive him out again. Every occurrence of "Argus"
in the poem is inside his fixed epithet.
*Epithets:* "the slayer of Argus" (ἀργεϊφόντης, 2.103); "the guide" (διάκτορος,
2.103) — rendered "the courier" in its later occurrences (20.34, 24.339).
*Kin:* son of Zeus; father of Eudorus by Polymele (16.181–186).
**Refs:** 2.103, 5.390, 14.490, 16.181, 20.35, 21.497, 24.334, 24.339, 24.461,
24.679, 24.694. Books 2, 5, 14–16, 20–21, 24.
*See also:* Zeus, Priam, Achilles, Eudorus, Leto, Ares, Pelops.

**Hicetaon** (Ἱκετάων) · MORTAL — *Say:* hy-suh-TAY-on
One of Priam's brothers, a son of Laomedon, and one of the Trojan elders sitting at
the Scaean Gates in Book 3 who watch Helen come and say she is worth the suffering.
He does not fight. In the poem he functions mainly as a patronymic: his son
Melanippus, who had herded cattle at Percote until the war came close, is rebuked by
Hector into the front line and shot in the chest by Antilochus.
*Epithets:* "scion of Ares" (ὄζος Ἄρηος, 3.147), varied as "sprig of Ares" in the
genealogy at 20.238.
*Kin:* son of Laomedon; brother of Priam, Tithonus, Lampus and Clytius; father of
Melanippus.
**Refs:** 3.147, 15.546, 15.576, 20.238. Books 3, 15, 20.
*See also:* Priam, Laomedon, Melanippus, Antilochus, Hector.

**Hippasus** (Ἵππασος) · MORTAL — *Say:* HIP-uh-sus
A name attached to men on both sides, never a character himself. On the Trojan side
he is father of Charops and of Socus, "such a pair of men," both killed by Odysseus
in Book 11 — Odysseus taunts the dying Socus as "son of war-minded Hippasus, breaker
of horses." A second Hippasus is the father of Hypsenor, killed by Deiphobus at
13.411; a third the father of Apisaon of Paeonia, killed by Lycomedes at 17.348.
The last two die in the same verbatim line, hit "in the liver below the midriff."
*Kin:* father of Charops and Socus; also named as father of Hypsenor and of Apisaon.
**Refs:** 11.426, 11.431, 11.450, 13.411, 17.348. Books 11, 13, 17.
*See also:* Socus, Charops, Odysseus, Hypsenor, Apisaon.

**Hippolochus** (Ἱππόλοχος) · MORTAL — *Say:* hip-OL-oh-kus
Glaucus' father, son of Bellerophon and brother of Isander and Laodameia — so the
link between the Lycian house and Sarpedon, who is Laodameia's son by Zeus. He does
not appear: the name is Glaucus' patronymic throughout, and his one act reported is
sending his son to Troy with the charge "always to be the best, and to hold his head
above all others" (6.207–208), the line Peleus also gives Achilles. A second
Hippolochus is a son of Antimachus, killed with his brother Peisander by Agamemnon
in Book 11, who shears off his hands and his head and sends him rolling like a stone
through the crowd.
*Kin:* son of Bellerophon; father of Glaucus; brother of Isander and Laodameia.
**Refs:** 6.119, 6.144, 6.197, 6.206, 7.13, 11.122, 11.145, 12.309, 12.387, 17.140.
Books 6–7, 11–12, 17.
*See also:* Glaucus, Bellerophon, Sarpedon, Laodameia, Antimachus, Agamemnon.

---

**Hippothous** (Ἱππόθοος) · MORTAL — *Say:* hip-OTH-oh-us
Two men. The chief one leads the Pelasgian spearmen of Larisa in the Catalogue with
his brother Pylaeus, and dies in Book 17 in the fight over Patroclus' body: he has
run a shield-strap round the dead man's ankle-tendons and is dragging him off by the
foot "doing a favor to Hector and the Trojans" when Telamonian Ajax spears him
through the helmet's cheek-piece and the brain runs out along the socket. He falls
face-down on the corpse, "far from deep-soiled Larisa; nor did he repay his dear
parents the price of his rearing." Priam's son Hippothous is a different man,
named once in the roll-call of the nine sons Priam curses (24.251).
*Kin:* the Pelasgian is son of Lethus, son of Teutamus; brother of Pylaeus.
*Killed by:* Ajax (17.293–303).
**Refs:** 2.840, 2.842, 17.217, 17.288, 17.313, 17.318, 24.251. Books 2, 17, 24.
*See also:* Lethus, Larisa, Pelasgians, Ajax, Patroclus, Priam.

---

**Hyle** (Ὕλη) · PLACE — *Say:* HY-lee
A Boeotian town by the Cephisian lake, listed in the Catalogue. Two men are placed
there: Oresbius "of the glancing waist-guard," who lived there hoarding his wealth
with care and is killed by Hector in the Book 5 roll-call; and Tychius, "far the
best of leather-cutters," who made the seven-hide tower shield Ajax carries against
Hector.
**Refs:** 2.500, 5.708, 7.221. Books 2, 5, 7.
*See also:* Boeotians, Oresbius, Tychius, Ajax.

---

**Hyrtacus** (Ὕρτακος) · MORTAL — *Say:* HUR-tuh-kus
Asius' father, and in the poem only that: every occurrence is the patronymic "Asius,
Hyrtacus' son," including the couplet the poem repeats verbatim from the Catalogue
when Asius storms the wall — "whom horses carried from Arisbe, tall and fire-colored,
from the river Selleïs" (2.838 = 12.96). Hector names him twice in the same
formula when he searches the front for his missing captains in Book 13.
*Kin:* father of Asius, leader of the men of Percote, Sestus, Abydos and Arisbe.
**Refs:** 2.837, 2.838, 12.96, 12.110, 12.162, 13.760, 13.771. Books 2, 12–13.
*See also:* Asius, Arisbe, Percote, Adamas, Hector.

---

**Ida** (Ἴδη) · PLACE — *Say:* EYE-duh
The mountain above Troy, "of the many springs, mother of wild things," from whose
peaks the eight rivers of the Troad run down to the sea. It is Zeus's station for
most of the poem: he comes there from Olympus in Book 8, sits on Gargaron looking
out at the city and the ships, thunders from it, and sends Iris and Apollo out from
it with his orders. Hera climbs it to seduce him; the earth-quake of the theomachy
shakes "all the roots of many-springed Ida." Aeneas was got on its spurs, "a goddess
bedded with a mortal"; Apollo herded Laomedon's cattle "in the spurs of wooded,
many-folded Ida"; and Agamemnon's oath calls on "Father Zeus, ruling from Ida."
*Epithets:* "Ida of the many springs, mother of wild things" (πολυπίδαξ Ἴδη, μήτηρ
θηρῶν, 8.47).
**Refs:** 2.821, 3.276, 8.47, 11.184, 12.19, 14.283, 15.151, 20.59, 21.449.
Books 2–4, 7–8, 11–17, 20–24.
*See also:* Gargaron, Zeus, Hera, Troy, Scamander, Simois, Aeneas.

---

**Idaeus** (Ἰδαῖος) · MORTAL — *Say:* eye-DEE-us
Priam's herald, "wise" and "war-minded," and the poem's chief instrument of truce.
He carries the oath-offerings and the mixing-bowl for the Book 3 duel, and rouses
Priam to come out to the plain. In Book 7 he and Talthybius force their staffs
between Ajax and Hector to stop the duel at nightfall; then he goes alone to the
Achaean assembly with Paris' offer of the treasure but not of Helen, hears it
refused, and takes Agamemnon's answer back — the truce for the burning of the dead.
In Book 24 he drives the mule-wagon of ransom to Achilles' hut and is left holding
the teams outside. A different Idaeus is a son of Dares the Hephaestus-priest, who
leaps from his chariot in Book 5 and is snatched away in darkness by Hephaestus so
that the old man should not lose both sons.
*Epithets:* "the far-calling herald" (ἠπύτα κῆρυξ, 7.384).
**Refs:** 3.247, 5.11, 5.20, 7.276, 7.372, 7.381, 7.413, 24.325, 24.470. Books 3,
5, 7, 24.
*See also:* Priam, Talthybius, Hector, Ajax, Dares, Hephaestus, Achilles.

---

**Idomeneus** (Ἰδομενεύς) · MORTAL — *Say:* eye-DOM-uh-nyoos *or* eye-DOM-ih-nee-us
Leader of the Cretans of hundred-citied Crete, with Meriones, and one of the
Achaean elder statesmen — Agamemnon gives him a cup that is always full and honors
him "beyond all the Danaans of swift colts." He is one of the men Achilles names in
Book 1 as fit to arbitrate, and one of the nine who volunteer against Hector. His
aristeia fills the middle of Book 13: Poseidon, in the likeness of Thoas, sends him
back into the fight, and though "his hair was half-grey" he kills Othryoneus,
Asius, Alcathous and Oenomaus before Aeneas comes at him and he gives ground,
admitting his legs are no longer what they were. In Book 23 he is first to see
Diomedes coming out of the dust and quarrels with the lesser Ajax over whose horses
lead.
*Epithets:* "famed with the spear" (δουρὶ κλυτός, 2.645).
*Kin:* son of Deucalion (13.451); grandson of Minos.
*Kills:* Othryoneus (13.370–373); Asius (13.387–393); Alcathous, Oenomaus, Erymas.
**Refs:** 1.145, 2.645, 4.252, 4.265, 13.210, 13.361, 13.370, 13.467, 23.450.
Books 1–8, 10–13, 15–17, 19, 23.
*See also:* Meriones, Cretans, Poseidon, Asius, Othryoneus, Aeneas, Deucalion.

---

**Ilioneus** (Ἰλιονεύς) · MORTAL — *Say:* il-ee-OH-nyoos
A Trojan, the only son of Phorbas "rich in flocks, whom Hermes loved beyond all the
Trojans, and gave him wealth." Peneleos spears him under the brow at the root of the
eye, pushes the eyeball out, and drives the point through the nape; then draws his
sword, hews through the neck, and holds up head, helmet and the spear still standing
in the eye "like a poppy-head" to show the Trojans — telling them to carry word to
Ilioneus' father and mother to start wailing. It is the poem's grimmest single
image, and the Trojans' limbs shake at the sight.
*Kin:* only son of Phorbas.
*Killed by:* Peneleos (14.489–500).
**Refs:** 14.489, 14.492, 14.502. Book 14.
*See also:* Peneleos, Phorbas, Hermes, Promachus.

---

**Ilus** (Ἶλος) · MORTAL — *Say:* EYE-lus
An ancestor of the Trojan kings, given in Aeneas' recital of the line at
20.231–236: Tros' eldest son, brother of Assaracus and Ganymede, and father of
Laomedon — so Priam's grandfather. The poem does not gloss his name against Ilion.
He is dead long before the action, and what survives him is his tomb, "the man-made
mound," a fixed landmark in the middle of the plain: Hector holds his night council
beside it, Agamemnon's rout streams past it, Paris shoots Diomedes from behind its
pillar, and Priam and Idaeus drive past it on the road to the ships.
*Kin:* son of Tros; brother of Assaracus and Ganymede; father of Laomedon;
great-grandfather of Hector.
**Refs:** 10.415, 11.166, 11.372, 20.232, 20.236, 24.349. Books 10–11, 20, 24.
*See also:* Tros, Laomedon, Priam, Assaracus, Dardanus, Troy.

---

**Imbros** (Ἴμβρος) · PLACE — *Say:* IM-bros
An island off Troy, always "rugged," and in the poem a marker of the sea-road
rather than a place with people. Poseidon's undersea cave lies "midway between
Tenedos and rugged Imbros"; Iris dives into the sea "midway between Samos and rugged
Imbros" on her way to Thetis; Hera and Sleep pass over it and Lemnos on the way to
Ida. Eëtion of Imbros is the guest-friend who ransomed Lycaon out of Lemnos, and
Hecuba names it in her lament as one of the places across the sea Achilles sold her
sons into.
**Refs:** 13.33, 14.281, 21.43, 24.78, 24.753. Books 13–14, 21, 24.
*See also:* Lemnos, Samos, Tenedos, Poseidon, Eëtion, Lycaon.

---

**Iphiclus** (Ἴφικλος) · MORTAL — *Say:* IF-ih-klus
Father of Protesilaus and Podarces, and "rich in flocks," son of Phylacus; he does
not appear, and both his sons are named by the patronymic when the Phylacean
contingent is handed on from the first man killed at Troy to his younger brother
(2.703–708, 13.698). The Iphiclus Nestor claims to have outrun in the games at
Buprasium — "good runner though he was" — belongs to the same back-story generation,
but the text does not say he is the same man.
*Kin:* son of Phylacus; father of Protesilaus and Podarces.
**Refs:** 2.705, 13.698, 23.636. Books 2, 13, 23.
*See also:* Protesilaus, Podarces, Phylacus, Nestor, Phylace.

---

**Iphidamas** (Ἰφιδάμας) · MORTAL — *Say:* if-ID-uh-mas
Antenor's son, "a good man and a big one," and the answer to the Muse-question of
who first came against Agamemnon in his aristeia. Reared in Thrace by his mother's
father Cisses, who tried to keep him there by giving him his daughter, he left the
bridal chamber "chasing the report of the Achaeans" with twelve ships, beached them
at Percote, and walked the rest of the way to Ilion. His thrust bends on Agamemnon's
war-belt "like lead"; Agamemnon wrenches the spear from his grip and cuts his neck,
and he "slept a sleep of bronze," far from the wife he had bought with a hundred
cattle and a promise of a thousand goats and sheep. His brother Coon wounds
Agamemnon in the forearm trying to drag the body clear and is beheaded over it.
*Kin:* son of Antenor and Theano; grandson of Cisses; brother of Coon.
*Killed by:* Agamemnon (11.238–241).
**Refs:** 11.221, 11.234, 11.257, 11.261. Book 11.
*See also:* Antenor, Coon, Agamemnon, Theano, Cisses, Percote.

---

**Iris** (Ἶρις) · GOD — *Say:* EYE-ris
The gods' runner, and the poem's most reliable instrument: she carries Zeus's word
and no one else's business is ever her own. She rouses the Trojan assembly in
Priam's likeness at 2.786, leads the wounded Aphrodite out of the fighting and up
to Olympus (5.353–369), turns Hera and Athena back from the field with Zeus's
threat (8.398–425), and brings Achilles unarmed to the ditch on Hera's secret
errand (18.166–202). She has one line of her own judgment — telling Poseidon that
the hearts of the great can turn (15.203) — and Poseidon calls it the mark of a
messenger who knows what is fitting. In Book 23 she carries Achilles' prayer to the
winds; in Book 24 she goes down through the sea to Thetis and then to Priam, and
her speech to him repeats Zeus's word almost exactly.
*Epithets:* "wind-footed swift Iris" (ποδήνεμος ὠκέα Ἶρις, 2.786); "swift-footed
Iris" (πόδας ὠκέα Ἶρις, 2.790); "of the golden wings" (χρυσόπτερος, 8.398);
"storm-footed" (ἀελλόπος, 8.409).
**Refs:** 2.786, 3.121, 5.353, 8.398, 11.185, 15.157, 18.166, 24.143 …and
throughout. Books 2–3, 5, 8, 11, 15, 18, 23–24.
*See also:* Zeus, Hera, Aphrodite, Poseidon, Thetis, Priam, Achilles.

---

**Jason** (Ἰήσων) · MORTAL — *Say:* JAY-sun
In the back-story only: "shepherd of the people," to whom Hypsipyle bore Euneus,
the king of Lemnos who sends wine-ships to the Achaean camp and gives a thousand
measures separately to Agamemnon and Menelaus (7.467–471). The poem's interest in
him is entirely through his son, who buys Lycaon when Achilles sells him into
Lemnos (21.41) and whose silver mixing-bowl — paid as Lycaon's price to Patroclus —
becomes the foot-race prize in Book 23.
*Kin:* by Hypsipyle, father of Euneus.
**Refs:** 7.468, 7.469, 7.471, 21.41, 23.746. Books 7, 21, 23.
*See also:* Euneus, Hypsipyle, Lemnos, Lycaon.

**Laertes** (Λαέρτης) · MORTAL — *Say:* lay-UR-teez
Odysseus's father, who never appears: in every one of his occurrences the name is
a patronymic for his son. It carries the fixed vocative "Zeus-sprung son of
Laertes, Odysseus of many devices" (2.173), used by Athena, Agamemnon, Nestor,
Diomedes and Achilles in turn; Helen names Odysseus to Priam from the wall as
"Laertes' son, resourceful Odysseus" (3.200).
*Kin:* father of Odysseus.
**Refs:** 2.173, 3.200, 4.358, 8.93, 9.308, 10.144, 19.185, 23.723. Books 2–4, 8–10,
19, 23.
*See also:* Odysseus.

**Lampus** (Λάμπος) · MORTAL — *Say:* LAM-pus
One of the elders sitting with Priam at the Scaean Gates — "finished with war now
because of age, but speakers of quality, like cicadas" — and one of Laomedon's
sons, so Priam's brother; the genealogy Aeneas recites to Achilles puts him with
Clytius and Hicetaon (20.238). His son Dolops, "the strongest son that Lampus
fathered," is killed by Menelaus while fighting Meges (15.525–543). The name also
belongs to one of Hector's four chariot-horses, called by name at 8.185.
*Kin:* son of Laomedon; brother of Priam, Tithonus, Clytius and Hicetaon; father of
Dolops.
**Refs:** 3.147, 8.185, 15.526, 20.238. Books 3, 8, 15, 20.
*See also:* Laomedon, Priam, Clytius, Hicetaon, Dolops, Hector.

---

**Laodice** (Λαοδίκη) · MORTAL — *Say:* lay-OD-ih-see
Two women. Priam's daughter, "loveliest of the daughters of Priam" (3.124), married
to Antenor's son Helicaon; Iris takes her likeness to fetch Helen to the wall, and
Hecuba is leading her when she meets Hector in Book 6. A second Laodice is one of
Agamemnon's three daughters, offered to Achilles with Chrysothemis and Iphianassa
in the embassy's list of gifts (9.145 = 9.287).
*Kin:* Priam's daughter is the daughter of Priam and Hecuba, and wife of Helicaon;
the other is a daughter of Agamemnon.
**Refs:** 3.124, 6.252, 9.145, 9.287. Books 3, 6, 9.
*See also:* Priam, Hecuba, Helicaon, Antenor, Agamemnon, Chrysothemis, Iphianassa.

---

**Laomedon** (Λαομέδων) · MORTAL — *Say:* lay-OM-uh-don
Priam's father, dead before the poem, and its standing example of a man who cheats
gods. Poseidon tells the story twice: he and Apollo served him a whole year at a
stated wage — Poseidon building the city wall, Apollo herding cattle on Ida — and
at the term he robbed them by force and threatened to bind them and sell them
overseas and cut off their ears (21.441–457, cf. 7.452–453). He also refused
Heracles the horses promised for a service, and Heracles sacked Ilion for it
(5.640–651). His horses remain the standard of excellence: Nestor tells Antilochus
no man will pass him, not driving Arion nor "the horses of Laomedon, best of the
breed reared here" (23.348).
*Kin:* son of Ilus; father of Priam, Tithonus, Lampus, Clytius, Hicetaon and
Bucolion.
**Refs:** 3.250, 5.269, 5.640, 5.649, 7.453, 20.236, 21.444, 21.452, 23.348.
Books 3, 5–7, 15, 20–21, 23.
*See also:* Priam, Poseidon, Apollo, Heracles, Ilus, Lampus, Tros.

---

**Laothoe** (Λαοθόη) · MORTAL — *Say:* lay-OTH-oh-ee
One of Priam's wives, daughter of Altes lord of the Lelegians, and mother of Lycaon
and Polydorus — the two sons Achilles kills within a book of each other. Lycaon
names her as he supplicates: "to a short life my mother bore me — Laothoe, daughter
of the old man Altes" (21.84–89). Priam, watching from the wall, counts the same
two as missing and says that if they are dead it is grief to his heart and their
mother's, "a queen among women" (22.46–53).
*Kin:* daughter of Altes; wife of Priam; mother of Lycaon and Polydorus.
**Refs:** 21.85, 22.48. Books 21–22.
*See also:* Altes, Priam, Lycaon, Polydorus, Achilles.

---

**Lemnos** (Λῆμνος) · PLACE — *Say:* LEM-nos
The island Hephaestus fell on when Zeus flung him from the threshold of heaven,
where the Sintian men took him up (1.593); Philoctetes lies there abandoned with his
snake-bite while his ships fight at Troy (2.722); and it is "the city of godlike
Thoas" that Hera passes through on her way to find Sleep (14.230). It is also the
poem's slave-market: Achilles ships Lycaon there and sells him, and the son of Jason
pays the price (21.40–41) — a sale Lycaon and Achilles both throw back at each other
in Book 21, and one Priam expects for his other sons in Book 24.
**Refs:** 1.593, 2.722, 8.230, 14.230, 14.281, 21.40, 21.58, 21.79, 24.753.
Books 1–2, 7–8, 14, 21, 24.
*See also:* Hephaestus, Philoctetes, Thoas, Lycaon, Euneus, Imbros.

---

**Leonteus** (Λεοντεύς) · MORTAL — *Say:* lee-ON-tyoos
One of the two Lapith captains, always paired with Polypoetes: they lead forty ships
in the Catalogue and stand before the gate in Book 12 "like high-crowned oaks in the
mountains that abide the wind and rain through all their days," refusing to be driven
inside the wall. There he spears Hippomachus at the war-belt and then goes at the
rest with the sword. In Book 23 he throws the lump of pig-iron second and loses to
Polypoetes.
*Epithets:* "scion of Ares" (ὄζος Ἄρηος, 2.745).
*Kin:* son of Coronus, Caeneus' son.
**Refs:** 2.745, 12.130, 12.188, 23.837, 23.841. Books 2, 12, 23.
*See also:* Polypoetes, Coronus, Caeneus, Lapiths.

---

**Lesbos** (Λέσβος) · PLACE — *Say:* LEZ-bos
One of Achilles' conquests before the poem opens: the seven women skilled in
blameless work whom Agamemnon offers in the embassy are the ones Achilles himself
chose "when he himself took well-built Lesbos" (9.129–130 = 9.271–272), and the
woman sleeping beside him at the end of Book 9 is Diomede, Phorbas's daughter,
brought from there. Achilles names it again in Book 24 as the seaward bound of
Priam's old prosperity, "the seat of Macar."
**Refs:** 9.129, 9.130, 9.271, 9.664, 24.544. Books 9, 24.
*See also:* Achilles, Diomede, Macar, Priam.

---

**Leto** (Λητώ) · GOD — *Say:* LEE-toh
Mother of Apollo and Artemis by Zeus, named in the poem's ninth line as the parent
of the god who sends the plague. She takes the Trojan side: she heals Aeneas with
Artemis in Apollo's shrine on Pergamos (5.447), and in the theomachy she is matched
against Hermes — who declines to fight her, telling her to boast among the gods that
she overcame him, and leaves her to gather up her daughter's scattered bow and
arrows (21.497–504). Zeus names her in his catalogue of loves at 14.327, and it is
for matching herself against Leto that Niobe's twelve children are killed (24.607).
Achilles' horse Xanthus calls Apollo "the best of the gods, whom lovely-haired Leto
bore" (19.413).
*Epithets:* "lovely-haired" (ἠΰκομος, 1.36); "fair-cheeked" (καλλιπάρῃος, 24.607).
*Kin:* by Zeus, mother of Apollo and Artemis.
**Refs:** 1.9, 1.36, 5.447, 14.327, 16.849, 19.413, 20.40, 21.497, 24.607.
Books 1, 5, 14, 16, 19–21, 24.
*See also:* Apollo, Artemis, Zeus, Hermes, Niobe.

---

**Leïtus** (Λήϊτος) · MORTAL — *Say:* LEE-ih-tus
One of the five Boeotian leaders in the Catalogue, and the one of them the poem
keeps using. He kills Phylacus in Book 6, is among the captains Poseidon rouses in
Book 13, and in Book 17 Hector wounds him on the wrist at close quarters and stops
his fighting for good: "he shrank back, glancing round, for his heart no longer
hoped to hold a spear steady in hand and fight the Trojans."
*Kin:* son of Alectryon (17.602).
**Refs:** 2.494, 6.35, 13.91, 17.601, 17.605. Books 2, 6, 13, 17.
*See also:* Peneleos, Boeotians, Alectryon, Hector.

---

**Locrians** (Λοκροί) · PEOPLE — *Say:* LOK-ree-unz
The lesser Ajax's forty ships, from Cynus, Opoeis and the towns around the Boagrius,
"who live across from holy Euboea." Book 13 makes them the poem's only distinct
light infantry: they carry no bronze helmets, no shields and no ash spears, only
bows and slings of twisted wool, so they will not stand in the close fight — they
follow behind the armored line and shoot unseen, and it is their arrows that break
the Trojan formations and rout them (13.712–722).
**Refs:** 2.527, 2.535, 13.686, 13.712. Books 2, 13.
*See also:* Ajax son of Oïleus, Opoeis, Euboea.

---

**Lycaon** (Λυκάων) · MORTAL — *Say:* ly-KAY-on
Two men. The chief one is Priam's son by Laothoe, whose supplication in Book 21 is
the poem's most sustained refusal. Achilles had taken him once by night in his
father's orchard cutting fig-shoots for chariot-rails, shipped him to Lemnos and sold
him; ransomed by Eëtion of Imbros, he had been home eleven days when Achilles meets
him again in the river. He grasps the knees with one hand and the spear with the
other and pleads guest-right and the grain of Demeter; Achilles answers that
Patroclus died and was a better man, and that death and strong fate stand over
himself too, then cuts through his collarbone and slings him into the river to the
fish. His corslet is what Paris arms in for the duel (3.333). The second Lycaon is
Pandarus's father, of Zeleia, whose eleven new-built chariots stand under cloths at
home while Pandarus fights on foot (5.193–197); "Lycaon's shining son" throughout
Books 4 and 5 means Pandarus, not Priam's son.
*Kin:* Priam's son is son of Priam and Laothoe, brother of Polydorus; the other is
father of Pandarus.
*Killed by:* Achilles (21.114–119).
**Refs:** 2.826, 3.333, 4.89, 5.95, 5.193, 20.81, 21.35, 21.71, 21.127, 22.46,
23.746. Books 2–5, 20–23.
*See also:* Pandarus, Priam, Laothoe, Polydorus, Achilles, Eëtion, Lemnos.

---

**Lycia** (Λυκία) · PLACE — *Say:* LISH-uh *or* LIS-ee-uh
The homeland of Sarpedon and Glaucus, the farthest of Troy's allies — "from far
Lycia, from the eddying Xanthus" (2.877). Sarpedon names it as what he has left:
his wife, his little son, and great wealth (5.480–481). It is also where
Bellerophon was sent with the folded tablet, and where the king gave him his
daughter and the best of the land (6.168–195); and Glaucus's account of the estate
on the banks of Xanthus is the ground of Sarpedon's argument about why kings must
fight in front (12.310–321). Sleep and Death carry Sarpedon's body back to "the
rich country of wide Lycia" for burial (16.683).
**Refs:** 2.877, 5.105, 5.479, 6.168, 6.172, 6.225, 12.312, 12.319, 16.437, 16.673,
16.683. Books 2, 5–6, 12, 16–17.
*See also:* Lycians, Sarpedon, Glaucus, Bellerophon, Xanthus, Hippolochus.

---

**Lycians** (Λύκιοι) · PEOPLE — *Say:* LISH-unz
Troy's strongest allies, led by Sarpedon and Glaucus, and the only contingent the
poem treats as a rival to the Trojans themselves. Hector's rallying-cry names them
in their own clause — "Trojans and Lycians and Dardanians who fight at close
quarters" (8.173) — and it is they who first break through the Achaean wall in Book
12. Sarpedon shames them into the assault ("Lycians, why do you slacken your rushing
courage so?", 12.409) and again as he dies over his own body; when he is killed they
panic and abandon it, and Glaucus rounds on Hector for it in Book 17, threatening
that no Lycian will fight for Troy again.
**Refs:** 2.876, 4.197, 5.633, 5.676, 8.173, 12.317, 12.409, 16.421, 16.659, 17.146.
Books 2, 4–8, 10–17.
*See also:* Lycia, Sarpedon, Glaucus, Trojans, Hector.

**Lycomedes** (Λυκομήδης) · MORTAL — *Say:* ly-koh-MEE-deez

in Book 9, left with the lesser Ajax to hold the line while Telamonian Ajax goes

**Lycurgus** (Λυκοῦργος) · MORTAL — *Say:* ly-KUR-gus
Two men, both from stories told inside the poem. Diomedes cites the first as the
warning against fighting gods: Dryas' son, who chased the nurses of raving Dionysus
down Nysa beating them with an ox-goad, until Dionysus dived under the sea and Thetis
took him in terror; the gods were angered, Zeus struck him blind, and he did not live
long, "hated by all the immortal gods" (6.130–140). The second is the Arcadian of
Nestor's Book 7 reminiscence, who killed Areithous the Club-man by craft in a narrow
lane where the iron club was useless, and in old age passed the stripped armor to his
aide Ereuthalion.
*Kin:* the first is son of Dryas.
*Kills:* the Arcadian kills Areithous (7.142–145).
**Refs:** 6.130, 6.135, 6.139, 7.142, 7.144, 7.148. Books 6–7.
*See also:* Dryas, Dionysus, Diomedes, Areithous, Ereuthalion, Nestor.

**Lyrnessus** (Λυρνησσός) · PLACE — *Say:* lur-NES-us
The town Achilles sacked, taking Briseis out of it "with great toil" and killing
Mynes and Epistrophus, the sons of Euenus (2.690–693). Achilles wishes in Book 19
that Artemis had killed the girl with an arrow "on the day I chose her from the sack
of Lyrnessus." Aeneas names it too, in a different register: it was to Lyrnessus he
fled when Achilles came down on the cattle off Ida and chased him, and Achilles
wrecked the town behind him while Zeus saved the man (20.90–92, 20.191).

**Machaon** (Μαχάων) · MORTAL — *Say:* muh-KAY-on
One of the army's two physicians, son of Asclepius, who leads thirty ships from
Tricca with his brother Podaleirius. He is fetched by Talthybius to draw the arrow
from Menelaus's war-belt and sprinkle the soothing drugs Chiron once gave his father
(4.193–219). His own wounding turns the poem: Paris hits him in the right shoulder,
Nestor drives him out of the fighting, Achilles sees the pair from his ship's stern
and sends Patroclus to ask who the wounded man is — "and that was the beginning of
his evil" (11.504–605). Patroclus finds him drinking Hecamede's posset in Nestor's
hut and does not stay; Eurypylus later notes that of the two healers one lies wounded
and the other is still on the plain (11.833).
*Kin:* son of Asclepius; brother of Podaleirius.
**Refs:** 2.732, 4.193, 4.200, 4.213, 11.506, 11.598, 11.651, 11.833, 14.3.

**Maeonia** (Μῃονίη) · PLACE — *Say:* mee-OH-nee-uh

**Mecisteus** (Μηκιστεύς) · MORTAL — *Say:* muh-SIS-tyoos

Euryalus, and the man who went to Thebes for the burial of fallen Oedipus and beat

**Medon** (Μέδων) · MORTAL — *Say:* MEE-don

**Meges** (Μέγης) · MORTAL — *Say:* MEE-jeez
Leader of the forty ships from Dulichium and the Echinae, son of the horseman
Phyleus who had moved there in anger at his own father. He kills Antenor's bastard
son Pedaeus in Book 5, and in Book 13 is named among the leaders of the Epeians.

Lampus drives a spear into his shield — the corslet Phyleus had brought home from
Ephyra as a guest-gift saves him — and he shears the plume clean off Dolops's helmet
before Menelaus comes to his rescue. He is one of the seven who fetch Agamemnon's
gifts in Book 19.

*Also called:* Phyleus' son.

**Melanippus** (Μελάνιππος) · MORTAL — *Say:* mel-uh-NIP-us
Four men bear the name. The one the poem develops is Hicetaon's son, a cousin of
Hector's: he had been herding cattle in Percote until the Danaan ships came, and
then lived in Priam's house and was honored like his own children. Hector rebukes
him over Dolops's body and he follows; Menelaus sets Antilochus on him, and
Antilochus hits him in the chest by the nipple and springs on him like a hound on a
hit fawn, only to be driven off by Hector (15.546–585). The others are a Trojan
Teucer kills at 8.276, one of Patroclus's victims in the roll-call at 16.695, and an
Achaean among the men Achilles sends for Agamemnon's gifts (19.240).
*Kin:* the Trojan is son of Hicetaon.
*Killed by:* Hicetaon's son by Antilochus (15.576–578).

**Meleager** (Μελέαγρος) · MORTAL — *Say:* mel-ee-AY-jur
Phoenix's exemplum in the embassy, and the poem's model for Achilles' own case.
Oeneus' son, dead before the Catalogue is spoken (2.642), he killed the boar Artemis
sent against Calydon and then, in anger at his mother Althaea's curses, withdrew
from the fighting and lay beside his wife Cleopatra while the Curetes stormed the
walls. Elders, priests, his father, his sisters and his mother all begged him and he
refused the more; only when the towers were under bombardment and the city burning
did his wife's plea move him, and he went out — and got none of the gifts he had
been offered. Phoenix's point is the moral: come out while the gifts are still on
the table.
*Kin:* son of Oeneus and Althaea; husband of Cleopatra.
**Refs:** 2.642, 9.543, 9.550, 9.553, 9.565, 9.590. Books 2, 9.
*See also:* Phoenix, Oeneus, Althaea, Cleopatra, Calydon, Curetes, Artemis.

**Menelaus** (Μενέλαος) · MORTAL — *Say:* men-uh-LAY-us
Agamemnon's brother and Helen's husband, the man the war is nominally for, and the
one Achaean whose motive is personal. He volunteers unbidden for the muster (2.408),
takes the duel with Paris in Book 3 and all but wins it — the spear through the
shield, the sword shattering on the helmet-ridge, the chin-strap strangling Paris
until Aphrodite breaks it and carries the man off — and is then shot by Pandarus in
the broken truce, the arrow steered by Athena into the buckles of his war-belt
(4.127–140). The poem's word for him is restraint verging on softness: Agamemnon
overrules his mercy to the suppliant Adrestus and does the killing himself
(6.51–65), the army fears for him when
he offers to fight Hector (7.104–109). His full measure comes in Book 17, where he
stands over Patroclus's body, kills Euphorbus for it, and, when at last he must
leave it to the Ajaxes, goes off to find Antilochus so someone can carry the news to
Achilles. In Book 23 he charges Antilochus with fouling him in the chariot-race and
then, satisfied by an apology, gives the mare back.
*Epithets:* "dear to Ares" (ἀρηΐφιλος, 3.21); "tawny-haired" (ξανθός, 3.284); "good
at the war-cry" (βοὴν ἀγαθός, 2.408).
*Also called:* Atreus' son — but "Atreus' son" in the singular means Agamemnon
unless the scene is Menelaus's; see `canon.md`.
*Kin:* son of Atreus; brother of Agamemnon; husband of Helen.
*Kills:* Peisander (13.601–642), Euphorbus (17.45–60), Podes (17.578–581).
**Refs:** 1.159, 2.408, 3.21, 3.339, 4.128, 6.37, 7.94, 13.601, 17.1, 17.60,
23.566 …and throughout. Books 1–8, 10–11, 13, 15–17, 23.
*See also:* Agamemnon, Helen, Paris, Atreus, Antilochus, Euphorbus, Patroclus, Ajax.

**Menestheus** (Μενεσθεύς) · MORTAL — *Say:* muh-NES-thyoos
The Athenian leader, of Erechtheus' city, with fifty ships: "no man on the face of
the earth was his equal at marshaling horses and shield-bearing men — Nestor alone
rivaled him, for he was older" (2.553–555). The praise is not borne out in action.
His scene is Book 12, where Sarpedon's Lycians come at his stretch of wall and he
peers along it for help, unable to make himself heard over the din, until Thootes
fetches Telamonian Ajax and Teucer to his tower. He and Stichius carry Amphimachus's
body back in Book 13; his trusted companion Arcesilaus is killed by Hector at 15.331.
*Also called:* Peteos' son.
*Kin:* son of Peteos.
**Refs:** 2.552, 4.327, 12.331, 12.373, 13.195, 13.690, 15.331. Books 2, 4, 12, 13, 15.

**Menoetius** (Μενοίτιος) · MORTAL — *Say:* muh-NEE-tee-us
Patroclus' father, Actor's son, of Opoeis: he brought his son as a small boy to
Peleus' house after a killing over a game of dice, and Peleus raised the two
children together (23.85–90). He is alive in Phthia through the poem — Achilles asks
whether Patroclus has news that he or Peleus has died (16.14) — but he never appears,
and in nearly all of the name's 38 occurrences it is the patronymic "Menoetius' son"
or "Menoetius's stout son," meaning Patroclus. Nestor quotes the charge he gave at
parting: you are the elder, so advise Achilles well, he will listen (11.785–789).
Achilles' reproach to himself in Book 18 is that he promised to bring the man's
far-famed son back to Opoeis with his share of the spoils.
*Kin:* son of Actor; father of Patroclus.
**Refs:** 1.307, 9.202, 11.605, 11.765, 11.785, 16.14, 18.12, 18.325, 21.28, 23.85,
24.16 …and throughout. Books 1, 9, 11–12, 16–19, 21, 23–24.
*See also:* Patroclus, Achilles, Peleus, Actor, Opoeis, Nestor.

**Meriones** (Μηριόνης) · MORTAL — *Say:* muh-RY-oh-neez
Idomeneus's aide and second in command of the Cretans, and one of the poem's most
consistently competent fighters. Their exchange outside the huts in Book 13 — he has
come back for a spear, having shattered his on Deiphobus's shield, and Idomeneus
offers him twenty Trojan spears taken from the men he has killed — is the poem's
fullest picture of two veterans talking shop. He wounds Deiphobus in the arm, shoots
Harpalion through the buttock into the bladder as he retreats, kills the priest
Laogonus in Book 16 and trades taunts with Aeneas over a missed cast. He is Odysseus's
armorer for the night raid, lending the boar's-tusk helmet whose four-generation
provenance the poem tracks (10.260–271). In Book 23 he drives the slowest horses and
finishes fourth, then wins the archery: Teucer, who did not vow to Apollo, cuts only
the cord; Meriones snatches the bow, vows the hecatomb, and shoots the dove through
under the wing high under the clouds.
*Epithets:* "the equal of Enyalius, slayer of men" (ἀτάλαντος Ἐνυαλίῳ, 2.651).
*Kin:* son of Molus.
**Refs:** 2.651, 10.260, 13.249, 13.528, 13.650, 16.603, 17.717, 23.528, 23.870.
Books 2, 4–5, 7–10, 13–17, 19, 23.
*See also:* Idomeneus, Molus, Cretans, Deiphobus, Teucer, Odysseus, Harpalion.

**Mulius** (Μούλιος) · MORTAL — *Say:* MYOO-lee-us
Three men, all killed. The first is the Epeian of Nestor's Book 11 reminiscence,
Augeias' son-in-law, husband of his eldest daughter Agamede "who knew as many drugs
as the wide earth grows" — the first man Nestor ever killed, and he took his
whole-hoofed horses. The second is one of Patroclus's victims in the roll-call at

point passing out through the other ear.
*Kin:* the Epeian is son-in-law of Augeias and husband of Agamede.
*Killed by:* the Epeian by Nestor (11.742); the second by Patroclus (16.696); the

**Muses** (Μοῦσαι) · GOD
Daughters of aegis-bearing Zeus, singers in answer to Apollo's lyre at the gods'
feast in Book 1. Their function in the poem is to know: the poet invokes them before
the Catalogue because "you are goddesses, you are present, you know all things, while
we hear only the report and know nothing" (2.484–486), and the same line opens the
three later roll-calls (11.218, 14.508, 16.112). At 2.761 the singular Muse is asked
who was best of the men and horses. Their one action is punitive: they met Thamyris
the Thracian at Dorium, who had boasted he would win a singing contest against them,
and maimed him — took away his wondrous song and made him forget his harping.
*Kin:* daughters of Zeus.
**Refs:** 1.604, 2.484, 2.491, 2.594, 2.598, 2.761, 11.218, 14.508, 16.112.
Books 1, 2, 11, 14, 16.
*See also:* Zeus, Apollo, Thamyris, Olympus.

**Mycenae** (Μυκήνη) · PLACE — *Say:* my-SEE-nee
Agamemnon's city and the seat of his fleet — "the great fleet that followed you from
Mycenae" (9.44) — and, in the formula, "Mycenae rich in gold" (7.180 = 11.46). Hera
names it third of the cities dearest to her, with Argos and Sparta, and offers all
three to be sacked whenever they grow hateful to Zeus, if Troy may fall (4.52–53).
Tydeus came there once as a guest with Polyneices, raising troops for Thebes
(4.376–377). Periphetes, the best man in the city, is Hector's one kill in the rout
of Book 15.

**Myrmidons** (Μυρμιδόνες) · PEOPLE — *Say:* MUR-mih-donz
Achilles' own contingent, fifty ships from Phthia and Hellas; the Catalogue notes
that the same men "were called Myrmidons, and Hellenes, and Achaeans" (2.684), which
makes the last a regional name here and not the whole host. They sit out the war
with their leader — Agamemnon's insult is "go home and be king over your Myrmidons"
(1.180) — until Book 16, when Achilles arms them for Patroclus. Their two similes
there are the poem's harshest: wolves that have torn a stag, their muzzles red,
going down to lap black water; and roadside wasps that boys have been provoking, who
come out all at once. They mourn Patroclus, drive their chariots three times around
the body, and build Achilles' hut in the description that opens the last scene of
Book 24.
**Refs:** 1.180, 2.684, 9.185, 16.155, 16.200, 16.266, 18.355, 19.278, 23.6, 24.449.
Books 1–2, 7, 9, 11, 16, 18–19, 21, 23–24.
*See also:* Achilles, Patroclus, Phthia, Hellenes, Achaeans.

**Mysians** (Μυσοί) · PEOPLE — *Say:* MIZH-unz
Trojan-side allies, led in the Catalogue by Chromis and Ennomus the bird-reader,

gift-givers: the strong-hoofed mules Priam yokes for the journey to Achilles were
once a Mysian present to him (24.278).
**Refs:** 2.858, 10.430, 13.5, 14.512, 24.278. Books 2, 10, 13, 14, 24.

**Neleus** (Νηλεύς) · MORTAL — *Say:* NEE-lee-us
Nestor's father and king of Pylos, dead before the action: in nearly every one of

**Nereids** (Νηρηΐδες) · GOD — *Say:* NEER-ee-idz
Thetis's sisters, the sea-nymphs who gather around her in the depths when she
wails for Achilles at the opening of Book 18. The poem names thirty-three of
them in a four-line catalogue (18.39–49) — Glauce, Thaleia, Cymodoce and the rest

*Kin:* daughters of the old man of the sea; sisters of Thetis.

**Nestor** (Νέστωρ) · MORTAL — *Say:* NES-tor
The aged king of Pylos, third-generation ruler who has outlived two generations
of mortal men, and the counselor whose plans set the poem's machinery moving. He
tries and fails to compose the quarrel in Book 1; he proposes the wall and ditch
(7.325–343); he frames the embassy to Achilles and picks its members (9.162–172);
he devises the night raid; and it is his suggestion at 11.794–803 — send
Patroclus out in Achilles' armor — that Patroclus carries back and that kills
him. His authority is verbal, not martial: he is rescued out of the fighting in
Book 8, and at the funeral games Achilles gives him a prize outright because he
will not compete, "the hard weight of age is on you now."
*Epithets:* "the Gerenian horseman Nestor" (Γερήνιος ἱππότα Νέστωρ, 2.336); "the
Gerenian, warden of the Achaeans" (Γερήνιος οὖρος Ἀχαιῶν, 8.80); "the clear-voiced
speaker" (λιγὺς ἀγορητής, 1.248).
*Also called:* Neleus' son.
*Kin:* son of Neleus; father of Antilochus and Thrasymedes.
**Refs:** 1.247, 2.336, 4.293, 7.325, 8.80, 9.162, 10.18, 11.501 …and throughout.
Books 1–2, 4–11, 13–19, 23.
*See also:* Neleus, Antilochus, Thrasymedes, Patroclus, Pylos, Agamemnon.

**Night** (Νύξ) · GOD
Personified, and named once in a story Sleep tells Hera to explain why he will
not put Zeus to sleep a second time. Hunted through the house by a furious Zeus
and about to be flung from the sky into the sea, Sleep ran to Night for refuge,
and Zeus checked himself: "he stood in awe of doing what swift Night would hate."
The poem gives her no other action; the line is its shortest statement that some
things are older than Zeus.
*Epithets:* "Night, subduer of gods and men" (Νὺξ δμήτειρα θεῶν καὶ ἀνδρῶν,
14.259).
**Refs:** 14.259, 14.261. Book 14.
*See also:* Sleep, Zeus, Hera.

**Niobe** (Νιόβη) · MORTAL — *Say:* NY-oh-bee
Achilles' exemplum in the last book: he tells Priam that even Niobe remembered
food, though twelve children died in her halls — six daughters and six sons,
killed by Artemis and Apollo because she had matched herself against Leto, who
bore only two. The dead lay nine days unburied, the people having been turned to
stone by Zeus, and the gods buried them on the tenth; and Niobe, worn out with
weeping, ate. She is now stone herself on Sipylus, brooding on the griefs the
gods gave her. The story is the argument that persuades Priam to eat.
*Kin:* mother of six sons and six daughters.
**Refs:** 24.602, 24.606, 24.607. Book 24.
*See also:* Achilles, Priam, Leto, Apollo, Artemis, Sipylus.

---

**Nireus** (Νιρεύς) · MORTAL — *Say:* NY-ryoos
Leader of three ships from Syme, named three times over in three consecutive
lines of the Catalogue — the poem's most conspicuous piece of anaphora — as "the
most beautiful man who came under Ilion / of all the Danaans, after the blameless
son of Peleus." The compliment is immediately withdrawn: "But he was a slight
man, and few people followed him." He never appears again.
*Kin:* son of Aglaïa and lord Charopus.
**Refs:** 2.671, 2.672, 2.673. Book 2.
*See also:* Achaeans, Achilles.

---

**Ocean** (Ὠκεανός) · GOD
The stream that encircles the world and the god of it. As geography it is where
the sun and stars go down and Dawn rises out (8.485, 19.1), where Zeus goes
feasting with the Ethiopians (1.423), and the outermost rim of the Shield of
Achilles (18.607); the Bear alone "has no share in the baths of Ocean." As a
person he is the father from whom, Achilles says, all rivers and seas and springs
flow, and who nonetheless fears Zeus's thunderbolt (21.195–199); with Tethys he
is "the begetting of the gods," and Hera's pretext for leaving Olympus is that
she goes to reconcile their long estrangement.
*Also called:* Oceanus — the spelling the translation uses of the named god in
Book 14; "Ocean" elsewhere for the encircling stream.
*Kin:* consort of Tethys; father of Eurynome (18.399) and, as the poem has it,
of all waters.
**Refs:** 1.423, 8.485, 14.201, 14.246, 14.302, 18.607, 19.1, 21.195.
Books 1, 3, 5, 7, 8, 14, 16, 18–21, 23.
*See also:* Tethys, Hera, Achelous, Scamander, Eurynome.

---

**Odius** (Ὀδίος) · MORTAL — *Say:* OH-dee-us
Two men. The first leads the Halizones with Epistrophus "from far away, from
Alybe, where silver has its birthplace," and is the first man killed in the
general fighting of Book 5: Agamemnon spears him in the back between the
shoulders as he turns to run, and drives the point through his chest. The second
is an Achaean herald, sent with Eurybates to accompany the embassy to Achilles.
*Killed by:* Agamemnon (5.38–42).
**Refs:** 2.856, 5.39, 9.170. Books 2, 5, 9.
*See also:* Agamemnon, Epistrophus, Eurybates, Halizones.

---

**Odysseus** (Ὀδυσσεύς) · MORTAL — *Say:* oh-DIS-ee-us *or* oh-DISS-yoos
The Achaeans' tactician, and the man the poem sends whenever a thing must be
managed rather than won by force. He turns the army back from the ships in Book 2
and beats Thersites out of the assembly; he escorts Chryseis home; he speaks
first for the embassy in Book 9 and Nestor charges him "above all" to win
Achilles over; he goes out with Diomedes on the night raid and brings back
Rhesus' horses; and in Book 19 he holds Achilles to the army's need to eat before
fighting. Antenor's account of him at Troy long before the war (3.203–224) is the
poem's fullest portrait of a speaker: a man who stands with his eyes on the
ground like a fool until the words come out of him like snowflakes in winter.
*Epithets:* "resourceful" (πολύμητις, 1.311); "of many devices" (πολυμήχανος,
2.173); "the equal of Zeus in counsel" (Διὶ μῆτιν ἀτάλαντος, 2.169); "sacker of
cities" (πτολίπορθος, 2.278); "much-enduring brilliant Odysseus" (πολύτλας δῖος
Ὀδυσσεύς, 8.97); "of the many tales" (πολύαινος, 9.673); "hardy" (τλήμων,
10.231); "patient-hearted" (ταλασίφρων, 11.466); "of the subtle wiles"
(ποικιλομήτης, 11.482).
*Also called:* Laertes' son.
*Kin:* son of Laertes; father of Telemachus (2.260); of Ithaca.
**Refs:** 1.311, 2.169, 2.244, 3.200, 3.216, 9.169, 10.243, 11.466 …and
throughout. Books 1–11, 14, 16, 19, 23.
*See also:* Diomedes, Athena, Thersites, Laertes, Nestor, Achilles, Ithaca.

---

**Oeneus** (Οἰνεύς) · MORTAL — *Say:* EE-nyoos
King of Calydon in the generation before the war, and the root of two of the
poem's houses: Tydeus' father and so Diomedes' grandfather, and the father of
Meleager. It was his failure to give Artemis a harvest-offering from his orchard
slope — "he forgot, or he did not think of it: a great blindness of heart" — that
loosed the Calydonian boar and set the whole Meleager story going; and it is
Oeneus who begs his son at the threshold, one of the suppliants Meleager refuses.
Glaucus and Diomedes discover their inherited guest-friendship through him:
Oeneus once entertained Bellerophon twenty days and gave him a crimson war-belt.
*Kin:* son of Portheus, brother of Agrius and Melas (14.115–118); father of
Tydeus and Meleager; grandfather of Diomedes.
**Refs:** 2.641, 5.813, 6.216, 6.219, 9.534, 9.543, 9.581, 14.117.
Books 2, 5, 6, 9, 10, 14.
*See also:* Tydeus, Diomedes, Meleager, Bellerophon, Glaucus, Calydon, Portheus.

---

**Oenomaus** (Οἰνόμαος) · MORTAL — *Say:* ee-noh-MAY-us
Two men, one on each side. The first is an Achaean, named in the roll-call of
those Hector and Ares strip of their arms in Book 5, between Trechus the Aetolian
and Helenus son of Oenops. The second is a Trojan, one of the captains who storm
the wall around Asius in Book 12; Idomeneus kills him in Book 13 with a spear
mid-belly that bursts the corslet-plate and spills his bowels, and cannot strip
the armor for the missiles flying.
*Killed by:* the Achaean by Hector or Ares (5.706); the Trojan by Idomeneus
(13.506–510).
**Refs:** 5.706, 12.140, 13.506. Books 5, 12, 13.
*See also:* Idomeneus, Asius, Hector.

---

**Olympus** (Ὄλυμπος) · PLACE — *Say:* oh-LIM-pus
The mountain where the gods live, and by extension the divine household itself —
the place Thetis goes to petition, Hera and Athena start from, and Iris is sent
down from. Its gates are kept by the Seasons, who roll the cloud aside (5.749);
Zeus sits apart from the others on its topmost peak; and when he bows his head he
shakes the whole of it (1.530). The poem's assemblies of the gods are held there,
and it is measured against the world below in Zeus's boast that he could hang
earth and sea from a horn of Olympus with all the gods pulling (8.25).
*Epithets:* "snow-capped" (ἀγάννιφος, 1.420); "shining" (αἰγλήεις, 1.532).
*Also called:* Olympian, of the gods who dwell there.
**Refs:** 1.18, 1.420, 1.499, 1.530, 5.750, 8.3, 8.25, 20.5 …and throughout.
Books 1–8, 10–16, 18–22, 24.
*See also:* Zeus, Hera, Thetis, Ida, Iris.

---

**Opoeis** (Ὀποῦς) · PLACE — *Say:* oh-POH-is
A town in Locris, named in the Catalogue among the places the Locrians held, and
otherwise the home of Menoetius and Patroclus. Patroclus was brought out of it as
a small boy, exiled for killing Amphidamas' son in a quarrel over dice, and taken
into Peleus' house; and Achilles admits at 18.324–327 that he promised Menoetius
he would bring the son back to Opoeis with his share of the spoils.
**Refs:** 2.531, 18.326, 23.86. Books 2, 18, 23.
*See also:* Patroclus, Menoetius, Locrians, Amphidamas, Peleus.

---

**Orestes** (Ὀρέστης) · MORTAL — *Say:* oh-RES-teez
Three men. Agamemnon's son, "my late-born boy, reared in high plenty," is named
only as the measure of the honor promised Achilles in the embassy offer — a
son-in-law would be honored his equal — and the phrase is repeated verbatim when
Odysseus relays it. Of the other two, one is an Achaean stripped of his arms by
Hector and Ares in Book 5, "Orestes, driver of horses"; the other a Trojan, in
Asius' storming-party at the wall and killed by Leonteus at 12.193.
*Kin:* Agamemnon's Orestes is his only son, brother of Chrysothemis, Laodice and
Iphianassa.
*Killed by:* the Trojan by Leonteus (12.193).
**Refs:** 5.705, 9.142, 9.284, 12.139, 12.193. Books 5, 9, 12.
*See also:* Agamemnon, Achilles, Leonteus, Asius.

**Orion** (Ὠρίων) · OTHER — *Say:* oh-RY-un
The constellation. Hephaestus sets "the strength of Orion" on the Shield among
the Pleiades, the Hyades and the Bear — the Bear "who turns in her own place,
keeping watch on Orion, / and alone has no share in the baths of Ocean." His name
returns in Book 22 for the star Achilles is compared to as Priam watches him
cross the plain: "the star men call by name the Dog of Orion," brightest of all,
and an evil sign that brings fever on wretched mortals.
**Refs:** 18.486, 18.488, 22.29. Books 18, 22.
*See also:* Achilles, Ocean, Hephaestus.

---

**Othryoneus** (Ὀθρυονεύς) · MORTAL — *Say:* oth-ry-OH-nyoos
A newcomer to Troy from Cabesus, "lately come, chasing the report of the war,"
who asked Priam for Cassandra without bride-gifts and promised in exchange to
drive the Achaeans out of the land; Priam bowed his head to it, and Othryoneus
fought trusting the promise. Idomeneus spears him in mid-stride through the belly
and then jeers over the corpse, offering him instead the loveliest of Agamemnon's
daughters — "you will find we are not hard men over bride-terms" — while dragging
him off by the foot. Deiphobus names him among the lost Trojan leaders at 13.772.
*Killed by:* Idomeneus (13.370–383).
**Refs:** 13.363, 13.369, 13.374, 13.772. Book 13.
*See also:* Idomeneus, Cassandra, Priam, Asius, Deiphobus.

---

**Otrynteus** (Ὀτρυντεύς) · MORTAL — *Say:* oh-TRIN-tyoos
Father of Iphition, the first man Achilles kills on his return to the fighting.
He is a "sacker of cities" whose estate lay by the Gygaean lake in the rich
district of Hyde, under snowy Tmolus, and a naiad nymph bore him the son. He does
not appear himself; Achilles names him twice standing over the split skull —
"Lie there, son of Otrynteus — most terrifying of all men!"
*Kin:* father of Iphition, by a naiad nymph.
**Refs:** 20.383, 20.384, 20.389. Book 20.
*See also:* Iphition, Achilles, Hyde.

---

**Oïleus** (Ὀϊλεύς) · MORTAL — *Say:* oh-EYE-lyoos
Two men. The first is the father of the lesser Ajax, whose name is the standing
way of distinguishing him from Telamon's son — "swift Ajax, Oïleus' son" — and
also the father, by Rhene, of the bastard Medon, who lives in exile at Phylace
for killing a kinsman of his stepmother Eriopis. He is called "sacker of cities"
at 2.728 and never appears in the action. The second is a Trojan charioteer,
"Oïleus, lasher of horses," who leaps down from his car to face Agamemnon and is
killed at once (11.93).
*Kin:* father of Ajax son of Oïleus and, by Rhene, of Medon; husband of Eriopis.
*Killed by:* the charioteer by Agamemnon (11.93–95).
**Refs:** 2.527, 2.727, 2.728, 11.93, 13.694, 13.712, 14.442, 15.333, 23.754.
Books 2, 11–17, 23.
*See also:* Ajax son of Oïleus, Medon, Ajax, Locrians, Agamemnon.

**Paeonians** (Παίονες) · PEOPLE — *Say:* pee-OH-nee-unz
Trojan-side allies from Amydon on the Axius, "Axius, whose water spreads fairest
over the earth," led at first by Pyraechmes and later by Asteropaeus. Patroclus
kills Pyraechmes and panics them; and in Book 21 Achilles, having killed
Asteropaeus, hunts them down the river — Thersilochus, Mydon, Astypylus, Mnesus,
Thrasius, Aenius, Ophelestes — and would have killed more had the river not
called out to him in anger.
*Epithets:* "the Paeonians of the curved bows" (2.848); "the chariot-fighting
Paeonians" (ἱπποκορυσταί, 21.205).
*Kin:* led by Pyraechmes, then by Asteropaeus, son of Pelegon.
**Refs:** 2.848, 10.428, 16.287, 16.291, 21.205, 21.211. Books 2, 10, 16, 21.
*See also:* Pyraechmes, Asteropaeus, Axius, Pelegon, Amydon.

**Paiëon** (Παιήων) · GOD — *Say:* pay-EE-on
The gods' physician, kept distinct from Apollo. He appears twice, in the same two
lines both times: he "sprinkled pain-killing drugs upon the wound / and healed
him: for he was not of mortal make" — first of Hades, shot at Pylos by Heracles,
and then of Ares, when Zeus has finished telling him he is the most hateful of
the gods and orders him healed.
**Refs:** 5.401, 5.899, 5.900. Book 5.
*See also:* Ares, Hades, Zeus, Heracles, Apollo.

**Pandarus** (Πάνδαρος) · MORTAL — *Say:* PAN-duh-rus
The Lycian archer from Zeleia "to whom Apollo himself gave the bow," and the man
whose shot breaks the truce. Athena, in the likeness of Laodocus, persuades him
to shoot Menelaus during the sworn oath; the arrow is deflected by Athena herself
and only grazes, but the oath is broken and the war resumes. In Book 5 Aeneas
finds him and they charge Diomedes together: his spear-cast fails and Diomedes'
answering throw, steered by Athena, goes in beside the nose and cuts his tongue
off at the root.
*Also called:* Lycaon's son — this Lycaon is his father, not Priam's son of the
same name; see `canon.md`.
*Kin:* son of Lycaon.
*Killed by:* Diomedes (5.290–296).
**Refs:** 2.827, 4.88, 5.168, 5.171, 5.246, 5.795. Books 2, 4, 5.
*See also:* Diomedes, Aeneas, Athena, Menelaus, Lycaon, Zeleia.

**Panic** (Φόβος) · GOD
Rout personified, and one of the attendants of Ares — "Panic, his dear son,
strong and fearless, / who sends even the patient-hearted warrior running"
(13.299). He is paired with Terror in the pair's first appearance driving the
armies on (4.440), is figured with Terror around the Gorgon's head on Agamemnon's
shield (11.37), and is ordered with Terror to yoke Ares' horses when Ares means to
avenge Ascalaphus (15.119). The same English also renders Φύζα at 9.2, where the
Achaeans are held by "unearthly Panic, the companion of icy Rout."
*Kin:* son of Ares.
**Refs:** 4.440, 9.2, 11.37, 13.299, 15.119. Books 4, 9, 11, 13, 15.
*See also:* Terror, Ares, Strife, Ascalaphus.

**Panthous** (Πάνθοος) · MORTAL — *Say:* PAN-thoh-us
A Trojan elder, one of the old men sitting with Priam on the wall in Book 3, and
in the rest of the poem a patronymic: "Panthous' son" is Polydamas in most
places, Euphorbus in Book 17, and Hyperenor at 17.24. Menelaus, having killed
Euphorbus, notes that he has now killed three of the line and that Panthous and
his wife Phrontis got the arms of none of them; the family is called "the pride of
the sons of Panthous with their good ash spears."
*Kin:* husband of Phrontis; father of Polydamas, Euphorbus and Hyperenor.
**Refs:** 3.146, 14.450, 16.808, 17.9, 17.23, 17.40, 17.59, 18.250.
Books 3, 13–18.
*See also:* Polydamas, Euphorbus, Hyperenor, Menelaus, Priam, Phrontis.

**Paphlagonians** (Παφλαγόνες) · PEOPLE — *Say:* paf-luh-GOH-nee-unz
Trojan-side allies under Pylaemenes "of the shaggy heart," from the Eneti "where
the breed of wild mules is born," holding Cytorus, Sesamus, Cromna, Aegialus and
the heights of Erythini around the river Parthenius. In Book 13 they carry the
body of Harpalion off the field toward Ilion, his father walking among them in
tears with no blood-price for his son; Paris, who had been the dead man's
guest-friend among them, shoots in anger for him.
*Kin:* led by Pylaemenes.
**Refs:** 2.851, 13.656, 13.661. Books 2, 13.
*See also:* Pylaemenes, Harpalion, Paris.

---

**Paris** (Πάρις) · MORTAL
Priam's son, whose taking of Helen is the cause of the war and who is the least
admired man in the poem. He challenges the Achaean champions in Book 3, loses his
nerve when Menelaus comes forward, is shamed back by Hector, and is snatched out
of the losing duel by Aphrodite and set down in his scented chamber. He refuses
in assembly to give Helen back, offering only the goods (7.362–364). His weapon
is the bow: he shoots Diomedes through the foot and laughs, cripples Machaon,
kills Euchenor, and hits Nestor's horse — and it is Paris, with Apollo, whom the
dying Hector names as Achilles' killer to come. The translation keeps both of his
names as the Greek gives them, per the Book 3 policy.
*Epithets:* "godlike" (θεοειδής, 3.16); "brilliant" (δῖος, 7.355); recurrently
"the husband of lovely-haired Helen" (3.329, 11.369). Hector's "Paris — evil
Paris" renders Δύσπαρι (3.39).
*Also called:* Alexandros — one man, both names in use; Priam's son.
*Kin:* son of Priam and Hecuba; brother of Hector, Helenus, Deiphobus and the
rest; husband of Helen.
**Refs:** 3.16, 3.39, 3.380, 6.321, 6.503, 7.362, 11.369, 22.359 …and throughout.
Books 3–8, 11–13, 15, 22, 24.
*See also:* Helen, Hector, Menelaus, Aphrodite, Priam, Achilles.

**Patroclus** (Πάτροκλος) · MORTAL — *Say:* puh-TROH-klus
Achilles' companion, and the hinge of the poem. He is silent through the first
half — leading Briseis out of the hut at Achilles' order (1.345), receiving the
embassy, sent to Nestor to ask after the wounded Machaon — and it is Nestor's plan
that he take Achilles' armor and go out himself that he brings back in tears.
Achilles grants it with a limit; Patroclus exceeds it, drives the Trojans from
the ships, kills Sarpedon, and charges the wall four times. Apollo strikes him
from behind, knocks off helmet and corslet, Euphorbus spears him between the
shoulders, and Hector finishes him — his dying words the prophecy of Hector's own
death. His body is fought over through Book 17, his ghost comes to Achilles for
burial in Book 23, and his bones are to lie in one jar with Achilles'.
*Epithets:* "Zeus-sprung" (διογενής, 1.337).
*Also called:* Menoetius' son.
*Kin:* son of Menoetius of Opoeis, an exile in Peleus' house from boyhood.
*Kills:* Sarpedon (16.480–491), Pyraechmes, and many others.
*Killed by:* Hector, with Apollo and Euphorbus (16.783–822).
**Refs:** 1.337, 11.602, 16.2, 16.49, 16.480, 16.787, 16.822, 23.65 …and
throughout. Books 1, 8, 9, 11, 15–19, 21–24.
*See also:* Achilles, Menoetius, Hector, Sarpedon, Euphorbus, Nestor, Opoeis,
Automedon.

**Pedasus** (Πήδασος) · PLACE — *Say:* PED-uh-sus
Two towns and, in Book 16, a horse. The Trojan-side Pedasus is "steep Pedasus on
the Satnioeis," the town of Altes lord of the Lelegians, Laothoe's father;
Agamemnon kills Elatus there in Book 6, and Aeneas recalls Achilles sacking it
with Lyrnessus. A second Pedasus, "Pedasus of the vines," is one of the seven
citadels near sandy Pylos that Agamemnon offers Achilles. The horse Pedasus is
Achilles' trace-horse, taken when he sacked Eëtion's city, "a horse who, mortal
as he was, kept pace with immortal horses"; Sarpedon's cast kills him. A man of
the name, twin of Aesepus, is killed by Euryalus at 6.21.
**Refs:** 6.21, 6.35, 9.152, 9.294, 16.152, 16.467, 20.92, 21.87.
Books 6, 9, 16, 20, 21.
*See also:* Altes, Laothoe, Satnioeis, Achilles, Xanthus, Balius, Sarpedon.

**Peirithous** (Πειρίθοος) · MORTAL — *Say:* py-RITH-oh-us
The Lapith king of the back-story, first of the giant men of Nestor's Book 1
reminiscence — "strongest they grew of all men reared on earth" — who fought and
destroyed the beast-men of the mountains. Zeus names him in Book 14 among his own
sons by mortal women, born of Ixion's wife. He does not come to Troy: in the
Catalogue and at the wall the name marks his son Polypoetes, born to him by
Hippodameia on the day he drove the shaggy beast-men out of Pelion.
*Kin:* son of Zeus by Ixion's wife; father of Polypoetes by Hippodameia.
**Refs:** 1.263, 2.741, 2.742, 12.129, 12.182, 14.318. Books 1, 2, 12, 14.
*See also:* Polypoetes, Nestor, Lapiths, Zeus, Leonteus.

**Peiroos** (Πείροος) · MORTAL — *Say:* PY-roh-os
Leader of the Thracians with Acamas in the Catalogue, son of Imbrasus, come from
Aenus. In the closing exchange of Book 4 he kills Diores with a jagged stone that
crushes both tendons and the bones of his shin, then runs up and spears him
beside the navel; and as he springs back Thoas the Aetolian catches him in the
chest above the nipple, wrenches the spear out, and finishes him with the sword.
The Thracians save the body; the two dead men are left stretched beside each
other. His son Rhigmus is killed by Achilles at 20.485, where the printed text
spells the father "Peiros."
*Kin:* son of Imbrasus; father of Rhigmus.
*Kills:* Diores (4.517–526). *Killed by:* Thoas (4.527–531).
**Refs:** 2.844, 4.520, 4.525, 4.527. Books 2, 4.
*See also:* Diores, Thoas, Acamas, Thracians, Rhigmus.

**Peisander** (Πείσανδρος) · MORTAL — *Say:* py-SAN-der
Three men. The first is a son of Antimachus — the man who took Paris' gold and
argued against giving Helen back — caught with his brother Hippolochus in a
bolting chariot; the two beg Agamemnon for ransom, and he kills them both for
their father's counsel, spearing Peisander in the chest. The second is a Trojan
who comes at Menelaus in Book 13, breaks his spear on the shield, pulls a bronze
axe from under it, and is struck at the base of the nose so that his eyeballs
drop in the dust at his feet. The third is a Myrmidon captain, Maemalus' son,
best of them all with the spear after Patroclus.
*Kin:* the first is Antimachus' son and Hippolochus' brother; the Myrmidon is
Maemalus' son.
*Killed by:* Agamemnon (11.143–144); the Trojan by Menelaus (13.615–618).
**Refs:** 11.122, 11.143, 13.601, 13.606, 13.611, 13.614, 16.193.
Books 11, 13, 16.
*See also:* Antimachus, Hippolochus, Agamemnon, Menelaus, Patroclus.

**Pelasgians** (Πελασγοί) · PEOPLE — *Say:* puh-LAZ-jee-unz
Trojan-side allies, "the tribes of the Pelasgian spearmen" from deep-soiled
Larisa, led in the Catalogue by Hippothous and Pylaeus, the two sons of Lethus
son of Teutamus; Dolon places them among the allies camped toward the sea. In
Book 17 Hippothous is killed dragging Patroclus' body by the foot. The name is
also used adjectivally of things not this people: "Pelasgian Argos" is Achilles'
Thessalian country (2.681), and Achilles prays to "Zeus — lord — Dodonaean —
Pelasgian" (16.233).
*Kin:* led by Hippothous and Pylaeus, sons of Lethus.
**Refs:** 2.681, 2.840, 2.843, 10.429, 16.233, 17.288. Books 2, 10, 16, 17.

**Pelegon** (Πηλεγών) · MORTAL — *Say:* PEL-uh-gon
Father of Asteropaeus, and the link between the Paeonian contingent and the river
Axius: the river begot him on Periboea, eldest daughter of Acessamenus. He does
not appear; Asteropaeus gives the descent himself when Achilles asks who he is,
in the exchange before the duel in the river — "he fathered Pelegon, famed with

*Kin:* son of the river Axius and Periboea; father of Asteropaeus.
**Refs:** 21.141, 21.152, 21.159. Book 21.
*See also:* Asteropaeus, Axius, Paeonians, Achilles, Periboea.

**Peleus** (Πηλεύς) · MORTAL — *Say:* PEE-lee-us
Achilles' father, king of the Myrmidons, and in the great majority of its 146
occurrences a patronymic: "Peleus' son" is Achilles, and the name's work in the
poem is to keep saying whose son he is, beginning with the first line. Peleus
himself stays in Phthia. He is present as a voice in other men's memories —
Odysseus and Phoenix and Nestor all quote his parting charge, "always to be the
best, and to hold his head above all others" (11.784) — and as an argument: he
took Patroclus in as an exiled boy and raised him; his marriage to Thetis is the
gift the gods gave and the wound they left; and Achilles' surrender of Hector's
body is won when Priam makes him think of his own father, old and alone with one
son doomed out of season.
*Epithets:* "the old horseman Peleus" (7.125).
*Also called:* Aeacus' son — the fixed rendering of the bare patronymic for
Peleus, as "Aeacus' grandson" is Achilles; see `canon.md`.
*Kin:* son of Aeacus; husband of Thetis; father of Achilles; foster-father of
Patroclus.
**Refs:** 1.1, 9.252, 9.438, 11.769, 11.783, 16.33, 18.84, 24.534 …and
throughout. Books 1, 2, 7–11, 13, 15–24.
*See also:* Achilles, Thetis, Aeacus, Patroclus, Phoenix, Phthia, Priam.

**Pelion** (Πήλιον) · PLACE — *Say:* PEE-lee-on
The Thessalian mountain, home country of the Magnetes and the place from which
Peirithous drove the beast-men to the Aethices. Its use in the poem is chiefly
adjectival: "the Pelian ash that Chiron gave his dear father / from the peak of
Pelion, to be death for heroes" is Achilles' spear, the one weapon Patroclus
cannot take because no other Achaean can swing it. Achilles takes it out of its
case in Book 19 and carries it against Aeneas, Asteropaeus and Hector.
**Refs:** 2.744, 2.757, 16.143, 16.144, 19.390, 19.391, 20.277, 21.162, 22.133.
Books 2, 16, 19–22.
*See also:* Achilles, Chiron, Peleus, Peirithous, Peneius, Magnetes.

**Peneius** (Πηνειός) · PLACE — *Say:* puh-NEE-us
A Thessalian river of the Catalogue, named for the marvel at its confluence: the
Titaressus pours its water into Peneius but does not mingle with his silver
eddies, and "flows along on top of him like oil," being a branch broken off the
water of Styx, the dread oath. The Magnetes under Prothoos dwell around Peneius
and Pelion.
**Refs:** 2.752, 2.753, 2.757. Book 2.
*See also:* Titaressus, Styx, Pelion, Prothoos, Magnetes.

**Peneleos** (Πηνέλεως) · MORTAL — *Say:* puh-NEL-ee-os
One of the five Boeotian leaders of the Catalogue, and a steady presence in the
fighting at the ships. His killing of Ilioneus in Book 14 is the poem's most
gruesome vaunt: he spears him through the eye-socket and out the nape, hews the
head off with the sword, and holds it up on the spear-point "like a poppy-head"
to show the Trojans. He kills Lycon in Book 16 with a sword-stroke to the neck
that leaves the head hanging by skin, and in Book 17 he is wounded in the
shoulder by Polydamas' spear and leads the rout.
*Kills:* Ilioneus (14.493–500); Lycon (16.339–341).
**Refs:** 2.494, 13.92, 14.487, 14.489, 14.496, 16.335, 16.339, 17.597.
Books 2, 13, 14, 16, 17.
*See also:* Boeotians, Leïtus, Ilioneus, Lycon, Polydamas.

**Percote** (Περκώτη) · PLACE — *Say:* pur-KOH-tee
A town on the Asian side of the Hellespont, on the Trojan side of the war. Merops
of Percote, "who beyond all men knew the seer's art," foresaw the deaths of his
sons Adrestus and Amphius and could not keep them from the war; the poem repeats
his lines verbatim when Diomedes and Odysseus kill the pair at 11.329–332. Asius
son of Hyrtacus left his ships there and came the rest of the way to Ilion on
foot; Odysseus kills Pidytes of Percote in Book 6; and Melanippus herded cattle
there until the Achaean ships came.
**Refs:** 2.831, 2.835, 6.30, 11.229, 11.329, 15.548. Books 2, 6, 11, 15.
*See also:* Merops, Adrestus, Amphius, Asius, Melanippus, Hellespont.

**Pergamos** (Πέργαμος) · PLACE — *Say:* PUR-guh-mos
Troy's citadel, the high ground inside the city, and Apollo's station. He looks
down from it to shout the Trojans back into the fight at 4.508, sets the wounded
Aeneas down "in holy Pergamos, where his own temple stood" to be healed by Leto
and Artemis while a phantom of him fights on below, and sits on its height to
send Ares after Diomedes. Paris comes down "from the height of Pergamos, blazing
in his armor like the beaming sun" to rejoin Hector at 6.513.
**Refs:** 4.508, 5.446, 5.460, 6.513, 7.21. Books 4–7.
*See also:* Troy, Ilion, Apollo, Aeneas, Paris.

**Periphas** (Περίφας) · MORTAL — *Say:* PER-ih-fas
Two men. The first is "gigantic Periphas, far the best of the Aetolians,
Ochesius' shining son," killed by Ares in Book 5; Ares is stripping the body when
he sees Diomedes coming and leaves it lying where it fell. The second is a Trojan
herald, Epytus' son, who had grown grey in his heraldry at his old father's side,
"his heart a well of kindly counsel"; Apollo takes his shape to stir Aeneas at
17.324.
*Kin:* the Aetolian is Ochesius' son; the herald is Epytus' son.
*Killed by:* the Aetolian by Ares (5.842–848).
**Refs:** 5.842, 5.847, 17.324. Books 5, 17.
*See also:* Ares, Diomedes, Aeneas, Apollo.

**Peteos** (Πετεώς) · MORTAL — *Say:* PEE-tee-os
Father of Menestheus, the Athenian leader, and himself Zeus-nurtured. He does not
appear: in every occurrence the name is a patronymic marking his son — in the
Catalogue, in Agamemnon's tour of the ranks where Menestheus is rebuked for
hanging back, at the wall where "Menestheus, Peteos' son, shuddered to see them"
and sent Thootes for help, and in the muster of the Athenians in Book 13.
*Kin:* father of Menestheus.
**Refs:** 2.552, 4.327, 4.338, 12.331, 12.355, 13.690. Books 2, 4, 12, 13.
*See also:* Menestheus, Athenians.

**Phaenops** (Φαίνοψ) · MORTAL — *Say:* FEE-nops
The name of more than one Trojan father, and each time the poem uses it to weigh
a death. Xanthus and Thoon, "both late-born and beloved," are killed together by
Diomedes, leaving their father "worn with hard age" no other son, so that kinsmen
of the widowed house divide the estate; Phorcys, Phaenops' son, is killed by
Telamonian Ajax over Hippothous' body. A third Phaenops is Asius' son of Abydos,
Hector's dearest guest-friend, whose likeness Apollo borrows at 17.583 to shame
Hector for flinching from Menelaus.
*Kin:* father of Xanthus and Thoon (5.152); father of Phorcys (17.312); the
guest-friend is Asius' son.
**Refs:** 5.152, 17.312, 17.583. Books 5, 17.
*See also:* Xanthus, Phorcys, Asius, Abydos, Apollo, Hector.

**Phocians** (Φωκῆες) · PEOPLE — *Say:* FOH-shunz
A Catalogue contingent of forty ships from Cyparissus, Pytho, Crisa, Daulis,
Panopeus and the towns along the Cephisus, led by Schedius and Epistrophus, sons
of Iphitus; they arm on the left, next to the Boeotians. Both their named
casualties are called captains of the Phocians: Schedius son of Perimedes, killed
by Hector at the ships, and Schedius son of Iphitus, "far the best of the
Phocians," killed by Hector's cast in Book 17.
*Kin:* led by Schedius and Epistrophus, Iphitus' sons.
**Refs:** 2.517, 15.516, 17.307. Books 2, 15, 17.
*See also:* Schedius, Epistrophus, Panopeus, Boeotians, Achaeans.

**Phoenix** (Φοῖνιξ) · MORTAL — *Say:* FEE-niks
Achilles' old tutor, sent from Phthia by Peleus with the boy to teach him "to be
a speaker of words, and a doer of deeds"; he had fled his own father Amyntor's
house and been received by Peleus, who made him lord of the Dolopes at the edge
of Phthia. Agamemnon names him first of the embassy, and his is its longest and
central speech — the story of his own exile, the allegory of the Prayers limping
after Blindness, and the tale of Meleager. Achilles refuses the honor but keeps
him to sleep in the hut and sail home with him. Later he leads the fourth
Myrmidon company, is among those who try to comfort Achilles in Book 19, and
serves as umpire at the turn-post in the chariot race. Athena takes his likeness
to rouse Menelaus over Patroclus' body (17.555).
*Kin:* son of Amyntor; foster-father of Achilles; called "his own father's
follower," i.e. Peleus' (23.360). The "far-famed Phoenix" whose daughter Zeus
loved (14.321) is a different figure, the Phoenician eponym.
**Refs:** 9.168, 9.432, 9.607, 9.621, 16.196, 17.555, 19.311, 23.360. Books 9,
14, 16, 17, 19, 23.
*See also:* Achilles, Peleus, Phthia, Meleager, Dolopes, Menelaus.

**Phrygia** (Φρυγίη) · PLACE — *Say:* FRIJ-ee-uh
A land inland from Troy, allied to it and tied to Priam's house. Priam recalls
going there as an ally in his youth — "Phrygia of the vines," the hosts of Otreus
and Mygdon camped along the Sangarius, on the day the Amazons came — and Hecuba's
brother Asius, Dymas' son, lives there on the streams of Sangarius. Paris offers
Helen no woman as lovely "in the cities of Phrygia or lovely Maeonia"; Hector says
Troy's heirlooms have been sold off to Phrygia and Maeonia; and Achilles counts
Phrygia inland among the bounds of Priam's old prosperity.
**Refs:** 3.184, 3.401, 16.719, 18.291, 24.545. Books 3, 16, 18, 24.
*See also:* Phrygians, Sangarius, Priam, Hecuba, Maeonia, Amazons.

---

**Phthia** (Φθίη) · PLACE — *Say:* FTHY-uh *or* THY-uh
Achilles' homeland in Thessaly, Peleus' kingdom and the Myrmidons' country,
called "deep-soiled Phthia, feeder of men" and "mother of flocks." It is the
poem's standing image of the alternative to Troy: Achilles threatens in Book 1 to
sail home to it, and in Book 9 sets out the choice plainly — three days' sail and
a long life without fame, or death here with imperishable fame. Phoenix and
Patroclus were both received there by Peleus, and both the embassy and the
funeral looking-back turn on the day the two of them were sent out of Phthia to
Agamemnon.
**Refs:** 1.155, 1.169, 2.683, 9.363, 9.439, 9.479, 11.766, 19.330. Books 1, 2,
9, 11, 16, 19.
*See also:* Achilles, Peleus, Myrmidons, Hellas, Phoenix, Patroclus, Phthians.

**Phthians** (Φθῖοι) · PEOPLE — *Say:* FTHY-unz *or* THY-unz
Men of Phthia, named only in the Book 13 muster of the contingents holding Hector
off the ships, where they stand beside the Boeotians with the Ionians, Locrians
and Epeians. Their leaders there are not Achilles' Myrmidons but Medon — Oïleus'
bastard son, exiled to Phylace for killing a kinsman of his stepmother Eriopis —
and Podarces son of Iphiclus.
*Kin:* led at 13.693 by Medon and Podarces.
**Refs:** 13.686, 13.693, 13.699. Book 13.
*See also:* Phthia, Medon, Podarces, Boeotians, Myrmidons.

**Phyleus** (Φυλεύς) · MORTAL — *Say:* FY-lyoos
Father of Meges, "the horseman Phyleus dear to Zeus," who had once quarreled with
his own father and moved away to Dulichium. He does not come to Troy: the name is
almost always a patronymic for Meges, in the Catalogue, in the night muster, in
the Epeian ranks at 13.692, and through the fighting at the ships in Book 15,
where the corslet Phyleus brought home from Ephyra by the river Selleïs — a
guest-gift from Euphetes — turns Dolops' spear and keeps destruction from his
son's flesh. A Phyleus is also named among the men Nestor beat with the spear at
Amarynceus' funeral games (23.637).
*Kin:* father of Meges. The poem does not name Phyleus's own father, only that he
quarreled with him and left for Dulichium.
**Refs:** 2.628, 5.72, 10.110, 13.692, 15.519, 15.530, 16.313, 19.239, 23.637.
Books 2, 5, 10, 13, 15, 16, 19, 23.
*See also:* Meges, Dulichium, Epeians, Dolops, Ephyra, Nestor.

**Placus** (Πλάκος) · PLACE — *Say:* PLAY-kus
A wooded mountain in the Troad, under which stood Thebe, Eëtion's city. The name
comes only in the formula for Andromache's origin — "Eëtion, who lived under
wooded Placus, / in Thebe-under-Placus, lord over the Cilician men" — used at
their meeting at the Scaean Gates, again when she names her mother "queen under
wooded Placus," and a third time in her lament over Hector, where the two of them
are born to one fate, "you in Troy, in the house of Priam, / and I in Thebe,
under wooded Placus."
**Refs:** 6.396, 6.397, 6.425, 22.479. Books 6, 22.
*See also:* Thebe, Eëtion, Andromache, Cilicians.

**Pleiades** (Πληϊάδες) · OTHER
A constellation, named once: among the stars Hephaestus works into the Shield of

**Refs:** 18.486. Book 18.

**Pleuron** (Πλευρών) · PLACE — *Say:* PLOOR-on
An Aetolian city, named with Olenus and Pylene in the Catalogue of Thoas'
contingent and twice paired with "steep Calydon" — in Poseidon's disguise as
Thoas, who "in all Pleuron and steep Calydon ruled the Aetolians, honored like a
god," and in Diomedes' genealogy, where Portheus' three sons Agrius, Melas and
Oeneus lived there. Nestor names Ancaeus of Pleuron as the wrestler he beat at
Amarynceus' funeral games.
**Refs:** 2.639, 13.217, 14.116, 23.635. Books 2, 13, 14, 23.

**Podargus** (Πόδαργος) · OTHER — *Say:* poh-DAR-gus
Two horses of the name, and the poem keeps them apart. The first is one of
Hector's four, called by name with Xanthus, Aethon and Lampus when he tells them
to pay him back for the honey-hearted wheat Andromache set before them. The
second is Menelaus' own trace-horse in the chariot race at Patroclus' funeral,
yoked beside Aethe, Agamemnon's mare.
**Refs:** 8.185, 23.295. Books 8, 23.

**Polites** (Πολίτης) · MORTAL — *Say:* poh-LY-teez
A son of Priam, and the Trojan lookout: he sat "on the very top of the tomb of
old Aesyetes, watching for when the Achaeans should rush out from their ships,"
trusting the speed of his feet, and Iris takes his shape to bring Zeus's message
to the assembly at Priam's doors. In battle he carries his wounded brother
Deiphobus out of the fighting to the chariots and kills Echius at the ships. He is
one of the nine sons Priam curses and drives to yoke the wagon in Book 24, where
he is "Polites of the war-cry."
*Kin:* son of Priam; brother of Deiphobus.
*Kills:* Echius (15.339).
**Refs:** 2.791, 13.533, 15.339, 24.250. Books 2, 13, 15, 24.
*See also:* Priam, Deiphobus, Iris, Aesyetes.

**Polydamas** (Πουλυδάμας) · MORTAL — *Say:* pol-ih-DAY-mas *or* puh-LID-uh-mas
Panthous' son, Hector's companion, born in the same night with him: "one was far
the better in words, the other with the spear." He is the poem's counter-voice on
the Trojan side, and Hector takes his advice three times and refuses it twice, the
refusals being the ones that matter. He reads the eagle-and-snake omen at the
ditch and urges the Trojans not to press on to the ships, and Hector answers with
"One bird-sign is best: to fight for your fatherland" and a threat to kill him.
He urges retreat inside the walls the night Achilles reappears, and Hector's
refusal — praised by the Trojans, "the fools: for Pallas Athena had taken their
wits from them" — is what Hector remembers alone before the gates: "Polydamas
will be the first to lay reproach on me." He fights as well as he counsels,
killing Prothoenor and Otus and vaunting over the body.
*Also called:* Panthous' son.
*Kin:* son of Panthous; brother, in the family the poem names, of Euphorbus and
Hyperenor.
*Kills:* Prothoenor (14.450); Mecisteus (15.339); Otus of Cyllene (15.518).
**Refs:** 12.60, 12.210, 12.231, 13.756, 14.450, 15.518, 18.249, 18.313, 22.100
…and throughout. Books 11–18, 22.
*See also:* Hector, Panthous, Euphorbus, Prothoenor, Ajax, Meges.

**Polydorus** (Πολύδωρος) · MORTAL — *Say:* pol-ih-DOR-us
Priam's youngest and dearest son, by Laothoe daughter of Altes, forbidden by his
father to fight; "in his boy's folly, showing off the speed of his feet" he darts
through the front-fighters and Achilles' javelin takes him in the back, so that
he sinks clutching his bowels. It is the sight of his brother dying that brings
Hector out of the crowd against Achilles. Lycaon names him as the brother already
killed while pleading for his own life at 21.91, and Priam names the two of them
together at 22.46 as the sons of Laothoe he cannot see among the Trojans. A
different Polydorus is one of the men Nestor out-threw with the spear at
Amarynceus' funeral games (23.637); the poem keeps them separate.
*Kin:* son of Priam and Laothoe; brother of Lycaon.
*Killed by:* Achilles (20.407–418).
**Refs:** 20.407, 20.419, 21.91, 22.46, 23.637. Books 20–23.
*See also:* Priam, Laothoe, Lycaon, Altes, Achilles, Hector, Nestor.

**Polyidus** (Πολύϊδος) · MORTAL — *Say:* pol-ih-EYE-dus
Two men. The first is a Trojan, son of Eurydamas "the old reader of dreams,"
killed with his brother Abas by Diomedes in Book 5 — and the poem's point is that
"the old man read no dreams for them at their going." The second is a seer of
Corinth, father of Euchenor, who told his son often that he would either waste to
death of a sickness at home or fall under Trojan hands at the ships; Euchenor
sailed knowing his fate, and Paris' arrow found him under the ear.
*Kin:* the Trojan is Eurydamas' son and Abas' brother; the Corinthian is
Euchenor's father.
*Killed by:* the Trojan Polyidus by Diomedes (5.148–151).
**Refs:** 5.148, 13.663, 13.666. Books 5, 13.
*See also:* Eurydamas, Abas, Euchenor, Diomedes, Paris.

**Polypoetes** (Πολυποίτης) · MORTAL — *Say:* pol-ih-PEE-teez
A Lapith leader, son of Peirithous and Hippodameia, born on the day his father
drove the shaggy beast-men out of Pelion; he brings forty ships from Argissa and
Gyrtone with Leonteus, and the two are inseparable in the poem. They hold the gate
against Asius "like high-crowned oaks in the mountains that abide the wind and
rain through all their days," and Polypoetes kills Damasus through the
bronze-cheeked helmet, then Pylon and Ormenus. In Book 23 he throws the lump of
iron past the whole field — Epeius, Leonteus and Telamonian Ajax — and his
companions carry the prize away.
*Epithets:* "steadfast in battle" (μενεπτόλεμος, 2.740).
*Kin:* son of Peirithous and Hippodameia; grandson of Zeus by Peirithous.
*Kills:* Astyalus (6.29); Damasus, Pylon, Ormenus (12.182–187).
**Refs:** 2.740, 6.29, 12.129, 12.182, 23.836, 23.844, 23.848. Books 2, 6, 12,
23.
*See also:* Leonteus, Peirithous, Lapiths, Asius, Epeius, Ajax.

**Poseidon** (Ποσειδάων) · GOD — *Say:* poh-SY-dun
Zeus's brother, lord of the sea and the Achaeans' most active divine partisan. He
and Apollo built Laomedon's wall and were cheated of their wage, which he still
holds against Troy in Book 21, when he offers Apollo the first blow and is refused.
His great sequence is Books 13–15: he watches from the peak of Thracian Samos,
comes down in three strides, and works behind Zeus's back — rallying the Argives
as Calchas, leading them with a sword "like a lightning-flash," then stretching
the strife taut against Hector — until Zeus sends Iris to order him off and he
yields, protesting that the three brothers drew sky, sea and underworld by lot and
he is Zeus's equal in honor. He resents the Achaean wall for outshining his own
work and unbuilds it after the war; he saves Aeneas from Achilles because the race
of Dardanus must not perish; and he alone with Hera and Athena holds to his hatred
of Ilion to the end.
*Epithets:* "the earth-shaker" (ἐνοσίχθων / ἐννοσίγαιος, 7.445); "wide-ruling"
(εὐρυσθενής, 7.455); "the holder of the earth" (γαιήοχος, 9.183); "blue-maned"
(κυανοχαίτης, 13.563).
*Kin:* son of Cronos and Rhea; brother of Zeus and Hades.
**Refs:** 7.445, 12.17, 13.19, 13.43, 14.384, 15.158, 15.205, 20.291, 21.435,
24.26 …and throughout. Books 1, 2, 7, 8, 11–15, 20, 21, 23, 24.
*See also:* Zeus, Hades, Apollo, Laomedon, Aeneas, Samos, Achaeans.

**Priam** (Πρίαμος) · MORTAL — *Say:* PRY-am
King of Troy, old and past fighting, whose name the poem uses as much for the
city and the doom as for the man — "Priam's city," "Priam and the people of Priam
of the good ash spear." He appears rarely in battle books and decisively at the
frame: on the tower in Book 3, calling Helen to him and refusing to blame her, and
driving out through the Scaean Gates to swear the oaths for the duel, then leaving
because he cannot watch his son fight; in Book 22 on the wall, tearing his hair
and begging Hector to come inside, arguing that Achilles too has a father. That
argument he carries into the Achaean camp in Book 24, going alone by night with
Hermes for guide, kissing "the terrible, man-slaughtering hands that had killed so
many of his sons," and winning Hector's body back. He tells Achilles he had fifty
sons when the Achaeans came, and not one is left him.
*Epithets:* "of the good ash spear" (ἐϋμμελίω, 4.47); "the equal of the gods in
counsel" (θεόφιν μήστωρ ἀτάλαντος, 7.366).
*Also called:* Dardanus' descendant (Δαρδανίδης, 3.303).
*Kin:* son of Laomedon; husband of Hecuba, daughter of Dymas, and of many wives
including Laothoe; father of Hector, Paris, Helenus, Deiphobus, Polydorus,
Lycaon, Polites and the rest, and of Laodice and Cassandra.
**Refs:** 1.19, 2.37, 3.105, 3.303, 4.47, 6.242, 7.366, 22.25, 24.160, 24.477
…and throughout. Books 1–9, 11–24.
*See also:* Hector, Hecuba, Laomedon, Troy, Ilion, Achilles, Hermes, Dardanus.

**Proetus** (Προῖτος) · MORTAL — *Say:* PREE-tus
A king of the Argives in Glaucus' back-story, who drove Bellerophon out of the
land. His wife Anteia was mad to lie with Bellerophon and, refused, lied to her
husband that he had forced her; Proetus shrank from killing him — "his heart stood
in awe of that" — but sent him to Lycia with the folded tablet of "many
life-destroying marks" for the king there to read, meaning his death. The poem's
one instance of writing.
*Kin:* husband of Anteia; son-in-law of the lord of Lycia.
**Refs:** 6.157, 6.160, 6.163, 6.164, 6.177. Book 6.
*See also:* Bellerophon, Anteia, Glaucus, Lycia.

**Promachus** (Πρόμαχος) · MORTAL — *Say:* PROM-uh-kus
A Boeotian, Alegenor's son, killed by Acamas in Book 14 as he was hauling
Archelochus' body away by the feet. Acamas vaunts over him — "Look how your
Promachus sleeps, beaten down by my spear" — as blood-price for his brother, and
the vaunt is what rouses Peneleos, whose killing of Ilioneus answers it in kind.
*Kin:* son of Alegenor.
*Killed by:* Acamas (14.476–477).

**Protesilaus** (Πρωτεσίλαος) · MORTAL — *Say:* proh-tes-ih-LAY-us
Leader of the contingent from Phylace, and the first Achaean to die: "a Dardanian
man had killed him as he leapt from his ship, far the first of the Achaeans." He
is already dead when the Catalogue reaches him — "by then the black earth held him
under" — leaving a wife with torn cheeks in Phylace and a half-built house, and
his brother Podarces, the younger and lesser man, leads his forty ships. His ship
becomes the poem's landmark for the crisis at the camp: Hector breaks through
where the ships of Ajax and Protesilaus are drawn up, lays his hand on its stern
at 15.705, and Patroclus' first cast in Book 16 is thrown into the press around
it.
*Kin:* son of Iphiclus, Phylacus' line; brother of Podarces.
**Refs:** 2.698, 2.706, 2.708, 13.681, 15.705, 16.287. Books 2, 13, 15, 16.
*See also:* Podarces, Phylace, Hector, Patroclus, Phthians.

**Prothoenor** (Προθοήνωρ) · MORTAL — *Say:* proh-thoh-EE-nor
One of the five Boeotian leaders of the Catalogue and, in Book 14, Polydamas'
victim: the spear takes him in the right shoulder and he falls clutching the earth
in his palm. Polydamas' vaunt over him — that the man will lean on the spear for a
staff walking down into Hades — stirs Telamonian Ajax, who casts back, misses
Polydamas, and kills Archelochus instead, then asks across the field whether that
man is "a worthy exchange for Prothoenor dead."
*Kin:* son of Areïlycus.
*Killed by:* Polydamas (14.450–452).
**Refs:** 2.495, 14.450, 14.471. Books 2, 14.
*See also:* Polydamas, Ajax, Archelochus, Boeotians.

**Pylaemenes** (Πυλαιμένης) · MORTAL — *Say:* py-LEM-uh-neez
Leader of the Paphlagonians from the Eneti, "of the shaggy heart," called "captain
of the great-hearted Paphlagonian shieldmen." Menelaus spears him through the
collarbone as he stands, while Antilochus kills his driver Mydon in the same
exchange. His son Harpalion is killed by Meriones in Book 13, and the poem there
calls him "the son of king Pylaemenes" — the father, killed in Book 5, is present
again as a name.
*Kin:* father of Harpalion.
*Killed by:* Menelaus (5.576–579).
**Refs:** 2.851, 5.576, 13.643. Books 2, 5, 13.
*See also:* Paphlagonians, Menelaus, Antilochus, Harpalion, Mydon.

---

**Pylians** (Πύλιοι) · PEOPLE — *Say:* PY-lee-unz
Nestor's people, whose land the Alpheius "flows wide through." They are named
mostly inside Nestor's own reminiscences — the fight with the Arcadians by the
Celadon, and the long Book 11 tale of the cattle-raid on Elis, the division of the
spoil at Pylos, and the battle with the Epeians in which Zeus "handed great power
to the Pylians." In the present action they are the contingent Antilochus leaves
when he runs to Achilles with the news of Patroclus' death, and "great longing was
built for the Pylians" until Menelaus sends them Thrasymedes.
**Refs:** 5.545, 7.134, 11.687, 11.737, 11.753, 17.704, 23.633. Books 5, 7, 11,
17, 23.
*See also:* Nestor, Pylos, Antilochus, Thrasymedes, Epeians, Alpheius.

---

**Pylos** (Πύλος) · PLACE — *Say:* PY-los
Nestor's kingdom, "sandy Pylos," Neleus' before him; the Catalogue gives him
ninety ships from Pylos, Arene and the towns along the Alpheius. Nestor was
already king "among the third" generation there when he came to Troy, and the
place belongs mostly to his memories: the cattle driven at night into Neleian
Pylos, the debts divided among the leading men, the return from Buprasion. Agamem-
non offers seven citadels "on the edge of sandy Pylos" among the gifts for
Achilles. Dione names it as where Heracles shot Hades among the dead.
**Refs:** 1.252, 2.77, 2.591, 5.397, 9.153, 11.682, 11.712, 23.303. Books 1, 2,
4, 5, 9, 11, 23.
*See also:* Nestor, Neleus, Pylians, Alpheius, Heracles.

**Rhesus** (Ῥῆσος) · MORTAL — *Say:* REE-sus
Thracian king, son of Eïoneus, whose men are "new-come, outermost of all" — they
stand outside the Catalogue. Dolon, questioned under the knife, gives him up: his
horses are the finest and biggest Dolon has ever seen, "whiter than snow, and like
the winds in running," and his armor is golden, fit for gods and not for mortal
men. Diomedes kills him in his sleep, thirteenth of the Thracians, while Odysseus
leads the horses out; Apollo wakes his kinsman Hippocoon, who finds the place
empty and the men gasping. A river of Ida also bears the name (12.20).
*Kin:* son of Eïoneus; kinsman of Hippocoon.
*Killed by:* Diomedes (10.474–497).
**Refs:** 10.435, 10.474, 10.519, 12.20. Books 10, 12.
*See also:* Dolon, Diomedes, Odysseus, Thracians, Hippocoon.

---

**Rhodes** (Ῥόδος) · PLACE
Tlepolemus' island, sending nine ships of "the lordly Rhodians," who "dwelt
through Rhodes in three divisions: Lindus, Ialysus, and chalk-white Cameirus." He
came there as an exile and a wanderer after killing Licymnius, his father
Heracles' aged uncle, and the settlers were loved by Zeus, who "poured wondrous
wealth upon them."
**Refs:** 2.654, 2.655, 2.667. Book 2.
*See also:* Tlepolemus, Rhodians, Heracles, Licymnius.

---

**Samos** (Σάμος) · PLACE — *Say:* SAY-mos
Two islands of the name, and the poem distinguishes them. The Samos of Odysseus'
Catalogue entry (2.634) lies among Ithaca, Zacynthus and the facing mainland. The
other is "wooded Samos, Thracian Samos" — Samothrace — from whose topmost peak
Poseidon watches the fighting, because from there "all Ida showed clear, and
Priam's city showed clear, and the ships of the Achaeans"; Iris plunges into the
sea between Samos and rugged Imbros, and Priam names Samos with Imbros and Lemnos
as the islands Achilles sold his sons into.
**Refs:** 2.634, 13.12, 13.13, 24.78, 24.753. Books 2, 13, 24.
*See also:* Poseidon, Imbros, Lemnos, Ithaca, Odysseus.

---

**Sarpedon** (Σαρπηδών) · MORTAL — *Say:* sar-PEE-don
King of the Lycians, son of Zeus by Laodameia, Bellerophon's daughter — the only
child of Zeus fighting at Troy whom the poem lets die. He comes "from far Lycia,
from the eddying Xanthus" with Glaucus, rebukes Hector for leaving the fighting to
the allies, survives Tlepolemus' spear through the thigh in Book 5, and is the
first man over the Achaean wall, tearing the breastwork away with his hands "and
he had made a road for many." His speech to Glaucus before that assault — why kings
are honored with the seat of honor and the fat land, and why, since death stands
over them in thousands, they must go — is the poem's clearest statement of the
warrior's bargain. Patroclus kills him with a spear through the midriff and he
falls like a felled oak; Zeus, who has weighed snatching him out alive and been
argued down by Hera, rains blood on the earth for him, then has Apollo wash the
body and give it to Sleep and Death to carry to Lycia. The fight over his stripped
corpse fills the rest of Book 16, and his armor is the prize Achilles sets for the
duel in the funeral games.
*Epithets:* "of the bronze helmet" (χαλκοκορυστής, 6.199).
*Kin:* son of Zeus and Laodameia; grandson of Bellerophon; cousin and companion
of Glaucus.
*Kills:* Tlepolemus (5.655–659); Alcmaon, Thestor's son (12.394). In the duel his
own casts miss Patroclus twice; the second kills the trace-horse Pedasus (16.466).
*Killed by:* Patroclus (16.480–505).
**Refs:** 2.876, 5.633, 5.657, 6.199, 12.292, 12.397, 16.419, 16.480, 16.667,
23.800 …and throughout. Books 2, 5, 6, 12, 14–17, 23.
*See also:* Glaucus, Lycians, Zeus, Patroclus, Tlepolemus, Bellerophon, Hector.

**Satnioeis** (Σατνιόεις) · PLACE — *Say:* sat-nee-OH-is
A river of the Troad, always named for the men who lived on it: Elatus, killed by
Agamemnon, "who lived by the banks of fair-flowing Satnioeis in steep Pedasus";
Satnius, son of Enops by a nymph who bore him "as he herded by the banks of
Satnioeis," killed by Ajax son of Oïleus; and Pedasus, the town Altes holds "on
the Satnioeis," home of Laothoe, mother of Lycaon and Polydorus.
**Refs:** 6.34, 14.445, 21.87. Books 6, 14, 21.
*See also:* Pedasus, Elatus, Satnius, Altes, Laothoe.

**Scaean** (Σκαιαί) · PLACE — *Say:* SEE-un
The Scaean Gates, Troy's main gate onto the plain, and with the oak beside them
the fixed landmark of the poem's approaches to the city. The elders sit there
watching Helen come up onto the tower; Priam drives out through them to swear the
oaths; Hector meets Andromache there on his way out and it is where Theano's
prayer asks Athena to bring Diomedes down; Achilles' chase brings Hector back to
them, "in front of Ilion and the Scaean Gates," and the dying Hector names them as
the place where Paris and Apollo will destroy Achilles.
*Also called:* the Scaean Gates.
**Refs:** 3.145, 3.149, 3.263, 6.237, 6.307, 6.393, 9.354, 11.170, 16.712,
18.453, 22.6, 22.360. Books 3, 6, 9, 11, 16, 18, 22.
*See also:* Troy, Ilion, Hector, Andromache, Priam, Achilles.

**Scamander** (Σκάμανδρος) · PLACE — *Say:* skuh-MAN-dur
Troy's chief river and the god who runs in it, "whom the gods call Xanthus, and
men Scamander" — the poem states the double name outright at 20.74. It is the
plain's standing feature: Ares set down on its high bank, the burial-ground of the
Achaean dead, the ford Priam and Hermes cross, the twin springs where the Trojan
women used to wash and past which Achilles chases Hector. In Book 21 the river
becomes a combatant: choked with corpses, he asks Achilles to do his killing on
the plain, then crests over him, calls his brother Simoïs to help drown him, and
is beaten only when Hera sends Hephaestus' fire against the water. He is one of
the eight Idaean rivers Apollo and Poseidon turn against the Achaean wall (12.21).
*Also called:* Xanthus — the gods' name for it (20.74). NB: "Xanthus" also names
one of Achilles' immortal horses, a Trojan killed by Diomedes (5.152), and a
separate river of Lycia (2.877, 6.172, 12.313).
*Kin:* "eddying Xanthus, whom immortal Zeus begot" (21.2).
**Refs:** 5.36, 5.78, 7.330, 11.499, 12.21, 20.74, 21.15, 21.223, 21.305, 22.148
…and throughout. Books 5–8, 11–12, 14, 20–22, 24.
*See also:* Simois, Achilles, Hephaestus, Hera, Xanthus, Troy.

**Schedius** (Σχεδίος) · MORTAL — *Say:* SKEE-dee-us
Two Phocian leaders of the name, both killed by Hector. The first is Iphitus' son,
Naubolus' grandson, who with his brother Epistrophus leads forty Phocian ships in
the Catalogue; he is "far the best of the Phocians," king over many men in famed
Panopeus, and Hector's cast — dodged by Ajax — takes him under the collarbone and
comes out at the base of the shoulder. The second is Schedius son of Perimedes,
"a captain of the Phocians," cut down by Hector at the ships in Book 15.
*Kin:* the Catalogue leader is son of Iphitus; the other is son of Perimedes.
*Killed by:* both by Hector (15.515; 17.306–311).
**Refs:** 2.517, 15.515, 17.306. Books 2, 15, 17.
*See also:* Phocians, Epistrophus, Panopeus, Hector, Ajax.

**Seasons** (Ὧραι) · GOD
The Ὧραι, keepers of the gates of heaven, "to whom great heaven and Olympus are
entrusted, to roll the thick cloud aside, or set it back." They appear only in the
formula for Hera's chariot passing out and back: the gates groan open self-moved
as she and Athena drive through (5.749 = 8.393), and when the ride is called off
they unyoke the lovely-maned horses, tether them at the ambrosial mangers and lean
the chariot against the shining wall.
**Refs:** 5.749, 8.393, 8.433. Books 5, 8.
*See also:* Hera, Athena, Olympus, Zeus.

**Selleïs** (Σελλήεις) · PLACE — *Say:* sel-EE-is
A river named only in contingent back-stories, always in the same phrase, "from
the river Selleïs." Heracles brought Astyocheia, Tlepolemus' mother, out of Ephyra
by it; Asius' tall fire-colored horses come from it, in a line the poem repeats
verbatim when he storms the wall (2.839 = 12.97); and Phyleus brought home from it
the corslet that saves Meges in Book 15.
**Refs:** 2.659, 2.839, 12.97, 15.531. Books 2, 12, 15.
*See also:* Ephyra, Tlepolemus, Asius, Arisbe, Meges.

**Simoeisios** (Σιμοείσιος) · MORTAL — *Say:* sim-oh-EE-see-os
A young Trojan killed by Telamonian Ajax in the first general engagement, born by
the banks of the Simois while his mother was coming down from Ida with her parents
to see the flocks, and named for the river. He never paid back to his parents the
cost of his rearing; Ajax's spear takes him in the chest by the right nipple and
he falls like a black poplar felled by a chariot-maker to bend a wheel-rim, left
lying to season along the river bank.
*Kin:* son of Anthemion.
*Killed by:* Ajax (4.473–489).
**Refs:** 4.474, 4.477, 4.488. Book 4.
*See also:* Ajax, Simois, Telamon.

**Simois** (Σιμόεις) · PLACE — *Say:* SIM-oh-is
The second of Troy's two rivers, running with the Scamander across the plain: the
armies fight "midway between the streams of Simois and Xanthus," and Hera halts
her horses where the two join, whereupon Simois makes ambrosia spring up for them
to graze. It is one of the eight Idaean rivers Apollo turns against the Achaean
wall — named there for the shields and helmets and "the race of half-god men"
fallen in its dust — and in Book 21 Scamander calls to it as "dear brother" for
help against Achilles. Spelled *Simoïs* at 20.53 and 21.307.
**Refs:** 4.475, 5.774, 5.777, 6.4, 12.22. Books 4–6, 12.
*See also:* Scamander, Simoeisios, Ida, Troy.

**Sleep** (Ὕπνος) · GOD
"Sleep, the brother of Death," personified, and Hera's accomplice in the Deception
of Zeus. He refuses her at first — a previous commission of hers, laying Zeus's
mind asleep so she could drive Heracles to Cos, nearly got him flung out of heaven,
and only Night saved him — and yields only when she swears by the water of Styx to
give him Pasithea, one of the younger Graces, whom he has longed for all his days.
He perches in a fir on Ida in the likeness of the bird "the gods call chalkis, and
men call kymindis," then runs to tell Poseidon that Zeus is out. In Book 16 he and
Death are "the twin brothers" who carry Sarpedon's body home to Lycia.
*Kin:* brother of Death; son of Night in effect, who shelters him (14.259).
**Refs:** 14.231, 14.233, 14.242, 14.264, 14.270, 14.286, 14.354, 16.454, 16.672,
16.682. Books 14, 16.
*See also:* Death, Hera, Zeus, Pasithea, Night, Sarpedon, Poseidon.

**Socus** (Σῶκος) · MORTAL — *Say:* SOH-kus
A wealthy Trojan, brother of Charops, who comes to his brother's rescue when
Odysseus is cutting through Hippasus' sons and drives a spear through Odysseus'
shield and corslet, tearing the flesh from his ribs — Athena keeps the point from
his vitals. Odysseus, knowing the wound is not mortal, tells him destruction has
caught him, and spears him between the shoulders as he turns to run. The wound
Socus deals is what brings the Trojans down on Odysseus alone in the crowd.
*Kin:* son of Hippasus; brother of Charops.
*Killed by:* Odysseus (11.446–449).
**Refs:** 11.427, 11.428, 11.440, 11.446, 11.450, 11.456. Book 11.
*See also:* Odysseus, Charops, Hippasus, Athena.

**Sparta** (Σπάρτη) · PLACE
Menelaus' city in the Peloponnese, named in the Catalogue among the towns of his
contingent and again by Hera, who names Argos, Sparta and wide-wayed Mycenae as
the three cities dearest to her and offers all three for Troy's destruction
(4.52–53). The poem more often calls the country **Lacedaemon** — "hollow
Lacedaemon full of ravines" heads the same catalogue entry — and it is from
Lacedaemon that Paris carried Helen off, and there that she left the daughter and
the household she keeps naming in Book 3.
*Also called:* Lacedaemon, the district in which the Catalogue places it (2.581).
**Refs:** 2.581, 2.582, 3.239, 3.244, 3.388, 3.443, 4.52. Books 2–4.
*See also:* Menelaus, Helen, Argos, Mycenae, Hera.

**Spercheius** (Σπερχειός) · PLACE — *Say:* spur-KY-us
The river of Phthia, "the sky-fed river," to whom Peleus vowed that Achilles would
cut his hair and sacrifice fifty rams at its sources if he came home. He does not
come home: standing apart from the pyre, Achilles cuts the tawny lock he had been
growing for the river and puts it in Patroclus' hands instead, telling Spercheius
plainly that his father's vow was made for nothing. The river also fathered
Menesthius, a Myrmidon company-leader, on Peleus' daughter Polydora.
*Kin:* father of Menesthius by Polydora.
**Refs:** 16.174, 16.176, 23.142, 23.144. Books 16, 23.
*See also:* Achilles, Peleus, Patroclus, Menesthius, Polydora, Phthia.

**Sthenelus** (Σθένελος) · MORTAL — *Say:* STHEN-uh-lus
Diomedes' charioteer and companion, one of the Epigoni whose fathers fell at
Thebes. He pulls Pandarus' arrow from Diomedes' shoulder in Book 5, spots Aeneas
and Pandarus coming and urges retreat, and drives off the horses of Tros; Athena
shoves him bodily off the chariot to take the reins herself. He answers Agamemnon's
shaming of the fathers by claiming the sons took seven-gated Thebes where the
fathers failed — the speech Diomedes silently declines to endorse. In Book 9
Diomedes vows that he and Sthenelus alone will fight on to the end of Ilion. **A
second Sthenelus**, son of Perseus and father of Eurystheus, belongs to the
Heracles back-story of Book 19 and is a different man.
*Also called:* Capaneus' son.
*Kin:* son of Capaneus.
**Refs:** 2.564, 4.367, 5.108, 5.111, 5.241, 5.835, 8.114, 9.48, 19.116, 19.123,
23.510. Books 2, 4–5, 8–9, 19, 23.
*See also:* Diomedes, Capaneus, Thebes, Athena, Eurystheus, Perseus.

**Stichius** (Στιχίος) · MORTAL — *Say:* STIK-ee-us
An Achaean captain, named three times and killed by Hector in the rout of Book 15.
The poem describes him inconsistently: at 13.195 and 13.691 he is one of the
Athenian captains under Menestheus, helping carry the dead back among the host,
but at 15.330, in the couplet on his death, he is "a leader of the bronze-shirted
Boeotians." The text does not reconcile the two.
*Killed by:* Hector (15.329–330).
**Refs:** 13.195, 13.691, 15.329. Books 13, 15.
*See also:* Menestheus, Athenians, Boeotians, Hector, Arcesilaus.

**Strife** (Ἔρις) · GOD
Discord personified and capitalized, one of the battle-abstractions who take the
field with Ares. She stands with Terror and Panic in the muster of Book 4, works
beside "Ares bane of mortals" over the corpses, and is blazoned on Athena's aegis
with Valor and blood-chilling Pursuit. Zeus sends her down to the ships at the
opening of Book 11 to raise the war-cry; "Strife of the many groans" then rejoices
alone among the gods to see the armies close. Hephaestus sets her on the shield
with Tumult and deadly Fate, and at the theomachy "strong Strife, driver of
armies" rises against Athena's shout.
*Epithets:* the fixed capitalization of the personified Δεῖμος / Φόβος / Ἔρις group
is set at 4.440; the poem also calls her "insatiably raging" (4.440) and "of the
many groans" (11.73).
**Refs:** 4.440, 5.518, 5.740, 11.3, 11.73, 18.535, 20.48. Books 4–5, 11, 18, 20.
*See also:* Terror, Panic, Ares, Athena, Tumult, Fate.

**Sun** (Ἠέλιος) · GOD
Helios, invoked in the oath-scenes as the god "who see all things and hear all
things": one of the two lambs of the truce is for Earth and Sun, and he is called
to witness both Agamemnon's oath over the sworn oaths in Book 3 and his oath about
Briseis in Book 19, with Earth and the Erinyes. He also marks the poem's time —
"when the Sun bestrode the middle of heaven" Zeus lifts his scales — and is once
made to act: Hera sends "the unwearied Sun, much against his will, into the
streams of Ocean" to end the day Achilles rearms, fulfilling the sunset clause of
Zeus's plan. Called "Hyperion the Sun" at 8.480 and 19.398, and named Helios once
(14.344).
**Refs:** 3.104, 3.277, 8.68, 8.480, 18.239, 18.241, 19.197, 19.259. Books 3, 8,
18, 19.
*See also:* Zeus, Hera, Earth, Ocean, Hyperion, Erinyes.

**Talthybius** (Ταλθύβιος) · MORTAL — *Say:* tal-THIB-ee-us
Agamemnon's herald, "the godlike herald," and the man sent to do what his king
does not do himself. With Eurybates he goes unwillingly to Achilles' hut to take
Briseis in Book 1; he fetches the lamb for the truce-sacrifice in Book 3, and calls
Machaon to the wounded Menelaus in Book 4; with Idaeus of Troy he parts Ajax and
Hector when night falls on their duel. In Book 19 he holds the boar for Agamemnon's
oath and throws the carcass into the sea. The poem closes his arc at 23.897, where
Agamemnon's prize is handed to Talthybius to carry — the mirror of 1.320.
**Refs:** 1.320, 3.118, 4.192, 4.193, 7.276, 19.196, 19.250, 19.267, 23.897.
Books 1, 3–4, 7, 19, 23.
*See also:* Agamemnon, Eurybates, Idaeus, Briseis, Machaon.

**Tartarus** (Τάρταρος) · PLACE — *Say:* TAR-tuh-rus
The pit below Hades — "where the deepest pit yawns under the earth, / where the
gates are iron and the threshold bronze, / as far beneath Hades as heaven is above
the earth." It is Zeus's standing threat at the opening of Book 8 against any god
who helps either side, and the measure of how far he is strongest. Iapetus and
Cronos sit at the world's limit with "deep Tartarus all around them," and Hera
swears her oath to Sleep by the gods beneath it, "who are called the Titans."
**Refs:** 8.13, 8.481, 14.279. Books 8, 14.
*See also:* Zeus, Hades, Cronos, Iapetus, Sleep, Hera.

**Telamon** (Τελαμών) · MORTAL — *Say:* TEL-uh-mon
Father of Ajax the greater and, by another woman, of Teucer; a king of Salamis in
the older generation who does not come to Troy. In nearly all of its occurrences
the name is a patronymic — "Telamonian Ajax," "Ajax, Zeus-sprung son of Telamon,
marshal of the people" — and the referent is Ajax, per `canon.md`. He appears in
his own right only at 8.283, where Agamemnon urges Teucer to keep shooting and be
a light "to your father Telamon, who reared you when you were little / and cared
for you in his own house, bastard though you were."
*Also called:* the source of the adjective Telamonian.
*Kin:* father of Ajax and of Teucer, who are brothers "by one father" (12.371).
**Refs:** 2.528, 2.768, 6.5, 7.234, 8.281, 8.283, 9.644, 12.371, 13.170, 17.284
…and throughout. Books 2, 4–9, 11–18, 23.
*See also:* Ajax, Teucer, Salamis, Ajax son of Oïleus.

**Terror** (Δεῖμος) · GOD
One of the battle-abstractions personified and capitalized with Panic and Strife
(4.440). He and Panic attend Athena as she arms, "glaring terribly, and around her
Terror and Panic," and Ares orders the pair to yoke his horses when he means to
break Zeus's ban and avenge Ascalaphus. He never acts alone.
**Refs:** 4.440, 11.37, 15.119. Books 4, 11, 15.
*See also:* Panic, Strife, Ares, Athena.

**Tethys** (Τηθύς) · GOD — *Say:* TEE-this
"Mother Tethys," Ocean's consort, named twice in the Deception of Zeus and only
there, in the lie Hera tells first to Aphrodite and then verbatim to Zeus: that
she is going to the ends of the earth to see "Oceanus, the begetting of the gods,
and mother Tethys," who reared her in their halls when Rhea gave her up as Zeus
put Cronos down, and to settle their long quarrel. The pair are the poem's nearest
thing to a cosmogony.
*Kin:* consort of Ocean; foster-mother of Hera.
**Refs:** 14.201, 14.302. Book 14.
*See also:* Ocean, Hera, Rhea, Cronos, Zeus.

**Teucer** (Τεῦκρος) · MORTAL — *Say:* TYOO-sur
Telamon's bastard son and Ajax's half-brother, "his brother by one father," and
the best archer in the Achaean host — Idomeneus calls him "best of the Achaeans /
in bowmanship, and good too in the standing fight." His method is fixed in Book 8:
he shoots from under Ajax's shield and slips back behind it "like a child to his
mother," and the poem gives him a roll-call of eight victims before Hector breaks
his collarbone with a stone and snaps his bowstring. In Book 15 he has Hector
lined up when Zeus himself breaks the fresh-twisted string, and Teucer, shuddering,
gives the bow up for spear and shield. He hits Glaucus on the wall (12.387) and
loses the archery contest at the funeral games by forgetting to vow to Apollo.
*Kin:* son of Telamon; half-brother of Ajax.
**Refs:** 6.31, 8.266, 8.273, 8.322, 12.371, 12.387, 13.313, 15.458, 15.462,
23.862. Books 6, 8, 12–16, 23.
*See also:* Ajax, Telamon, Hector, Glaucus, Meriones, Apollo.

**Theano** (Θεανώ) · MORTAL — *Say:* thee-AN-oh
Antenor's wife, made priestess of Athena by the Trojans, and the woman who
performs the poem's one Trojan supplication of a goddess: she opens the temple
doors on the city's height, takes the robe Hecuba has brought and lays it on
Athena's knees, and vows twelve unbroken yearling heifers if the goddess will
break Diomedes' spear and drop him before the Scaean Gates — "So she spoke,
vowing. But Pallas Athena turned her head away." She had also reared Antenor's
bastard son Pedaeus with her own children, to please her husband.
*Epithets:* "fair-cheeked" (καλλιπάρῃος, 6.298).
*Kin:* daughter of Cisseus (6.299; called Cisses at 11.223); wife of Antenor;
foster-mother of Pedaeus; aunt of Iphidamas.
**Refs:** 5.70, 6.298, 6.302, 11.224. Books 5–6, 11.
*See also:* Antenor, Athena, Hecuba, Pedaeus, Iphidamas, Diomedes.

**Thebe** (Θήβη) · PLACE — *Say:* THEE-bee
Eëtion's city under wooded Placus, "Thebe of the high gates," seat of the Cilician
men, sacked by Achilles before the poem opens. It is where Chryseis was taken —
the sack that begins the plague-chain — and where Achilles killed Eëtion without
stripping him, burning him in his armor and heaping a mound that the mountain
nymphs planted round with elms. Andromache was born there and names it in both her
laments. Not the Boeotian Thebes: see **Thebes**.
**Refs:** 1.366, 2.691, 6.397, 6.416, 22.479. Books 1–2, 6, 22.
*See also:* Eëtion, Andromache, Achilles, Chryseis, Placus, Lyrnessus, Thebes.

**Thebes** (Θῆβαι) · PLACE — *Say:* theebz
"Seven-gated Thebes," the Boeotian city of the war before this one. Tydeus went
there as an envoy for Polyneices, beat the Cadmeans at their own games and killed
the fifty men they set to ambush him; the Seven perished before the walls, and the
heaped earth covers Tydeus there still. Agamemnon uses the fathers' Theban exploits
to shame the sons (4.370–400), and Sthenelus retorts that the sons took the city
where the fathers failed. Euryalus once beat all comers at the games for fallen
Oedipus there. A different Thebes — Egyptian Thebes, "where the houses hold the
greatest treasures" — is Achilles' measure of a ransom he will not take (9.381–382).
**Refs:** 4.378, 4.406, 5.804, 6.223, 9.381, 9.382, 10.286, 14.114, 19.99, 23.679.
Books 4–6, 9–10, 14, 19, 23.
*See also:* Tydeus, Diomedes, Sthenelus, Polyneices, Cadmeans, Euryalus, Thebe.

**Thersites** (Θερσίτης) · MORTAL — *Say:* thur-SY-teez
The one common soldier in the poem who speaks in assembly. He is described at
length and unkindly — bandy-legged, lame in one foot, hunched, his head coming to
a point with sparse stubble on it, "the ugliest man who came under Ilion" — and he
attacks Agamemnon for his greed in terms not far from Achilles' own in Book 1.
Odysseus strikes him across the back and shoulders with the scepter until a bloody
welt rises and he sits down weeping, and the army, vexed as it is, laughs at him
sweetly. He is never mentioned again. Odysseus' abuse still calls him a
"clear-voiced speaker."
**Refs:** 2.212, 2.244, 2.246, 2.266. Book 2.
*See also:* Odysseus, Agamemnon, Achaeans.

**Thestor** (Θέστωρ) · MORTAL — *Say:* THES-tor
Three men. The first is Calchas' father, named in the line that introduces the seer
"far the best of bird-readers" (1.69), and never otherwise present. The second is
the father of Alcmaon, whom Sarpedon spears at the wall (12.394). The third is a
son of Enops, caught by Patroclus' second rush in his aristeia: he sits hunched in
his chariot with the reins fallen from his hands, struck out of his wits, and
Patroclus spears him through the right jaw and hauls him over the chariot-rail like
a fisherman landing a sacred fish.
*Kin:* the first is father of Calchas; the second father of Alcmaon; the third son
of Enops.
*Killed by:* the third by Patroclus (16.401–410).
**Refs:** 1.69, 12.394, 16.401, 16.402. Books 1, 12, 16.
*See also:* Calchas, Alcmaon, Sarpedon, Patroclus, Enops.

**Thetis** (Θέτις) · GOD — *Say:* THEE-tis *or* THET-is
A sea-nymph, daughter of the old man of the sea, married against her will to a
mortal, and the mother of Achilles — the figure through whom the plot of the poem
is made. She takes her son's grievance to Zeus, clasps his knees on the peak of
Olympus, and holds on until he bows his head: the promise to give the Trojans power
until the Achaeans honor Achilles is the mechanism of everything that follows, and
she can extract it because she once saved Zeus from being bound by summoning
Briareus. She leads the Nereids' lament for a son not yet dead, tells him plainly
that his own doom stands ready straight after Hector's, and goes to Hephaestus —
who owes her his nine years' refuge after his fall — for the new armor. In Book 24
Zeus sends her to her son with the order to give Hector back, and she delivers it
sitting beside him, stroking him with her hand.
*Epithets:* "silver-footed" (ἀργυρόπεζα, 1.538).
*Also called:* Achilles' "lady mother" (18.35, 24.126).
*Kin:* daughter of Nereus, "the old man of the sea," never named; sister of the
Nereids; wife of Peleus, "Aeacus' son"; mother of Achilles. She sheltered Dionysus
from Lycurgus (6.136) and, with Eurynome, saved the fallen Hephaestus (18.398).
**Refs:** 1.413, 1.495, 1.512, 1.537, 6.136, 18.51, 18.127, 18.369, 24.74, 24.120
…and throughout. Books 1, 4, 6, 8–9, 13, 15–16, 18–20, 23–24.
*See also:* Achilles, Peleus, Zeus, Hephaestus, Nereids, Briareus, Hera, Eurynome.

**Thoas** (Θόας) · MORTAL — *Say:* THOH-as
Leader of the Aetolians, Andraemon's son, who inherited the whole rule of Aetolia
because Oeneus' sons were dead and fair-haired Meleager with them. He kills Peiroos
over the body of Diores in Book 4 and cannot strip him for the Thracian topknots
around him; he volunteers for the duel with Hector; and at 15.281–299 he gives the
poem's most level piece of tactical advice, telling the best men to stand and blunt
Hector while the multitude falls back on the ships — "far the best of the Aetolians
— skilled with the javelin, / brave in the standing fight," and few could beat him
in debate. **Two other men bear the name:** Thoas king of Lemnos, to whom the
Phoenicians gave the silver mixing-bowl that becomes a prize at Patroclus' games
(14.230, 23.745), and a Trojan whom Menelaus kills at 16.311. See `canon.md`.
*Kin:* son of Andraemon.
**Refs:** 2.638, 2.643, 4.527, 4.529, 7.168, 13.92, 13.222, 14.230, 15.281, 16.311,
19.239, 23.745. Books 2, 4, 7, 13–16, 19, 23.
*See also:* Aetolians, Peiroos, Diores, Meleager, Oeneus, Euneus, Hypsipyle.

**Thoon** (Θόων) · MORTAL — *Say:* THOH-on
More than one Trojan of the name, all of them killed. The first is a son of
Phaenops, cut down with his brother Xanthus by Diomedes — the two were late-born
and beloved, and their father, worn with age, had no other son, so that kinsmen of
the widowed house divided the estate (5.152–158). The second falls to Odysseus in
Book 11. A Thoon is also named among Asius' captains storming the wall (12.140);
and in Book 13 Antilochus catches one as he turns and severs the vein that runs the
length of the back to the neck.
*Killed by:* Diomedes (5.152–155); Odysseus (11.422); Antilochus (13.545–548).
**Refs:** 5.152, 11.422, 12.140, 13.545. Books 5, 11–13.
*See also:* Phaenops, Xanthus, Diomedes, Odysseus, Antilochus, Asius.

**Thrace** (Θρῄκη) · PLACE — *Say:* thrayss
The land north of the Hellespont, "Thrace of the deep soil, mother of flocks,"
where Iphidamas was reared in Cisses' house. Boreas and Zephyrus blow out of it
across the Aegean; wine is brought daily from it to the Achaean camp; Poseidon
watches the war from Samothrace over the heads of the Thracians. Rhesus' late-come
contingent and Rhigmus come from there, and the cup Priam takes to Achilles among
his ransom was a Thracian gift.
**Refs:** 9.5, 9.72, 11.222, 13.301, 20.485, 24.234. Books 9, 11, 13, 20, 24.
*See also:* Thracians, Rhesus, Iphidamas, Rhigmus, Hellespont, Samothrace.

**Thracians** (Θρῇκες) · PEOPLE — *Say:* THRAY-shunz
Trojan-side allies from beyond the Hellespont, led in the Catalogue by Acamas and
the hero Peiroos, and marked by their hair: "the Thracians with their hair in
topknots, long spears in their hands," who stand over Peiroos' body and thrust
Thoas back. Ares wears Acamas' likeness to rally the Trojans, and Telamonian Ajax
kills "the man who was best among the Thracians." Their second, larger appearance
is Rhesus' company, new-come and camped outermost of all, whose watch Dolon betrays
and whom Diomedes kills sleeping — twelve men and their king — while Odysseus
drags the bodies clear for the horses.
**Refs:** 2.844, 4.519, 4.533, 4.537, 5.462, 6.7, 10.434, 10.470, 10.559, 13.4,
14.228. Books 2, 4–6, 10, 13–14, 23.
*See also:* Rhesus, Dolon, Diomedes, Acamas, Peiroos, Thrace, Thoas.

**Thrasymedes** (Θρασυμήδης) · MORTAL — *Say:* thras-ih-MEE-deez
Nestor's son and Antilochus' brother, "steadfast in battle" and "horse-taming."
He commands the night sentries in Book 9, lends Diomedes a sword and shield for the
night raid, and his shield is the one his father takes up in Book 14 — the son
having his father's. In Book 16 he kills Maris, who is standing over his brother's
body in fury; in Book 17 he and Antilochus are the last two who have not heard that
Patroclus is dead, and when Antilochus runs with the news it is Thrasymedes whom
Nestor sends to replace him.
*Epithets:* "steadfast in battle" (μενεπτόλεμος, 10.255).
*Kin:* son of Nestor; brother of Antilochus.
**Refs:** 9.81, 10.255, 14.10, 16.321, 17.378, 17.705. Books 9–10, 14, 16–17.
*See also:* Nestor, Antilochus, Maris, Patroclus, Diomedes.

**Tlepolemus** (Τληπόλεμος) · MORTAL — *Say:* tluh-POL-uh-mus *or* tuh-LEP-oh-lee-mus
Heracles' son by Astyocheia, leader of the nine Rhodian ships. The Catalogue gives
him an exile's biography: grown to manhood he killed his father's aging uncle
Licymnius on the instant, and fled over the sea from Heracles' sons and grandsons
to settle Rhodes in three tribes, where Zeus poured wondrous wealth upon them. Fate
drives him against Sarpedon — the son and the grandson of Zeus — and he opens with
a taunt about Heracles' six ships and the sack of Ilion; Sarpedon's spear goes
through his neck, and murky night covers his eyes, while his own cast only grazes
Sarpedon's thigh-bone. **A second Tlepolemus**, Damastor's son, is one of the
Trojans Patroclus kills in heaps at 16.416.
*Kin:* son of Heracles and Astyocheia.
*Killed by:* Sarpedon (5.655–659).
**Refs:** 2.653, 2.657, 2.661, 5.628, 5.632, 5.648, 5.655, 5.660, 5.669, 16.416.
Books 2, 5, 16.
*See also:* Heracles, Sarpedon, Rhodes, Licymnius, Astyocheia, Selleïs.

**Trojans** (Τρῶες) · PEOPLE
The defenders of Troy and, loosely, their allies with them — the poem's second
great collective, and after Zeus its commonest name. They are the army the
Achaeans face and the people whose city is doomed: the poem sets against the
Achaeans' anonymous mass a Trojan side that is a city with women and children in
it, and its formulas of address gather in the allies — "Trojans and Lycians and
Dardanians who fight at close quarters." Their assemblies, their truce, their
oath-breaking under Pandarus, their one night camped in the open on the plain, and
their lamentations over Hector are as fully narrated as anything on the Achaean
side.
*Epithets:* "horse-taming" (ἱππόδαμοι, 2.230); the individual form of the same word
is "breaker of horses."
**Refs:** 1.152, 2.123, 3.2, 4.509, 8.173, 12.440, 18.243, 22.1 …and throughout.
Books 1–24.
*See also:* Troy, Ilion, Hector, Priam, Dardanians, Lycians, Achaeans.

**Tros** (Τρώς) · MORTAL — *Say:* tros
Two men. The first is the eponym of the Trojans, in the fourth generation of the
house of Dardanus: "Erichthonius fathered Tros, lord of the Trojans; / and from
Tros in turn three blameless sons were born: / Ilus, and Assaracus, and godlike
Ganymede." Zeus gave him the immortal horses in payment for Ganymede, and it is
that stock — "the horses of Tros" — that Anchises stole a breed from, that Diomedes
takes from Aeneas, and that he drives in the chariot race. The second Tros is
Alastor's son, killed at 20.463: he comes to clasp Achilles' knees hoping to be
spared as a man of his own age, and Achilles, "all raw fury," stabs him in the
liver — the first of the poem's three refused supplications.
*Kin:* the ancestor is son of Erichthonius, father of Ilus, Assaracus and Ganymede,
and great-grandfather of Priam; the victim is Alastor's son.
*Killed by:* the victim, by Achilles (20.463–472).
**Refs:** 5.222, 5.265, 8.106, 20.230, 20.231, 20.463, 20.468, 23.291, 23.377.
Books 5, 8, 20, 23.
*See also:* Dardanus, Erichthonius, Ilus, Assaracus, Ganymede, Aeneas, Achilles,
Lycaon, Hector.

**Troy** (Τροίη / Ἴλιος) · PLACE
The city, and the poem's fixed point: everything happens on the plain before it or
inside its walls. The translation keeps both surface names the Greek uses —
**Ilion**, "holy Ilion," "the well-built citadel of Ilion," and **Troy**,
"well-walled," "wide-wayed," "deep-soiled" — and both are headwords. The city is
never taken in the poem, but its fall is stated as certain from Book 4 on ("the
day will come when holy Ilion shall perish, / and Priam, and the people of Priam
of the good ash spear," 4.164–165 = 6.448–449), and Zeus names it the dearest of
mortal cities to him even as he concedes it. It is Poseidon and Apollo's own work,
built for Laomedon, and its wall is what Hector must come outside of to die.
*Also called:* Ilion — the same city, both spellings throughout; its citadel is
Pergamos.
**Refs:** 1.71, 1.129, 2.113, 2.141, 4.46, 4.164, 6.315, 7.20, 21.544, 22.411
…and throughout. Books 1–24.
*See also:* Ilion, Trojans, Priam, Hector, Pergamos, Scaean Gates, Laomedon.

**Tydeus** (Τυδεύς) · MORTAL — *Say:* TY-dee-us
Diomedes' father, one of the Seven against Thebes, dead before the poem opens and
covered by the heaped earth at Thebes. The name is overwhelmingly a patronymic:
"Tydeus' son" is Diomedes, and in most of its 109 occurrences the father is not the
referent. Where he is, he is the standing measure the sons are held against —
Agamemnon shames Diomedes with the story of the envoy who beat the Cadmeans at
their games and killed the fifty men they ambushed him with, and concludes that
"the son he fathered is worse than he in battle — better in the assembly"; Athena
tells Diomedes he is "no son of Tydeus" if fear holds him, recalling that his father
was a small man in build but a fighter. Diomedes says he does not remember him.
*Kin:* son of Oeneus' line, of Portheus through Oeneus; married a daughter of
Adrastus and settled at Argos; father of Diomedes.
**Refs:** 2.406, 4.370, 4.372, 4.384, 4.399, 5.800, 5.813, 6.222, 14.114 …and
throughout. Books 2, 4–11, 14, 16, 19, 21, 23.
*See also:* Diomedes, Thebes, Oeneus, Adrastus, Polyneices, Agamemnon, Athena.

**Xanthus (horse)** (Ξάνθος) · OTHER — *Say:* ZAN-thus
One of Achilles' two immortal horses, yoked with Balius; the harpy Podarge
conceived the pair by the West Wind as she grazed in a meadow beside the stream of
Ocean. In Book 19, when Achilles reproaches them for leaving Patroclus dead,
white-armed Hera gives Xanthus voice: he drops his head until his mane spills to
the ground and answers that they will bring Achilles back safe this time, but that
his day of destruction is near, and that the blame is a great god's and strong
Fate's — "it is fated to be broken by force — by a god, and by a man." The Erinyes
then stop his voice. The token *Xanthus* is otherwise counted under the river
Scamander; see `canon.md`'s homonym register.
*Also called:* named always with Balius as a pair.
*Kin:* offspring of Podarge and the West Wind; yoked with Balius, and with the
mortal Pedasus in the side-traces.
**Refs:** 16.149, 19.400. Books 16, 19.
*See also:* Balius, Podarge, Achilles, Automedon, Pedasus, Scamander, Erinyes,
Zephyrus.

**Zeleia** (Ζέλεια) · PLACE — *Say:* zuh-LY-uh
Pandarus' town, "under the lowest foot of Ida," whose men drink the dark water of
the Aesepus. The Catalogue calls them Trojans; the town itself appears twice more,
in the vow Athena puts in Pandarus' mouth and then in his own performance of it —
a hecatomb of firstborn lambs to Apollo the Lycian-born "when he came home to the
town of holy Zeleia." He does not come home.
**Refs:** 2.824, 4.103, 4.121. Books 2, 4.
*See also:* Pandarus, Ida, Aesepus, Apollo, Lycaon.

**Zephyrus** (Ζέφυρος) · GOD — *Say:* ZEF-ih-rus
The west wind, named in Book 23 only, where Iris finds all the winds feasting
indoors at "the house of gusting Zephyrus" and delivers Achilles' prayer: he and
Boreas rise with an unearthly roar, drive the clouds before them, and fall on the
pyre so that Patroclus burns all night. Elsewhere in the poem the same wind is
called "the West Wind" without the name — as the sire of Xanthus and Balius by the
harpy Podarge (16.150), and in Xanthus' own boast that the two of them could run
with its breath (19.415).
*Kin:* father of Xanthus and Balius by Podarge (named there as "the West Wind").
**Refs:** 23.195, 23.200, 23.208. Book 23.
*See also:* Boreas, Iris, Achilles, Patroclus, Xanthus (horse), Balius, Podarge.

**Zeus** (Ζεύς) · GOD — *Say:* ZOOS
The presiding god of the poem and the agent of its plot. Its fifth line announces
that through all the deaths "the will of Zeus moved toward fulfillment," and the
mechanism is set in Book 1: Thetis clasps his knees, he bows his head — the pledge
that cannot be taken back — and promises to give the Trojans power until the
Achaeans honor Achilles. He enforces it by banning the gods from the field on pain
of Tartarus, by his golden scales, and by a long plan-speech at 15.56–77 that lays
out Books 16 through 22 in advance. He is not omnipotent in it so much as bound by
it: he cannot save Sarpedon, his own dearest son, without unmaking the order the
other gods live by, and instead rains drops of blood on the earth; he weighs
Hector's fate and lets it sink; Hera deceives him into sleep and he wakes furious
but does not undo it. In Book 24 he ends the poem by sending Thetis to Achilles
and Iris to Priam, ransom for a body.
*Epithets:* "aegis-bearing" (αἰγίοχος, 1.202); "Zeus the cloud-gatherer"
(νεφεληγερέτα, 1.511); "Zeus the counselor / of the counsels" (μητίετα, 1.175);
"who thunders on high" (ὑψιβρεμέτης, 1.354); "who delights in thunder"
(τερπικέραυνος, 1.419); "lord of lightning" (ἀστεροπητής, 1.580); "wide-seeing"
(εὐρύοπα, 1.498); "high-throned" (ὑψίζυγος, 4.166); "father of men and gods"
(πατὴρ ἀνδρῶν τε θεῶν τε, 1.544); "the dispenser of war" (ταμίης πολέμοιο, 4.84);
"the loud-thunderer, Hera's lord" (ἐρίγδουπος πόσις Ἥρης, 7.411); "Zeus of All
Voices" (Πανομφαῖος, 8.250); "Zeus of Guest-right" (Ζεὺς ξείνιος, 13.625).
*Also called:* "son of Cronos" (Κρονίδης / Κρονίων, 1.398) — the patronymic that
resolves to Zeus throughout; "Cronos" alone is the Titan father.
*Kin:* son of Cronos and Rhea; brother of Poseidon, Hades and Hera, who is also
his wife; the three brothers divide sky, sea and underworld by lot (15.187–193).
Father of Athena, of Apollo and Artemis by Leto, of Ares and Hebe by Hera, of
Aphrodite by Dione, of Hermes, of Heracles by Alcmene, and of Sarpedon.
**Refs:** 1.5, 1.503, 1.528, 8.5, 15.187, 16.431, 22.209, 24.103 …and throughout.
Books 1–24.
*See also:* Hera, Thetis, Achilles, Poseidon, Athena, Apollo, Sarpedon, Hector,
Cronos, Tartarus, Fate.
