**Eumelus** (Εὔμηλος) · MORTAL
Leader of eleven ships from Pherae and Iolcus, and owner of the best horses in the
host: the Muse-question at 2.761–767 answers that the finest were "the mares of
Pheres' line, which Eumelus drove," matched in coat and years, reared by Apollo in
Pereia. He is "supreme in horsemanship" and rises first for the chariot race in
Book 23, but Athena — retaliating for Apollo's sabotage of Diomedes — shatters his
yoke, and he is thrown and comes in last, dragging his chariot. Achilles pities
him and proposes second prize; Antilochus objects, and Eumelus is given
Asteropaeus' corslet instead.
*Also called:* Admetus' son.
*Kin:* son of Admetus and Alcestis, "brilliant among women, loveliest of the
daughters of Pelias" (2.715); grandson of Pheres.
**Refs:** 2.714, 2.764, 23.288, 23.354, 23.381, 23.481, 23.558, 23.565. Books 2,
23.
*See also:* Admetus, Alcestis, Athena, Diomedes, Antilochus, Asteropaeus.

---

**Euphorbus** (Εὔφορβος) · MORTAL
The Dardanian who strikes Patroclus first — from behind, between the shoulders,
after Apollo has already dazed him — then pulls his spear free and runs back into
the crowd rather than face even an unarmed man. The poem gives him a resumé in the
same breath: he outshone all men of his age at the spear, at horsemanship and in
the footrace, and had brought down twenty men his first time out with a chariot.
Dying, Patroclus names him second of his three killers, after Apollo. In Book 17
he claims the body from Menelaus and is speared through the throat; his blood-soaked
hair, "hair like the Graces'," braided in gold and silver, gets the poem's olive-sapling
simile.
*Also called:* Panthous' son — the patronymic also serves Polydamas and Hyperenor.
*Kin:* son of Panthous.
*Kills:* wounds Patroclus (16.806–815).
*Killed by:* Menelaus (17.45–60).
**Refs:** 16.808, 16.850, 17.47, 17.59, 17.81. Books 16–17.
*See also:* Patroclus, Menelaus, Panthous, Polydamas, Apollo, Hector, Graces.

---

**Euryalus** (Εὐρύαλος) · MORTAL
Third captain of the Argive contingent under Diomedes, behind Diomedes himself and
Sthenelus, "a godlike man." He kills four Trojans in the Book 6 rout, among them
the twins Aesepus and Pedasus, whom the water-nymph Abarbarea bore to Bucolion. In
Book 23 he alone stands up to Epeius at boxing — Diomedes girds him and talks him
up — and is knocked flat with a blow to the cheek, led away through the gathering
with his feet trailing, spitting thick blood, his wits astray.
*Kin:* son of Mecisteus, son of Talaus. His father once came to Thebes for the
burial of fallen Oedipus and beat all the sons of Cadmus (23.679–680).
*Kills:* Dresus, Opheltius, Aesepus and Pedasus (6.20–28).
**Refs:** 2.565, 6.20, 23.677. Books 2, 6, 23.
*See also:* Diomedes, Sthenelus, Mecisteus, Epeius, Bucolion.

---

**Eurypylus** (Εὐρύπυλος) · MORTAL
Thessalian leader of forty ships from Ormenion and Hypereia, and one of the nine
who stand up to answer Hector's challenge in Book 7. He kills Hypsenor the
Scamander-priest's son in Book 5 and Melanthius in Book 6, and in Book 11, coming
to the rescue of the hard-pressed Ajax, is shot through the right thigh by Paris.
His wound is the hinge of the plot: Patroclus, sent by Achilles to ask after the
wounded, meets him limping from the field, and though on an errand he will not
abandon him — he cuts the arrow out, washes the wound and lays on a root Chiron
taught Achilles. Patroclus is still sitting in his hut at 15.390, delighting him
with talk, when the Trojans storm the wall; the delay is the last thing between him
and his own death. A different Eurypylus is the hero of Cos (2.677).
*Also called:* Euaemon's son — the recurring line is "Euaemon's shining son."
*Kin:* son of Euaemon.
*Kills:* Hypsenor (5.76–83); Melanthius (6.36); Apisaon (11.578).
**Refs:** 2.736, 5.76, 6.36, 7.167, 11.583, 11.809, 12.2, 15.392, 16.27. Books 2,
5–8, 11–12, 15–16.
*See also:* Patroclus, Paris, Ajax, Machaon, Achilles.

---

**Eurystheus** (Εὐρυσθεύς) · MORTAL
The king Heracles served, present only in the back-story. Athena complains at
8.362–369 that Zeus forgets how often she was sent to rescue his son "worn to
breaking under Eurystheus's labors" — among them the descent to Hades to fetch the
hound. His birth is the point of Agamemnon's Blindness-parable in Book 19: Hera,
trapping Zeus in an oath, hurried this child into the light before his month and
held back Alcmene's labor, so that the rule Zeus meant for Heracles fell to
Eurystheus, and Zeus groaned ever after to see his son "doing shameful labor under
Eurystheus's tasks." His herald Copreus carried the orders (15.639–640).
*Kin:* son of Sthenelus, son of Perseus.
**Refs:** 8.363, 8.367, 15.640, 19.123, 19.133. Books 8, 15, 19.
*See also:* Heracles, Hera, Alcmene, Blindness, Sthenelus, Perseus, Copreus.

---

**Eëtion** (Ἠετίων) · MORTAL
King of Thebe-under-Placus, lord over the Cilician men, and Andromache's father.
Achilles sacked his city before the poem opens — the raid that also took Chryseis —
and killed him there, but "did not strip him: his heart stood in awe of that,"
burning him in his armor and heaping a mound over which the mountain nymphs planted
elms. Achilles killed all seven of his sons in one day and ransomed his wife back.
The spoils of Thebe run through the poem: the lyre Achilles is singing to when the
embassy arrives, the horse Pedasus, and the lump of self-smelted iron he sets up as
a prize at Patroclus' games, "that the huge strength of Eëtion used to throw." Two
other men bear the name: Eëtion of Imbros, who ransomed Lycaon (21.43), and the
father of Podes, Hector's feast-companion (17.575).
*Kin:* father of Andromache and of seven sons; father-in-law of Hector.
*Killed by:* Achilles (6.414–420).
**Refs:** 1.366, 6.396, 6.416, 8.187, 9.188, 16.153, 21.43, 22.472, 23.827. Books
1, 6, 8–9, 16–17, 21–23.
*See also:* Andromache, Achilles, Hector, Thebe, Placus, Chryseis, Podes.

---

**Fate** (Μοῖρα) · GOD
Personified only where the translation capitalizes her; elsewhere μοῖρα is the
common noun "fate," and the death-line formula μοῖρα κραταιή is rendered "fate the
overpowering." She walks the shield of Achilles beside Strife and Tumult, "deadly
Fate, holding one man fresh-wounded and alive, another unwounded, another dead,
dragging him by the feet through the fighting," her cloak red with the blood of men
(18.535–538). Elsewhere she is a spinner: what she "spun for him with her thread at
his birth" is said of Achilles by Zeus (20.127–128) and of Hector by Hecuba
(24.209–210), in the same wording. Agamemnon names her with Zeus and the Erinys as
the causes of his blindness, and the horse Xanthus names "a great god, and strong
Fate" as the killers of Patroclus.
*See also:* Strife, Blindness, Erinys, Xanthus, Zeus, Hecuba.

---

**Gargaron** (Γάργαρον) · PLACE
The peak of Ida where Zeus's precinct and smoking altar are, and the seat from
which he watches the war: he halts his horses there at 8.48–52, pours mist around
them, and sits "looking out at the Trojans' city and the ships of the Achaeans."
It is where Hera comes to him arrayed in the kestos, and where he sleeps after,
"broken by sleep and love, his wife in his arms," while Sleep runs to Poseidon
with the news; and where Iris and Apollo find him wreathed in a fragrant cloud when
he wakes.
**Refs:** 8.48, 14.292, 14.352, 15.152. Books 8, 14–15.
*See also:* Ida, Zeus, Hera, Sleep, Iris.

---

**Glaucus** (Γλαῦκος) · MORTAL
Co-leader of the Lycians with Sarpedon, and one of the two men — with Asteropaeus —
whom Sarpedon picks out as best of the allies after himself. In Book 6 he meets
Diomedes between the lines and answers the challenge with the poem's most famous
image, "As the generation of leaves, so is the generation of men," then recites the
Bellerophon story; the two discover their grandfathers' guest-friendship, refuse to
fight, and exchange armor — at which "Zeus took Glaucus's wits away," gold for
bronze, a hundred oxen's worth for nine. Teucer's arrow through his bared arm takes
him out of the fight at the wall; Apollo heals it at his prayer in Book 16 so that
he can rally the Lycians over Sarpedon's body. In Book 17 he rounds on Hector for
letting Sarpedon's corpse go and threatens to take the Lycians home. His
great-grandfather Glaucus, son of Sisyphus, bears the same name (6.154).
*Kin:* son of Hippolochus; grandson of Bellerophon; cousin of Sarpedon through
Laodameia.
*Kills:* Iphinous (7.13–16); Bathycles (16.593–598).
**Refs:** 2.876, 6.119, 6.234, 7.13, 12.309, 12.387, 16.492, 16.530, 17.140.
Books 2, 6–7, 12, 14, 16–17.
*See also:* Sarpedon, Diomedes, Hippolochus, Bellerophon, Teucer, Apollo, Hector.

---

**Graces** (Χάριτες) · GOD
Divine craftswomen and, in Book 14, marriageable persons. They wove the ambrosial
robe Aphrodite is wearing when Diomedes cuts her hand (5.338). Hera buys Sleep's
help against Zeus by offering him "one of the younger Graces" to marry, and he names
his price: Pasithea, "whom I myself have longed for all my days." Their name is also
the poem's measure of male beauty — Euphorbus dies with "hair like the Graces'."
*Also called:* the younger Graces (14.267, 14.275); Pasithea is the one named.
**Refs:** 5.338, 14.267, 14.275, 17.51. Books 5, 14, 17.
*See also:* Aphrodite, Hera, Sleep, Pasithea, Euphorbus.

---

**Hades** (Ἅιδης) · GOD
Lord of the dead, and by metonymy the underworld itself — most of the poem's
forty-six occurrences are the destination in phrases like "went down to the house of
Hades," which is where the wrath of the proem sends "many strong souls of heroes"
in the fourth line. As a person he is the third of Cronos' sons: Poseidon's account
of the lots at 15.187–193 gives Zeus the wide heaven, himself the grey sea, and
"Hades the misty dark," with earth and Olympus common to all three. Heracles shot
him at Pylos "among the dead, and gave him over to pain" (5.395–397); Athena wears
his helmet to hide from Ares (5.845); and in the theomachy he leaps screaming from
his throne, afraid Poseidon will crack the earth open and expose his "terrible,
mouldering houses, which the very gods abhor" (20.61–65). Achilles learns from
Patroclus' ghost that there is a soul and an image in his house "but the wits are
not in it at all."
*Epithets:* "the gate-closer" (πυλάρτης, 8.367); "of the famed colts"
(κλυτόπωλος, 5.654).
*Also called:* Aïdoneus (5.190, 20.61).
*Kin:* son of Cronos and Rhea; brother of Zeus, Poseidon and Hera.
**Refs:** 1.3, 5.395, 5.654, 5.845, 8.367, 9.158, 15.188, 15.191, 16.856, 20.294,
22.213, 23.103 …and throughout. Books 1, 3, 5–9, 11, 13–16, 20–24.
*See also:* Zeus, Poseidon, Persephone, Erebus, Patroclus, Heracles.

---

**Haemon** (Αἵμων) · MORTAL
Three men. The first is a Pylian, "the lord Haemon," one of the five captains
Nestor is arraying his companions around when Agamemnon reaches him in Book 4. The
second is a Cadmean, father of Maeon — one of the two leaders of the fifty-man
ambush the Thebans set for Tydeus, and the only man Tydeus let go home alive. The
third is named once as Alcimedon's grandfather, father of Laerces, among the
Myrmidons.
**Refs:** 4.296, 4.394, 17.467. Books 4, 17.
*See also:* Nestor, Maeon, Tydeus, Alcimedon, Laerces.

---

**Hebe** (Ἥβη) · GOD
Daughter of Zeus and Hera, and the gods' cup-bearer: the poem's first sight of
Olympus at the opening of Book 4 has her pouring nectar among the seated gods while
they look out at the city of the Trojans. She also works as Hera's groom, setting
the bronze eight-spoked wheels on the chariot when her mother goes to war (5.722),
and it is she who bathes Ares and dresses him in graceful clothing after Paiëon has
healed his belly-wound.
*Epithets:* "the lady Hebe" (πότνια Ἥβη, 4.2).
*Kin:* daughter of Zeus and Hera; sister of Ares.
**Refs:** 4.2, 5.722, 5.905. Books 4–5.
*See also:* Hera, Zeus, Ares, Paiëon.

---

**Hector** (Ἕκτωρ) · MORTAL
Priam's eldest son, Troy's champion, and the man on whom the city rests — "for
Hector alone was the saving of Ilion" (6.403). He is the poem's second protagonist:
he duels Ajax to a standstill in Book 7, breaks the Achaean gate with a stone in
Book 12, fires the ships in Book 15, and strips Achilles' armor from Patroclus'
body, the act that brings Achilles back and destroys him. His domestic scene with
Andromache and the infant Astyanax at the Scaean Gates (6.390–496) is set against
his own certainty that Ilion will fall; he twice rejects Polydamas' counsel to
withdraw, the second time fatally. In Book 22 he waits alone outside the wall,
runs three times around the city, is tricked by Athena in Deiphobus' shape, and
takes Achilles' spear through the gullet. Achilles drags his body for twelve days
until Priam ransoms it, and the poem closes on his burial.
*Epithets:* "of the flashing helmet" (κορυθαίολος, 2.816); "glorious" (φαίδιμος,
4.505); "man-slaughtering" (ἀνδροφόνος, 1.242 — given last to his own head at
24.724); "breaker of horses" (ἱππόδαμος, 7.38), rendered "tamer of horses" in the
line that ends the poem (22.161, 24.804).
*Also called:* Priam's son — the default referent of the patronymic, though it also
serves Paris, Helenus, Deiphobus, Polydorus, Lycaon and the Book 24 roll-call.
*Kin:* son of Priam and Hecuba; husband of Andromache, daughter of Eëtion; father
of Astyanax; brother of Paris, Helenus, Deiphobus, Polydorus, Lycaon, Cassandra and
Laodice.
*Kills:* Patroclus (16.818–863), with Apollo and Euphorbus striking before him;
Amphimachus, Schedius, Asaeus, Autonous and many more.
*Killed by:* Achilles (22.326–330).
**Refs:** 1.242, 2.816, 3.38, 6.390, 6.440, 7.38, 12.445, 16.818, 18.284, 22.326,
24.724, 24.804 …and throughout. Books 1–24.
*See also:* Achilles, Priam, Andromache, Astyanax, Polydamas, Patroclus, Ajax,
Apollo, Deiphobus.

---

**Hecuba** (Ἑκάβη) · MORTAL
Priam's wife and Hector's mother. She meets Hector at the palace in Book 6 and
offers him wine, then leads the older women to lay the loveliest robe of her Sidonian
store on Athena's knees — a prayer Athena refuses. On the wall at 22.79–89 she bares
her breast and begs him to come inside; she leads the lament over his body twice,
at the wall and at the bier, where she says his flesh is unmarked and "dear to the
gods you were while you lived with me." In Book 24 she opposes Priam's journey to
the ships outright, calling Achilles savage and faithless and wishing she could eat
his liver, then yields and makes her husband pour a libation before he goes.
*Epithets:* "gentle-giving" (ἠπιόδωρος, 6.251).
*Kin:* daughter of Dymas, sister of Asius (16.718); wife of Priam; mother of Hector,
Paris, Deiphobus, Helenus, Laodice, Cassandra and the rest.
**Refs:** 6.293, 6.451, 16.718, 22.234, 22.430, 24.193, 24.283, 24.747. Books 6,
16, 22, 24.
*See also:* Priam, Hector, Andromache, Athena, Achilles, Dymas, Asius.

---

**Helen** (Ἑλένη) · MORTAL
Menelaus' wife, taken to Troy by Paris, and the cause the whole war is fought over —
"Argive Helen, for whose sake so many Achaeans have perished at Troy." The poem
handles her almost entirely through her own speech, and every scene of it is
self-reproach: she names herself "dog-face" to Priam on the wall while identifying
the Achaean captains for him, calls herself "the dog, the evil-scheming horror" to
Hector, and wishes a storm had carried her off the day she was born. Aphrodite
compels her to Paris' bed in Book 3 with a threat, and she goes in silence. She
speaks last of the three women who lament Hector, mourning the only man in Troy who
never gave her a hard word: "there is no one else now in wide Troy gentle to me, or
kind: they all shudder at me."
*Epithets:* "of the trailing robe" (τανύπεπλος, 3.228).
*Kin:* daughter of Zeus (3.418, "Helen sprung of Zeus"); wife of Menelaus; taken by
Paris; she names a daughter left behind at Sparta (3.175).
**Refs:** 2.161, 3.121, 3.171, 3.228, 3.418, 6.343, 7.350, 24.761. Books 2–4, 6–9,
11, 13, 19, 22, 24.
*See also:* Paris, Menelaus, Priam, Hector, Aphrodite, Antenor, Laodice.

---

**Helenus** (Ἕλενος) · MORTAL
Priam's son, "far the best of bird-readers," whose counsel twice sets the plot
moving: at 6.75–101 he sends Hector back into the city to have Hecuba petition
Athena, which produces the Andromache scene; and at 7.44–53 he hears "the voice of
the gods who are forever," relays that it is not yet Hector's fate to die, and
prompts the challenge that brings on the duel with Ajax. He also fights — he leads
one of the storming-parties at the wall with Deiphobus, kills Deïpyrus with a
Thracian sword, and is shot in the bow-hand by Menelaus and carried out. Priam
curses him by name among the nine surviving sons in Book 24. A different Helenus,
son of Oenops, is one of the Achaeans Hector and Ares kill at 5.707.
*Kin:* son of Priam and Hecuba; brother of Hector, Paris and Deiphobus.
*Kills:* Deïpyrus (13.576–580).
**Refs:** 5.707, 6.75, 7.44, 12.94, 13.576, 13.582, 24.249. Books 5–7, 12–13, 24.
*See also:* Priam, Hector, Deiphobus, Menelaus, Cassandra, Deïpyrus.

---

**Hellas** (Ἑλλάς) · PLACE
Not yet a name for Greece: in this poem it is a district of Phthia, listed in the
Catalogue with Alos, Alope, Trachis and Phthia as the country of Achilles'
contingent, and fixed as "Hellas of the beautiful women." Achilles offers it as his
marriage-market — "There are Achaean girls enough through Hellas and through Phthia"
— and Phoenix fled from it to Peleus after the quarrel with his father Amyntor,
crossing "Hellas of the wide spaces." Bathycles, the richest of the Myrmidons, had
his home there.
**Refs:** 2.683, 9.395, 9.447, 9.478, 16.595. Books 2, 9, 16.
*See also:* Hellenes, Phthia, Achilles, Phoenix, Myrmidons, Bathycles.

---

**Hellenes** (Ἕλληνες) · PEOPLE
Still a narrow regional term, twice only. At 2.684 it is one of the three names of
Achilles' own contingent — "and they were called Myrmidons, and Hellenes, and
Achaeans" — and at 2.530 the lesser Ajax "with the spear surpassed all Hellenes and
Achaeans," a pairing that keeps the two distinct. It is not a name for the whole
host; that is Achaeans, Argives, Danaans.
**Refs:** 2.530, 2.684. Book 2.
*See also:* Hellas, Myrmidons, Achaeans, Achilles, Ajax son of Oïleus.

---

**Hellespont** (Ἑλλήσποντος) · PLACE
The strait beside the Achaean camp, always "strong-flowing" or "wide" or
"boundless," and the poem's standing figure for the far edge of the beachhead. Nestor
proposes the barrow of the unnamed dead be heaped "beside the wide Hellespont" so
that men sailing by in later ages will see it; Achilles threatens to load his ships
and be seen sailing it at earliest light; Poseidon's wall-destroying flood in Book
12 smooths the beach "along the strong-flowing Hellespont." It bounds the Thracian
contingent in the Catalogue and, in Priam's mind, the whole extent of his former
wealth.
**Refs:** 2.845, 7.86, 9.360, 12.30, 15.233, 17.432, 18.150, 23.2, 24.346, 24.545.
Books 2, 7, 9, 12, 15, 17–18, 23–24.
*See also:* Troy, Ida, Poseidon, Nestor, Achilles.

---

**Hephaestus** (Ἥφαιστος) · GOD
The smith-god, lamed and comic in Book 1 — where he bustles round the hall pouring
nectar to break up his parents' quarrel, and tells how Zeus once caught him by the
foot and flung him from the threshold of heaven to fall all day and land on Lemnos —
and formidable everywhere after. He made Agamemnon's sceptre and the houses of the
gods; his name stands by metonymy for fire ("the flame of Hephaestus," 2.426). His
great work is the armor and the shield of Achilles, forged at Thetis' asking, with
the earth, heaven, sea, two cities, the plowing and harvest, the vineyard, the herd
and the lions, the dancing-floor and the river of Ocean on it (18.478–608). In Book
21 Hera sets him on the Scamander: he burns the plain, then the river itself, until
the water boils in its channel and the god begs off.
*Epithets:* "crook-footed" (ἀμφιγυήεις, 1.607); "famed for craft" (κλυτότεχνης,
1.571); "famed" (περικλυτός, 1.607).
*Kin:* son of Hera (1.572, 21.330); husband of Charis (18.382).
**Refs:** 1.571, 1.600, 2.101, 2.426, 14.239, 18.137, 18.369, 18.473, 20.36,
21.342, 21.367, 21.381. Books 1–2, 5, 8–9, 14–15, 17–23.
*See also:* Hera, Thetis, Achilles, Charis, Scamander, Zeus, Lemnos.

---

**Hera** (Ἥρη) · GOD
Zeus's wife and sister, and the Achaeans' most relentless partisan — she will trade
Argos, Sparta and Mycenae, the three cities dearest to her, for the sack of Troy
(4.51–56), and Zeus says she would eat Priam and his sons raw if she could get at
them. She sets the poem going by putting the assembly into Achilles' mind (1.55),
and her Book 14 deception is its central act of divine mischief: she borrows the
kestos from Aphrodite, buys Sleep with the promise of Pasithea, and takes Zeus on
Gargaron so that Poseidon can turn the battle while he sleeps. Zeus's fury when he
wakes — and his reminder of the time he hung her from heaven with anvils on her
feet — does not change her; she extorts his consent for Troy's fall in Book 4 and
again in Book 21, and sends Hephaestus against the river to save Achilles.
*Epithets:* "white-armed" (λευκώλενος, 1.55); "ox-eyed lady Hera" (βοῶπις πότνια
Ἥρη, 1.551); "eldest goddess" (πρέσβα θεά, 5.721); "of the golden throne"
(χρυσόθρονος, 1.611).
*Kin:* daughter of Cronos and Rhea; sister and wife of Zeus; sister of Poseidon and
Hades; mother of Ares, Hebe and Hephaestus.
**Refs:** 1.55, 1.400, 1.536, 4.5, 4.50, 5.721, 8.198, 14.159, 14.292, 15.5, 19.407
…and throughout. Books 1–2, 4–5, 7–11, 13–16, 18–21, 24.
*See also:* Zeus, Athena, Sleep, Aphrodite, Hephaestus, Poseidon, Hebe, Ares.

---

**Heracles** (Ἡρακλῆς) · MORTAL
Always in the past tense, and always as the measure of a generation stronger than
this one. His Troy is the earlier sack: he came for the horses of Laomedon "with
only six ships and fewer men, and sacked the city of Ilion, and widowed her
streets," and Laomedon cheated him of the horses (5.638–651). Sleep and Hera between
them wrecked his homecoming and blew him to Cos, for which Zeus hurled gods through
the house; Poseidon shows Aeneas the wall the Trojans built him against the
sea-monster. Achilles takes him as the precedent for his own death — "not even the
might of Heracles escaped his doom" — and Agamemnon takes his birth as the great
instance of Blindness, when Hera's trick handed his rule to Eurystheus. He wounded
Hades at Pylos, and he battered Nestor's Pylos so hard that eleven of Neleus' twelve
sons were killed.
*Epithets:* "the might of Heracles" (βίη Ἡρακληείη, 2.658) — the periphrasis is
kept, and is the usual form of the name.
*Kin:* son of Zeus and Alcmene; father of Tlepolemus by Astyocheia and of Thessalus.
**Refs:** 2.658, 2.666, 5.638, 11.690, 14.255, 15.25, 18.117, 19.98, 20.145. Books
2, 5, 11, 14–15, 18–20.
*See also:* Zeus, Hera, Alcmene, Eurystheus, Laomedon, Tlepolemus, Sarpedon, Nestor.

---

**Hermes** (Ἑρμείας) · GOD
The gods' messenger and escort. He passes the sceptre from Zeus to Pelops in the
Book 2 genealogy, steals Ares out of the bronze jar where the sons of Aloeus had
him bound, fathers Eudorus on Polymele, and is paired against Leto in the theomachy —
where he declines to fight, saying it is a hard thing to trade blows with the wives
of Zeus. His great scene is Book 24: Zeus sends him to bring Priam through the
Achaean camp unseen, and he comes as a young man, takes the reins, drugs the
gate-keepers, opens the gate, identifies himself at Achilles' door, and comes back
before dawn to wake the old man and drive him out again. Every occurrence of "Argus"
in the poem is inside his fixed epithet.
*Epithets:* "the slayer of Argus" (ἀργεϊφόντης, 2.103); "the guide" (διάκτορος,
2.103) — rendered "the courier" in its later occurrences (20.34, 24.339).
*Kin:* son of Zeus; father of Eudorus by Polymele (16.181–186).
**Refs:** 2.103, 5.390, 14.490, 16.181, 20.35, 21.497, 24.334, 24.339, 24.461,
24.679, 24.694. Books 2, 5, 14–16, 20–21, 24.
*See also:* Zeus, Priam, Achilles, Eudorus, Leto, Ares, Pelops.

**Hicetaon** (Ἱκετάων) · MORTAL
One of Priam's brothers, a son of Laomedon, and one of the Trojan elders sitting at
the Scaean Gates in Book 3 who watch Helen come and say she is worth the suffering.
He does not fight. In the poem he functions mainly as a patronymic: his son
Melanippus, who had herded cattle at Percote until the war came close, is rebuked by
Hector into the front line and shot in the chest by Antilochus.
*Epithets:* "scion of Ares" (ὄζος Ἄρηος, 3.147), varied as "sprig of Ares" in the
genealogy at 20.238.
*Kin:* son of Laomedon; brother of Priam, Tithonus, Lampus and Clytius; father of
Melanippus.
**Refs:** 3.147, 15.546, 15.576, 20.238. Books 3, 15, 20.
*See also:* Priam, Laomedon, Melanippus, Antilochus, Hector.

---

**Hippasus** (Ἵππασος) · MORTAL
A name attached to men on both sides, never a character himself. On the Trojan side
he is father of Charops and of Socus, "such a pair of men," both killed by Odysseus
in Book 11 — Odysseus taunts the dying Socus as "son of war-minded Hippasus, breaker
of horses." A second Hippasus is the father of Hypsenor, killed by Deiphobus at
13.411; a third the father of Apisaon of Paeonia, killed by Lycomedes at 17.348.
The last two die in the same verbatim line, hit "in the liver below the midriff."
*Kin:* father of Charops and Socus; also named as father of Hypsenor and of Apisaon.
**Refs:** 11.426, 11.431, 11.450, 13.411, 17.348. Books 11, 13, 17.
*See also:* Socus, Charops, Odysseus, Hypsenor, Apisaon.

---

**Hippolochus** (Ἱππόλοχος) · MORTAL
Glaucus' father, son of Bellerophon and brother of Isander and Laodameia — so the
link between the Lycian house and Sarpedon, who is Laodameia's son by Zeus. He does
not appear: the name is Glaucus' patronymic throughout, and his one act reported is
sending his son to Troy with the charge "always to be the best, and to hold his head
above all others" (6.207–208), the line Peleus also gives Achilles. A second
Hippolochus is a son of Antimachus, killed with his brother Peisander by Agamemnon
in Book 11, who shears off his hands and his head and sends him rolling like a stone
through the crowd.
*Kin:* son of Bellerophon; father of Glaucus; brother of Isander and Laodameia.
**Refs:** 6.119, 6.144, 6.197, 6.206, 7.13, 11.122, 11.145, 12.309, 12.387, 17.140.
Books 6–7, 11–12, 17.
*See also:* Glaucus, Bellerophon, Sarpedon, Laodameia, Antimachus, Agamemnon.

---

**Hippothous** (Ἱππόθοος) · MORTAL
Two men. The chief one leads the Pelasgian spearmen of Larisa in the Catalogue with
his brother Pylaeus, and dies in Book 17 in the fight over Patroclus' body: he has
run a shield-strap round the dead man's ankle-tendons and is dragging him off by the
foot "doing a favor to Hector and the Trojans" when Telamonian Ajax spears him
through the helmet's cheek-piece and the brain runs out along the socket. He falls
face-down on the corpse, "far from deep-soiled Larisa; nor did he repay his dear
parents the price of his rearing." Priam's son Hippothous is a different man,
named once in the roll-call of the nine sons Priam curses (24.251).
*Kin:* the Pelasgian is son of Lethus, son of Teutamus; brother of Pylaeus.
*Killed by:* Ajax (17.293–303).
**Refs:** 2.840, 2.842, 17.217, 17.288, 17.313, 17.318, 24.251. Books 2, 17, 24.
*See also:* Lethus, Larisa, Pelasgians, Ajax, Patroclus, Priam.

---

**Hyle** (Ὕλη) · PLACE
A Boeotian town by the Cephisian lake, listed in the Catalogue. Two men are placed
there: Oresbius "of the glancing waist-guard," who lived there hoarding his wealth
with care and is killed by Hector in the Book 5 roll-call; and Tychius, "far the
best of leather-cutters," who made the seven-hide tower shield Ajax carries against
Hector.
**Refs:** 2.500, 5.708, 7.221. Books 2, 5, 7.
*See also:* Boeotians, Oresbius, Tychius, Ajax.

---

**Hyrtacus** (Ὕρτακος) · MORTAL
Asius' father, and in the poem only that: every occurrence is the patronymic "Asius,
Hyrtacus' son," including the couplet the poem repeats verbatim from the Catalogue
when Asius storms the wall — "whom horses carried from Arisbe, tall and fire-colored,
from the river Selleïs" (2.838 = 12.96). Hector names him twice in the same
formula when he searches the front for his missing captains in Book 13.
*Kin:* father of Asius, leader of the men of Percote, Sestus, Abydos and Arisbe.
**Refs:** 2.837, 2.838, 12.96, 12.110, 12.162, 13.760, 13.771. Books 2, 12–13.
*See also:* Asius, Arisbe, Percote, Adamas, Hector.

---

**Ida** (Ἴδη) · PLACE
The mountain above Troy, "of the many springs, mother of wild things," from whose
peaks the eight rivers of the Troad run down to the sea. It is Zeus's station for
most of the poem: he comes there from Olympus in Book 8, sits on Gargaron looking
out at the city and the ships, thunders from it, and sends Iris and Apollo out from
it with his orders. Hera climbs it to seduce him; the earth-quake of the theomachy
shakes "all the roots of many-springed Ida." Aeneas was got on its spurs, "a goddess
bedded with a mortal"; Apollo herded Laomedon's cattle "in the spurs of wooded,
many-folded Ida"; and Agamemnon's oath calls on "Father Zeus, ruling from Ida."
*Epithets:* "Ida of the many springs, mother of wild things" (πολυπίδαξ Ἴδη, μήτηρ
θηρῶν, 8.47).
**Refs:** 2.821, 3.276, 8.47, 11.184, 12.19, 14.283, 15.151, 20.59, 21.449.
Books 2–4, 7–8, 11–17, 20–24.
*See also:* Gargaron, Zeus, Hera, Troy, Scamander, Simois, Aeneas.

---

**Idaeus** (Ἰδαῖος) · MORTAL
Priam's herald, "wise" and "war-minded," and the poem's chief instrument of truce.
He carries the oath-offerings and the mixing-bowl for the Book 3 duel, and rouses
Priam to come out to the plain. In Book 7 he and Talthybius force their staffs
between Ajax and Hector to stop the duel at nightfall; then he goes alone to the
Achaean assembly with Paris' offer of the treasure but not of Helen, hears it
refused, and takes Agamemnon's answer back — the truce for the burning of the dead.
In Book 24 he drives the mule-wagon of ransom to Achilles' hut and is left holding
the teams outside. A different Idaeus is a son of Dares the Hephaestus-priest, who
leaps from his chariot in Book 5 and is snatched away in darkness by Hephaestus so
that the old man should not lose both sons.
*Epithets:* "the far-calling herald" (ἠπύτα κῆρυξ, 7.384).
**Refs:** 3.247, 5.11, 5.20, 7.276, 7.372, 7.381, 7.413, 24.325, 24.470. Books 3,
5, 7, 24.
*See also:* Priam, Talthybius, Hector, Ajax, Dares, Hephaestus, Achilles.

---

**Idomeneus** (Ἰδομενεύς) · MORTAL
Leader of the Cretans of hundred-citied Crete, with Meriones, and one of the
Achaean elder statesmen — Agamemnon gives him a cup that is always full and honors
him "beyond all the Danaans of swift colts." He is one of the men Achilles names in
Book 1 as fit to arbitrate, and one of the nine who volunteer against Hector. His
aristeia fills the middle of Book 13: Poseidon, in the likeness of Thoas, sends him
back into the fight, and though "his hair was half-grey" he kills Othryoneus,
Asius, Alcathous and Oenomaus before Aeneas comes at him and he gives ground,
admitting his legs are no longer what they were. In Book 23 he is first to see
Diomedes coming out of the dust and quarrels with the lesser Ajax over whose horses
lead.
*Epithets:* "famed with the spear" (δουρὶ κλυτός, 2.645).
*Kin:* son of Deucalion (13.451); grandson of Minos.
*Kills:* Othryoneus (13.370–373); Asius (13.387–393); Alcathous, Oenomaus, Erymas.
**Refs:** 1.145, 2.645, 4.252, 4.265, 13.210, 13.361, 13.370, 13.467, 23.450.
Books 1–8, 10–13, 15–17, 19, 23.
*See also:* Meriones, Cretans, Poseidon, Asius, Othryoneus, Aeneas, Deucalion.

**Ilioneus** (Ἰλιονεύς) · MORTAL
A Trojan, the only son of Phorbas "rich in flocks, whom Hermes loved beyond all the
Trojans, and gave him wealth." Peneleos spears him under the brow at the root of the
eye, pushes the eyeball out, and drives the point through the nape; then draws his
sword, hews through the neck, and holds up head, helmet and the spear still standing
in the eye "like a poppy-head" to show the Trojans — telling them to carry word to
Ilioneus' father and mother to start wailing. It is the poem's grimmest single
image, and the Trojans' limbs shake at the sight.
*Kin:* only son of Phorbas.
*Killed by:* Peneleos (14.489–500).
**Refs:** 14.489, 14.492, 14.502. Book 14.
*See also:* Peneleos, Phorbas, Hermes, Promachus.

---

**Ilus** (Ἶλος) · MORTAL
An ancestor of the Trojan kings, given in Aeneas' recital of the line at
20.231–236: Tros' eldest son, brother of Assaracus and Ganymede, and father of
Laomedon — so Priam's grandfather. The poem does not gloss his name against Ilion.
He is dead long before the action, and what survives him is his tomb, "the man-made
mound," a fixed landmark in the middle of the plain: Hector holds his night council
beside it, Agamemnon's rout streams past it, Paris shoots Diomedes from behind its
pillar, and Priam and Idaeus drive past it on the road to the ships.
*Kin:* son of Tros; brother of Assaracus and Ganymede; father of Laomedon;
great-grandfather of Hector.
**Refs:** 10.415, 11.166, 11.372, 20.232, 20.236, 24.349. Books 10–11, 20, 24.
*See also:* Tros, Laomedon, Priam, Assaracus, Dardanus, Troy.

---

**Imbros** (Ἴμβρος) · PLACE
An island off Troy, always "rugged," and in the poem a marker of the sea-road
rather than a place with people. Poseidon's undersea cave lies "midway between
Tenedos and rugged Imbros"; Iris dives into the sea "midway between Samos and rugged
Imbros" on her way to Thetis; Hera and Sleep pass over it and Lemnos on the way to
Ida. Eëtion of Imbros is the guest-friend who ransomed Lycaon out of Lemnos, and
Hecuba names it in her lament as one of the places across the sea Achilles sold her
sons into.
**Refs:** 13.33, 14.281, 21.43, 24.78, 24.753. Books 13–14, 21, 24.
*See also:* Lemnos, Samos, Tenedos, Poseidon, Eëtion, Lycaon.

**Iphiclus** (Ἴφικλος) · MORTAL
Father of Protesilaus and Podarces, and "rich in flocks," son of Phylacus; he does
not appear, and both his sons are named by the patronymic when the Phylacean
contingent is handed on from the first man killed at Troy to his younger brother
(2.703–708, 13.698). The Iphiclus Nestor claims to have outrun in the games at
Buprasium — "good runner though he was" — belongs to the same back-story generation,
but the text does not say he is the same man.
*Kin:* son of Phylacus; father of Protesilaus and Podarces.
**Refs:** 2.705, 13.698, 23.636. Books 2, 13, 23.
*See also:* Protesilaus, Podarces, Phylacus, Nestor, Phylace.

**Iphidamas** (Ἰφιδάμας) · MORTAL
Antenor's son, "a good man and a big one," and the answer to the Muse-question of
who first came against Agamemnon in his aristeia. Reared in Thrace by his mother's
father Cisses, who tried to keep him there by giving him his daughter, he left the
bridal chamber "chasing the report of the Achaeans" with twelve ships, beached them
at Percote, and walked the rest of the way to Ilion. His thrust bends on Agamemnon's
war-belt "like lead"; Agamemnon wrenches the spear from his grip and cuts his neck,
and he "slept a sleep of bronze," far from the wife he had bought with a hundred
cattle and a promise of a thousand goats and sheep. His brother Coon wounds
Agamemnon in the forearm trying to drag the body clear and is beheaded over it.
*Kin:* son of Antenor and Theano; grandson of Cisses; brother of Coon.
*Killed by:* Agamemnon (11.238–241).
**Refs:** 11.221, 11.234, 11.257, 11.261. Book 11.
*See also:* Antenor, Coon, Agamemnon, Theano, Cisses, Percote.
