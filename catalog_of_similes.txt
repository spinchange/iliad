A CATALOG OF SIMILES

A companion to the translation


FOREWORD

The extended simile is Homer's signature instrument, and the Iliad
is its greatest gallery: some three hundred and forty comparisons,
nearly two hundred of them developed past a single line — little
poems set into the poem, each opening on a world the narrative
itself is forbidden to visit. For the strange fact about the
Iliad's similes is that almost none of them are about war. They
are about weather, herdsmen, harvests, insects, children, women
working wool for a pitiful wage; the war is the tenor, but the
vehicle is peace. The poem of fifty-one days on a single plain
keeps, in its similes, a second poem — of seasons, of the whole
Mediterranean year, of ordinary work and ordinary grief — and it
is through that second poem that the deaths of the first one
land. A boy's head drops like a garden poppy heavy with rain; an
army holds its line the way a spinner holds her scales level; a
king's grief at a pyre is a father's at his son's. The simile is
where the Iliad admits what the world is otherwise like, and how
much of it the war is costing.

Technically the Homeric simile runs long past the point of
comparison. It begins from one shared feature — speed, noise,
stubbornness — and then keeps going, accreting detail the
narrative does not need: the shepherd who hears the torrents from
far off, the sailors who will never profit from the beacon they
see, the boys whose strength is "a child's strength." Critics
once treated the overflow as decoration. It is the opposite: the
overflow is the meaning, the place where the poem's attention
escapes the battlefield and dwells, for four or five lines, on
the world that is not fighting. Then the pivot — "so," the
hinge-word — swings shut, and we are back on the plain.

This catalog is a showcase, not a census. It gathers the similes
of this translation that seemed most worth setting side by side —
including, without apology, a few formal comparisons and outright
metaphors, and one great inversion — arranged by theme rather
than by book, so that the poem's families of images can be seen
as families: the lions together, the fires together, the mothers
together. Every quotation is verbatim from the translation and
cited by the Greek line numbers the translation shares; where a
quoted sentence runs on past its excerpt, the quotation ends at a
line-break and the ellipsis is the reader's to imagine. Notes on
recurrence are added where the poem repeats an image across
books, because that too is part of the art.


I. LIONS, WOLVES, AND THE BEASTS OF PREY

The predators are the war-poem's native heraldry, but Homer's are
never mere emblems: they are hungry, harried, often failing, and
the poem's sympathy drifts between the lion and the herdsman with
perfect impartiality.

11.548-557 · Ajax forced back from the ships

    As a fiery lion from a yard of cattle
    is driven off by dogs and countrymen,
    who will not let him take the fat of the herd,
    watching all night long; and he, craving the meat,
    charges in but gets nowhere, for javelins thick and fast
    fly out to meet him from daring hands,
    and burning brands besides, and he flinches at those for all his fury,
    and at dawn he goes off apart, sullen at heart;
    so Ajax then, sullen at heart, fell back from the Trojans,
    much against his will; for he feared for the Achaeans' ships.

  The greatest of the defeated-lion similes: all night's hunger,
  and nothing for it but dawn and sullenness.

16.156-163 · The Myrmidons arming

    And they, like wolves
    that eat flesh raw, hearts full of unspeakable courage,
    who have brought down a great antlered stag in the mountains
    and tear it, and every muzzle among them is red with blood;
    and in a pack they go to a spring of dark water
    to lap with their thin tongues the black surface-water,
    belching up clotted gore; and the heart in their chests
    is fearless still, though every belly is stretched:

  Homer's most physical pack-image — the thin tongues, the black
  water — for an army that has been starved of war for weeks.

18.318-323 · Achilles begins the lament

    like a bearded lion
    whose cubs a deer-hunter has snatched away
    out of the thick wood; and he comes back too late, and grieves,
    and goes down glen after glen tracking the man's footprints,
    hunting him out — for bitter anger takes hold of him:
    so, groaning heavily, Achilles spoke among the Myrmidons:

  Grief rendered as a predator's grief: too late, and already
  turning into pursuit. The whole last third of the poem is in
  this simile.

20.164-173 · Achilles comes out to meet Aeneas

    And the son of Peleus rose from the other side to meet him like a lion,
    a raider, whom men are wild to kill —
    a whole district, gathered; and at first he comes on
    heedless of them, but when one of the battle-quick young men
    hits him with a spear, he crouches, jaws gaping, foam
    gathering on his teeth, and the brave heart in him groans,
    and with his tail he lashes his ribs and flanks
    on both sides, and whips himself on to fight,
    and glaring he drives straight in, to see whether he will kill
    some man, or die himself in the front of the crowd:

  The lion literally whips himself into battle — the tail on the
  flanks — as though even fury had to be goaded.

21.573-580 · Agenor stands alone at the gates

    As a leopard comes out of deep brush
    to face the huntsman, and her heart neither
    fears nor thinks of flight when she hears the baying —
    for even if he is quicker, and stabs or hits her with a cast,
    still, run through on the very spear, she does not leave off
    her valor, until she closes with him or goes down:
    so the son of proud Antenor, brilliant Agenor,
    refused to run before he had put Achilles to the test.

  Courage defined from inside the doomed animal: run through on
  the very spear, she does not leave off.

22.93-97 · Hector waits for Achilles

    And as a mountain snake at his hole waits for a man,
    fed full of evil poisons, and the terrible anger has entered him,
    and he glares dreadfully, coiling around his hole:
    so Hector, holding his fury unquenchable, would not give way,
    his shining shield propped on the jut of the tower;

  The hole is the point: the snake defends the one place it
  cannot leave. Hector's hole is Troy.

22.308-311 · Hector's last charge

    like the high-flying eagle
    that drops to the plain through the lowering clouds
    to snatch a soft lamb or a cowering hare:
    so Hector swooped, shaking his sharp sword.

  For his final act the poem lends the dying man its supreme
  predator — and lets the softness of the prey say the rest.

21.22-26 · Achilles in the river

    As before a huge-mawed dolphin the other fish
    flee and fill the inlets of a fair-anchoring harbor,
    terrified — for he devours whatever he catches:
    so the Trojans down the channels of the terrible river
    cowered under the overhangs.

  The one marine predator in the poem, produced at the exact
  moment the battlefield turns to water.


II. THE SMALL, THE STUBBORN, AND THE SWARM

Homer will compare heroes to anything that has the required
virtue — including vermin. The insect similes are among the
poem's proudest: smallness observed without condescension.

11.558-565 · Ajax again — the donkey

    As when a donkey going past a cornfield masters the boys who drive him,
    a slow one, on whose ribs many sticks have been broken,
    and he gets in and crops the deep grain, while the boys
    beat him with sticks — but their strength is a child's strength —
    and only just drive him out when he has eaten his fill,
    so then great Ajax, Telamon's son, was harried
    by the high-hearted Trojans and their allies called from many lands,

  Back-to-back with the lion (11.548-557) for the same retreat:
  first the noble reading, then the true one. No other ancient
  poet would have dared the pairing; none ever honored an army's
  best defensive fighter with a beaten donkey who wins.

16.259-267 · The Myrmidons pour out

    At once they came pouring out like roadside wasps
    that boys are forever provoking, forever tormenting —
    wasps with their nests along the road — as boys will,
    the little fools: they make a common evil for many.
    If some traveling man going by then stirs the wasps
    without meaning it, out they come with fearless hearts,
    every one flying at him, defending their young:

  The Trojans are the boys who kept poking; the innocent
  passer-by gets the war. Few similes carry so much quiet blame.

17.570-573 · Athena puts courage into Menelaus

    and set in his chest the boldness of the fly,
    which — beaten off again and again from a man's skin —
    persists in biting; and human blood is sweet to it:
    with boldness like that she filled his darkened heart,

  A goddess's gift, and the poem chooses the fly: the least
  dignified and most indestructible courage there is.

16.641-644 · Around the body of Sarpedon

    And they crowded round the corpse always — as flies
    in a farmstead buzz about the brimming milk-pails
    in the season of spring, when the milk wets the vessels:
    so they crowded round the corpse.

  The spring milking (first heard at 2.469-471, of armies
  mustering) returns over a dead son of Zeus: abundance and
  carrion in a single image.

21.12-16 · The rout driven into Xanthus

    As when locusts rise on the air before the rush of fire
    and flee toward a river: the unwearying flame burns on,
    leaping up suddenly, and they cower down in the water:
    so before Achilles the sounding stream of Xanthus,
    the deep-eddying, was choked with horses and men together.

  Fire driving living things into water — the simile knows, three
  hundred lines early, exactly how this book will end for the
  river himself.

3.2-7 · The Trojan advance

    like the clamor of cranes that rises against the sky,
    when they flee the winter and the measureless rain
    and fly clamoring toward the streams of Ocean,
    bearing slaughter and doom to the Pygmy men,
    and out of the morning air they carry their evil strife —

  The poem's first marching simile reaches to the edge of the
  known world — and against it, in the next line, the Achaeans
  come on in silence.

2.459-466 · The muster on the plain

    And as the many tribes of winged birds —
    geese, or cranes, or long-necked swans —
    on the Asian meadow around the streams of Caystrius
    fly this way and that, glorying in their wings,
    settling with cries, always advancing, and the meadow rings —
    so their many tribes from the ships and huts
    poured out onto the Scamandrian plain, and beneath them the ground
    thundered terribly under the feet of men and horses.

  From the great chain of five similes that heralds the Catalogue
  of Ships: the army as wildfowl, glorying in sheer number.

3.151-153 · The old men on the wall

    but speakers
    of quality, like cicadas that in the woods
    perch on a tree and send out their lily-thin voice:

  Age done in six words: lily-thin voice.

8.306-308 · The death of Gorgythion

    And as a poppy in a garden lets its head fall sideways,
    heavy with its own seed and the rains of spring,
    so his head bowed to one side under the weight of the helmet.

  Perhaps the most beautiful death in the poem — a boy nobody
  remembers, given the one flower-simile in the Iliad.


III. FIRE, WEATHER, AND THE SEA

The poem's largest family. Armies are weather in the Iliad —
they gather, darken, break, and pass — and the men inside them
are the farmers and sailors the weather happens to.

2.455-458 · The bronze of the advancing army

    As an annihilating fire blazes up through a boundless forest
    on the peaks of a mountain, and the glare shows from far away,
    so, as they marched, from the tremendous bronze
    a gleam went flashing through the upper air to the sky.

4.422-428 · The Danaan companies move

    As when on a booming beach the swell of the sea
    rises, wave on wave, under the West Wind's driving:
    out on the deep it first rears its crest, and then
    breaking on the land it thunders aloud, and around the headlands
    it arches and comes to a peak and spits out the salt foam:
    so wave on wave the companies of the Danaans moved
    without pause into war;

4.452-456 · The armies meet

    As when winter torrents pouring down the mountains
    hurl together their weight of water at a meeting of glens
    out of great springs, deep in a hollow ravine,
    and far off in the mountains a shepherd hears the thunder of it:
    such was the roar and the toil of them as they came together.

  The shepherd is the surplus that makes it Homer: someone
  harmless, far away, hearing the collision he is spared.

14.16-20 · Nestor's indecision

    As when the great open sea darkens with soundless swell,
    boding the quick paths of the shrill winds —
    only boding: it neither rolls forward nor to either side,
    until some deciding blast comes down from Zeus:
    so the old man weighed it, his heart torn two ways —

  A state of mind as a state of water: the swell before the wind
  chooses. Homer's psychology is meteorological.

15.618-622 · The Danaan line holds

    they held, locked like a tower — like a rock-face,
    sheer and huge, hard by the grey salt water,
    that abides the quick paths of the shrill winds
    and the swollen waves belching against it:
    so the Danaans stood the Trojans, steady, and did not run.

15.624-628 · Hector falls on them

    and fell on them as a wave falls on a swift ship,
    wind-fed, ravening, out of the clouds: and the whole ship
    is buried in spray, and the terrible blast of the wind
    roars in the sail, and the sailors tremble at heart,
    terrified: for they are carried only just out from under death:

  Cliff and wave, back to back, for the same collision — then the
  poem goes below decks to the sailors, where the fear lives.

13.137-143 · Hector charges

    like a boulder off a cliff-face
    that a river swollen with winter shoves down the ledge,
    breaking with prodigious rain the shameless rock's moorings;
    bounding high it flies, and the timber crashes
    under it; and it runs on unswerving, steady, until it reaches
    the level plain — and there it rolls no more, for all its rush:
    so Hector for a while threatened to sweep clean to the sea,

  The simile promises the stop before the narrative delivers it:
  the plain is already waiting for the boulder.

14.414-418 · Hector felled by the stone

    As when an oak goes down under the stroke of father Zeus,
    torn out by the roots, and the terrible smell of sulphur
    rises off it, and courage fails whoever sees it
    from close by — for hard is the bolt of great Zeus:
    so Hector's fury dropped in an instant to the dust.

  You can smell this simile — the sulphur of the split oak is the
  only odor in all the poem's weather.

12.278-289 · The stones fly thick as snow

    And as flakes of snow fall thick and fast on a winter's day,
    when Zeus the counselor has bestirred himself to snow
    on mankind, and shows them what his missiles are,
    and lulling the winds he pours it down steadily, until he has covered
    the peaks of the high mountains, and the jutting headlands,
    and the clover meadows, and men's rich worked fields;
    and it is poured out over the harbors and shores of the grey sea,
    and only the wave, breaking on it, keeps it off; but all things else
    are wrapped from above, when the storm of Zeus drives down upon it:
    so from both sides their stones flew thick and fast,

  The gentlest storm in the poem, spread over eight lines for a
  hail of stones: the world silenced and whitened down to the one
  black edge of sea that will not take the snow.

16.384-392 · The Trojan mares in rout

    And as the whole black earth is weighed down under a storm
    on a day of harvest-season, when Zeus pours down
    his most violent water, when in rage he turns bitter
    against men who by force render crooked judgments in the assembly
    and drive justice out, careless of the gods' vengeance:
    all their rivers rise in flood as they run,
    and the torrents cut the hillsides into gullies,
    and down to the darkening sea they roar headlong
    from the mountains, groaning huge, and the works of men are diminished:

  The one simile in the Iliad where Zeus's weather is moral — the
  flood sent against crooked judges — set, with terrible tact, in
  the middle of a rout the gods engineered.

20.490-494 · The aristeia begins to burn

    As unearthly fire rages through the deep glens
    of a parched mountain, and the deep forest burns,
    and the wind, driving it everywhere, whirls the flame around:
    so everywhere he stormed with his spear, like more than man,
    harrying the men he killed; and the black earth ran with blood.

3.221-223 · Odysseus speaking

    But when he let the great voice out of his chest,
    and the words came like snowflakes in winter,
    then no other mortal could rival Odysseus.

  Words as weather: dense, continuous, covering everything.

19.357-361 · The army pours out for the last battle

    As when thick snowflakes flutter down from Zeus,
    cold under the blast of Boreas born in the clear sky,
    so thick then the helmets, glittering bright,
    streamed out of the ships, and the bossed shields,
    the strong-plated corslets and the ash-wood spears.


IV. STARS, FIRE-LIGHT, AND SMOKE

Light in the Iliad is almost always a warning. The loveliest
lights in the poem — stars, beacons, harvest-fires — mark
watchfires of the enemy, armor coming to kill, cities already
burning.

8.555-561 · The Trojan watchfires

    As when in the sky the stars around the shining moon
    appear in all their glory, when the air falls windless,
    and all the lookout-peaks show clear, and the jutting headlands,
    and the glens; and from heaven the measureless air breaks open,
    and every star is seen; and the shepherd's heart is glad:
    so many, between the ships and the streams of Xanthus,
    shone the fires the Trojans burned before Ilion.

  The most famous night in Greek poetry. The glad shepherd looks
  up at a thousand fires that mean, for the Achaeans below them,
  that tomorrow may be the end. (The clear-air lines return,
  verbatim as the Greek returns them, at 16.297-300 — where the
  sky that "breaks open" is relief itself, the fire thrust back
  from the ships.)

22.26-32 · Achilles first seen from the wall

    streaking across the plain, blazing like the star
    that rises at harvest-time, and its rays shine clear
    among many stars in the milking-dark of night —
    the star men call by name the Dog of Orion.
    Brightest of all it is; and it is made an evil sign,
    and brings great fever down on wretched mortals:
    so shone the bronze on his chest as he ran.

  Priam watches beauty and pestilence arrive as one thing. The
  whole tragedy of Achilles is in the astronomy: brightest of
  all, and an evil sign.

22.317-321 · The point of the spear

    And as a star moves among stars in the milking-dark of night —
    Hesperus, the most beautiful star that stands in heaven —
    so the light went from the keen point that Achilles
    balanced in his right hand, purposing evil for brilliant Hector,
    searching the beautiful flesh for where it would yield the most.

  The evening star, the most beautiful in heaven, is the last
  light Hector sees: the poem's cruelest gift of loveliness.

18.207-214 · The fire over Achilles' head

    As when smoke rises from a city into the upper air,
    far off, from an island that enemies have surrounded,
    where all day long the men are put to the test of hateful war
    from their own city walls; and then, with the setting sun,
    beacon-fires blaze up one after another, and the glare
    shoots high for the neighboring islanders to see,
    in hope that they may come with ships as warders-off of ruin:
    so from the head of Achilles the light went up to the upper air.

  The besieged island calling for rescue — a signal of hope in
  the narrative, and pure terror to those it faces.

19.374-379 · The light of the new shield

    he took up, and its light went out far, like the moon's.
    As when out at sea a light shows to sailors,
    the glare of a burning fire, that burns high in the mountains
    at a lonely steading — while the storm-winds carry them,
    unwilling, over the fish-filled sea away from their dear ones:
    so from Achilles' shield the light went up to the upper air,

  The counter-image to the rescue-beacon of Book 18: this time
  the sailors see the light and are blown away from it, from home
  and their dear ones — Achilles' own future, glimpsed in his own
  armor.

21.522-525 · Achilles at the walls

    As when smoke rises and reaches the wide heaven
    from a burning city — the wrath of the gods has sent it up,
    and it lays labor on all, and grief on many:
    so Achilles laid labor and grief on the Trojans.

  A simile that is also a prophecy: Troy's actual smoke, held one
  book beyond the poem's edge, rises here on loan.


V. THE WORLD OF WORK

The Iliad's peacetime world survives chiefly here: the whole
economy of field, byre, workshop, and kitchen, carried into the
battle one simile at a time.

5.499-503 · The armies whitened with dust

    As the wind carries chaff across the sacred threshing-floors
    when men are winnowing, and tawny-haired Demeter
    in the driving wind divides the grain from the husks,
    and the chaff-heaps whiten: so the Achaeans then
    turned white from above with dust,

13.588-592 · An arrow glances off

    As off a broad winnowing-shovel, on a great threshing-floor,
    the dark-skinned beans or the chickpeas jump
    under the shrill wind and the winnower's swing,
    so from the corslet of glorious Menelaus
    the bitter arrow glanced far off, turned hard away.

  Death deflected into the kitchen-garden: the arrow becomes a
  bean off the shovel.

13.703-708 · The two Ajaxes

    but as in fallow land two wine-dark oxen strain
    at the jointed plow with one and the same heart, and around
    the roots of their horns the sweat wells up in streams,
    and only the polished yoke holds the two apart
    as they drive down the furrow, and the plow cuts to the field's edge:
    so the two of them took their stand, shoulder to shoulder.

  Wine-dark, elsewhere the sea's word, given to two oxen: the
  poem's highest compliment to teamwork.

17.742-746 · Carrying the body of Patroclus

    And they — like mules who have thrown their great strength into the work,
    dragging down from the mountain along a rugged track
    a roof-beam or a huge ship-timber, and inside them the heart
    is worn with weariness and sweat together as they strain:
    so the two of them, straining, carried the body.

  Grief as haulage. The dead friend has the weight of a
  roof-beam, and the poem does not flinch from saying so.

12.421-423 · Fighting across the breastwork

    But as two men quarrel over boundary-stones,
    measuring-rods in hand, in a common field,
    who in a little strip of ground contend over the fair share:

  The wall of the Achaeans reduced to a farmers' property-line
  dispute — war seen suddenly from very high up.

12.433-435 · The battle in balance

    as an honest working woman holds the scales,
    who with the weight in one pan and the wool in the other
    draws them level, to win a pitiful wage for her children:

  The famous spinner: the exact center of the poem's compassion.
  Two armies hang level in the scales of a woman working piece-
  rates to feed her children.

15.410-413 · Deadlock at the ships

    But as a chalk-line trues a ship-timber straight
    in the hands of a skilled carpenter, who knows
    his whole craft well by the promptings of Athena,
    so their battle and their war were drawn even and taut.

18.600-601 · The dancers on the Shield

    as when a potter, seated, tries
    the wheel fitted to his hands, to see if it will spin —

  The craftsman's simile inside the craftsman's masterpiece:
  Hephaestus's dancers spin like the wheel a potter tests.

17.389-393 · The tug-of-war over the corpse

    As when a man gives his people the hide of a great bull
    to stretch, soaked through with fat:
    they take it and stand in a circle and stretch it
    outward; and quickly the moisture goes, and the fat sinks in
    with so many pulling, and the whole hide is stretched taut through:

  The most unsparing simile in the poem: Patroclus's body as the
  hide in the tanners' circle. Homer honors the dead man by
  refusing to look away from what a body is.

16.406-410 · Patroclus hooks a man from his chariot

    and hauled him by the spear-shaft over the chariot-rail — as a man
    sitting on a jutting rock hauls a sacred fish
    out of the sea with his line and his glittering bronze:
    so he hauled him gaping from the chariot on the shining spear
    and tumbled him down on his face; and the life left him as he fell.

  Gruesome and delicate at once — the gaping mouth belongs to
  both halves of the comparison.

21.257-264 · The river gains on Achilles

    As when a ditch-tending man leads a stream of water
    from a dark spring down through his plantings and gardens,
    mattock in hand, knocking the clogs from the channel,
    and as it flows all the pebbles are rolled and tumbled
    under it, and it runs murmuring quickly down
    the sloping ground, and outruns the very man who guides it:
    so the wave of the current forever caught Achilles,
    fast as he was: gods are stronger than men.

  The fastest man alive outrun by garden irrigation. The simile's
  last clause is the whole theology of the poem in six words.

15.679-684 · Ajax defends the decks

    And as a man well skilled in trick-riding,
    who has yoked together four horses picked from many
    and comes sweeping in from the plain toward the great city
    along the public road — and many marvel at him,
    men and women both — while he, steady, sure, and always
    leaping, changes from horse to horse as they fly along:

  A circus act at the poem's most desperate hour: Ajax leaping
  ship to ship becomes the stunt-rider the whole town turns out
  to watch.

23.760-763 · Odysseus runs a close second

    and brilliant Odysseus drove in
    close behind — close as the weaving-rod to the breast
    of a fair-girdled woman, when she pulls it deftly in her hands,
    drawing the spool out along the warp, and holds the rod
    near her breast: so close Odysseus ran,

  Distance measured in the most intimate unit the poem knows: the
  space between a weaving-woman's rod and her breast.

24.80-82 · Iris dives into the sea

    Down she shot to the bottom like the lead sinker
    that rides the horn of a field-ox down the line
    and goes among the ravening fish, carrying death.

  A god's descent weighted with fishing-tackle — and even here,
  in three lines, the little engine ends on death.

4.141-147 · The wound of Menelaus

    As when some woman of Maeonia or Caria stains
    ivory with crimson, to be a cheek-piece for horses;
    it lies in a treasure-room, and many horsemen
    have prayed to carry it; but it lies there, a king's treasure,
    both ornament for the horse and glory for the driver:
    so, Menelaus, your shapely thighs were stained
    with blood, your shins, and your fine ankles beneath.

  Blood on skin as craft — the craftswoman, the treasure-room,
  the waiting buyers — for the wound that nearly ends the war.

5.902-904 · Ares healed

    As when fig-juice thickens the white milk at a stir,
    liquid before, and it curdles fast as a man turns it,
    so quickly he healed rushing Ares.

  Divine medicine as cheese-making: the homeliest miracle in the
  poem.

21.362-365 · The river boils

    As a cauldron boils within, hurried by a great fire,
    melting down the lard of a fatted hog,
    bubbling up on every side, with dry split wood beneath it:
    so his beautiful streams blazed with the fire, and the water boiled,

  A god cooked in his own bed like Sunday lard: the Theomachy's
  comedy at full heat.

15.362-366 · Apollo kicks down the wall

    as when a boy by the sea
    heaps sand into shapes in his childish play,
    and then, still playing, scatters them again with his feet and hands:
    so you, archer Phoebus, poured into ruin the long
    labor and misery of the Argives,

  The most frightening simile in the Iliad, precisely because
  nothing in it is frightening: the Achaean wall — books of
  labor, blood, and prayer — is a child's sandcastle to a god.


VI. TREES

Men fall like trees in every epic; Homer planted a whole forest
first, so each felling costs something particular.

6.146-149 · Glaucus to Diomedes

    As the generation of leaves, so is the generation of men.
    The wind pours some leaves to the ground, and others the forest
    grows in their fullness when the season of spring comes on:
    so with the generations of men — one grows, one dies away.

  The most quoted lines in the poem, and the only simile spoken
  as an argument: asked for his lineage before combat, Glaucus
  answers that lineage is foliage. (Apollo repeats the thought
  from above at 21.464-466, as contempt; Glaucus made it
  dignity.)

4.482-487 · The death of Simoeisius

    and he fell to the ground in the dust
    like a poplar that has grown up in the wet meadow
    of a great marsh — smooth, with the branches growing
    at its very top — and a chariot-maker with the bright iron
    fells it, to bend a wheel-rim for a beautiful car,
    and it lies there seasoning along the river bank:

  The poem's first great tree-death. The boy becomes timber
  before our eyes — felled for someone else's beautiful car, left
  seasoning by the river where he was born.

16.482-486 · The death of Sarpedon

    He fell as an oak falls, or a white poplar,
    or a towering pine, that carpenters up in the mountains
    cut down with fresh-whetted axes to be a ship's timber:
    so he lay stretched out before his horses and chariot,
    bellowing, clawing at the blood-soaked dust.

  Zeus's own son gets all three trees at once — and then the
  simile hands him back, not timber after all but a man
  clawing the dust.

17.53-58 · The death of Euphorbus

    As a man rears a flourishing olive sapling
    in a lonely place, where water wells up in plenty —
    a fair shoot, and a thriving; and the breathing of every
    wind rocks it, and it breaks thick with white blossom;
    but suddenly a wind comes with a great squall
    and wrenches it out of its trench and lays it flat on the earth:

  The tenderest of the tree-deaths: not wild growth but a
  nursling, somebody's cherished planting, taken by the same
  element that rocked it.


VII. THE HUMAN HEART

The similes that reach past nature into the household: mothers,
children, labor-pangs, dreams. Here the war-poem keeps its
tenderness, and pays for it.

4.130-131 · Athena deflects the arrow

    She brushed it back from the skin just so far as a mother
    brushes a fly from her child when he lies in sweet sleep,

  War-goddess as nursery-mother, for exactly one and a half
  lines.

16.7-10 · Achilles to the weeping Patroclus

    like a little girl
    running at her mother's side, begging to be carried,
    clutching her skirt, dragging at her hurrying steps,
    and looking up at her in tears until she lifts her:

  Teasing on the surface, and underneath it the poem's whole
  reading of the friendship: Patroclus will get what the child
  gets — to be lifted and carried — but not alive.

9.323-327 · Achilles on his years of war

    As a bird carries to her unfledged chicks
    every morsel she takes, and it goes hard with herself,
    so I have lain awake through many an unsleeping night
    and fought through bloody days of battle,
    contending with men for the sake of their women.

  Achilles' own simile for himself: not lion or fire but a mother
  bird going hungry. It is the strangest self-portrait in Greek
  poetry, and it is spoken in anger.

11.269-272 · Agamemnon's wound

    As when the sharp shaft of labor seizes a woman in her pangs,
    the piercing shaft the Eileithyiai of hard labor send,
    Hera's daughters, keepers of the bitter pangs,
    so sharp pains sank into the strength of Atreus' son.

  The king of men measured against childbirth — the one pain the
  heroic world concedes it cannot rank above its own.

17.4-6 · Menelaus over the body

    as a mother-cow, lowing,
    stands over her first calf, who never knew birth before:
    so tawny-haired Menelaus stood over Patroclus.

  First calf: the inexperience is the tenderness. The poem's
  gentlest fighter gets its gentlest animal.

23.222-224 · Achilles at the pyre

    As a father mourns, burning the bones of his son,
    a bridegroom, whose death has grieved his unhappy parents:
    so Achilles mourned, burning the bones of his companion,

  No animal, no weather — by Book 23 the only adequate comparison
  for grief is other grief.

9.14-15 · Agamemnon before the assembly

    letting fall tears like a spring of dark water
    that pours its black stream down a goat-proof cliff.

  The image returns at 16.3-4 for Patroclus's tears — the poem's
  two great weepings, king and friend, drawn from one spring.

15.80-83 · Hera flies to Olympus

    As when the mind of a man darts, who has traveled
    over much country, and thinks in his quick wits
    "would I were here, or there," and wishes himself many places,
    so swiftly, straining, flew the lady Hera;

  The speed of thought, discovered eight centuries before it
  became a cliché — and given, precisely, to the traveled mind:
  you can only wish yourself where you have been.

6.506-511 · Paris comes down from the citadel

    As when a stalled horse, corn-fed at the manger,
    breaks his tether and gallops drumming across the plain,
    used to bathing in the fair-flowing river,
    glorying: he holds his head high, and the mane
    streams along his shoulders; sure of his own splendor,
    his knees carry him lightly to the haunts and pastures of horses:

  Pure animal joy, morally weightless — which is the judgment:
  this is Paris. When the same lines return at 15.263-268 they
  describe Hector, and mean courage. One simile, two verdicts;
  the poem trusts placement to tell them apart.

17.434-438 · The horses of Achilles weep

    As a grave-stone stands unmoving, set on the barrow
    of a dead man or a woman,
    so they stood, unstirring, with the beautiful chariot,
    drooping their heads to the earth; and hot tears
    ran from their eyelids to the ground as they mourned

  Immortal horses compared to the stillest human thing there is —
  a grave-marker: the deathless standing as monument to the dead.

23.598-600 · Menelaus forgives Antilochus

    And that man's heart
    was warmed like the dew about the ears of ripening corn,
    when the fields are bristling:

  The one simile in the poem for reconciliation — and it is
  harvest dew: quiet, early, and brief.

22.158-165 · The chase

    a brave man fled in front — but a far better one pursued him —
    fast: since it was no beast of sacrifice, no ox-hide,
    they strove for — the prizes given for men's running:
    they ran for the life of Hector, the tamer of horses.
    As when prize-winning single-hoofed horses wheel round the turn-posts
    at full gallop, and a great prize is set out —
    a tripod, or a woman — in the games for a man who has died:
    so the two of them whirled three times around Priam's city

  The negative simile, Homer's rarest device: he names the normal
  prizes only to strike them out. And inside the comparison, the
  funeral games have already begun — a great prize is set out,
  for a man who has died.

22.199-201 · The chase, unending

    And as in a dream a man cannot catch one who runs —
    the one cannot escape him, nor the other catch —
    so he could not run him down, nor the other get away.

  Two lines of pure nightmare-mechanics, three thousand years
  before anyone else wrote down what dreams do.

16.745-750 · Patroclus mocks the falling Cebriones

    "Well, well — how nimble the man is! What easy tumbling!
    If ever he found himself on the fish-teeming sea,
    this man could feed a whole crew, diving for oysters,
    leaping overboard even in rough weather —
    as easily as he tumbles now from his chariot onto the plain.
    So: there are acrobats among the Trojans too."

  A simile inside a taunt inside a killing: the dying charioteer
  becomes an oyster-diver in the mouth of a man who will himself
  be dead within two hundred lines. The gallows-humor is part of
  the record; the poem does not prettify what battle does to the
  living, either.

24.317-319 · The omen for Priam

    Wide as the door of some rich man's high-roofed
    treasure-chamber stands, a door well fitted with bolts,
    so wide his wings spread out on either side.

  An eagle's wingspan measured by a doorway — the domestic scale
  reclaiming even the sky, in the poem's last hours.

24.480-484 · Priam appears before Achilles

    As when thick blindness takes a man, who in his own country
    kills another, and comes among a different people,
    to a rich man's house, and wonder holds all who see him:
    so Achilles wondered, seeing godlike Priam;
    and the others wondered too, and looked at one another.

  The last great simile of the Iliad, and its most audacious: the
  roles run backward. It is the suppliant king who is compared to
  a killer arriving out of nowhere, and the killer of his sons
  who feels the wonder. In the poem's final reckoning, guilt and
  grief have changed places so completely that only an inverted
  simile can describe the room. Everything the catalog holds —
  the beasts, the storms, the harvests, the mothers — narrows at
  the end to this: two men looking at each other, and wonder.
