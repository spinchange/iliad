BOOK 1

The wrath: the quarrel over Briseis, the plague, and Thetis's bargain
with Zeus.

TRANSLATOR'S COMMENTARY

This translation aims at plain, dignified modern English free verse, kept
line-for-line with the Greek of the Monro-Allen text (books/book_01.txt) so
that any English line can be checked against its original. It follows the
printed text throughout, including passages that ancient critics athetized
(marked as spurious but retained); atheteses are recorded in the notes, not
acted on. Where the printed text itself embodies an editorial choice against
the manuscript majority — most strikingly at 1.350, where it reads "boundless
sea" where nearly all manuscripts have the famous "wine-dark sea" — the note
says so.

The poem's first word, mēnis, is rendered "wrath" rather than "rage" or
"anger": mēnis in Homer is not a mood but a durable, quasi-divine cosmic
grudge, and English "wrath" carries the required archaic, more-than-human
weight. The other load-bearing terms of the quarrel are kept consistent so
their economy stays visible: timē is always "honor," geras always "prize"
(the concrete, publicly awarded object in which honor is embodied — Chryseis,
Briseis), and atē "blindness" (the god-sent ruin-bringing delusion Agamemnon
will later plead). The quarrel of Book 1 is unintelligible unless the reader
can see that Achilles' complaint is not about a woman but about the public
arithmetic of honor; the vocabulary is kept rigid for that reason.

Recurring epithets are translated by fixed English equivalents ("swift-footed
Achilles," "white-armed Hera," "the lord of men Agamemnon"), footnoted at
first occurrence only, and logged in translation/CONVENTIONS.md as binding
for later books. Fixity is deliberate: the epithets are load-bearing oral
formulas, and varying them for elegance would erase exactly the texture that
makes them Homeric. Proper names use familiar Latinized spellings (Achilles,
Ajax, Clytemnestra), also logged there.

On the one Book 1 entry in disputed_lines.md (1.4-5): Zenodotus athetized
both lines, and his text is reported to have read daita, making the corpses a
"feast" for the birds. The translation follows the vulgate ("prey for dogs /
and every bird"), keeping the grim literal image rather than the sardonic
"banquet"; the opening sentence is already as harsh as English can decently
make it, and the "feast" reading, though grotesquely apt, rests on a single
reported reading of doubtful standing. See note [3].

One grammatical crux was settled by the project's morphology files: at 1.291
the treebank parses protheousin as a form of protithēmi ("set forth, grant"),
and the translation follows it ("do they therefore give him leave to hurl
insults?"); the rival parse is recorded in note [25].

   1  Sing, goddess, the wrath[1] of Peleus' son Achilles,
   2  ruinous wrath, that laid griefs beyond counting on the Achaeans
   3  and hurled down to Hades many strong souls[2]
   4  of heroes, and made the men themselves prey for dogs
   5  and every bird — and the will of Zeus moved toward fulfillment —[3]
   6  from the moment those two first stood apart in strife,
   7  Atreus' son, lord of men[4], and brilliant Achilles.
   8  Which of the gods, then, set the two of them fighting?
   9  The son of Leto and Zeus. Angered at the king,
  10  he sent an evil plague up through the army, and the people were perishing,
  11  because Atreus' son had dishonored Chryses, his priest.
  12  For Chryses had come to the Achaeans' swift ships
  13  to free his daughter, bearing ransom past counting,
  14  holding in his hands, on a golden staff, the wreaths[5]
  15  of Apollo who strikes from afar[6], and he begged all the Achaeans,
  16  but the two sons of Atreus most of all, marshals of the people:
  17  "Sons of Atreus, and you other strong-greaved Achaeans[7],
  18  may the gods who keep their homes on Olympus grant you
  19  to sack Priam's city and come home safe;
  20  only set my dear child free, and accept this ransom,
  21  in awe of Zeus's son, Apollo who strikes from afar."
  22  Then all the other Achaeans shouted assent:
  23  respect the priest, take the shining ransom.
  24  But it did not please the heart of Atreus' son Agamemnon;
  25  he sent him off harshly, and laid a hard command on him:
  26  "Old man, let me not find you by the hollow ships,
  27  either lingering now or coming back again later,
  28  or the god's staff and wreath may be no help to you.
  29  The girl I will not release. Sooner shall old age come upon her
  30  in our house, in Argos, far from her fatherland,
  31  walking to and fro at the loom and sharing my bed.[8]
  32  Now go — do not provoke me — so you may get home the safer."
  33  So he spoke, and the old man was afraid and obeyed his word.
  34  He went in silence along the shore of the loud-roaring sea[9],
  35  and then, walking far apart, the old man prayed long
  36  to lord Apollo, whom lovely-haired Leto bore:
  37  "Hear me, god of the silver bow, who stand guard over Chryse
  38  and holy Cilla, who rule Tenedos in strength —
  39  Smintheus![10] If ever I roofed a temple that pleased you,
  40  if ever I burned for you the fat-rich thighs
  41  of bulls and goats, grant me this wish:
  42  may the Danaans pay for my tears by your arrows."
  43  So he spoke in prayer, and Phoebus Apollo heard him.
  44  Down from the peaks of Olympus he came, angry at heart,
  45  with the bow on his shoulders and the covered quiver;
  46  the arrows clashed on the shoulders of the angry god
  47  as he moved, and he came on like nightfall.[11]
  48  Then he settled apart from the ships and let fly a shaft,
  49  and terrible was the clang of the silver bow.
  50  The mules he attacked first, and the quick-running dogs,
  51  but then he loosed his piercing arrows on the men themselves
  52  and struck; and the crowded pyres of the dead burned without ceasing.
  53  Nine days the god's shafts ranged through the army,
  54  and on the tenth Achilles called the people to assembly —
  55  the goddess white-armed Hera[12] had put it in his mind,
  56  for she cared for the Danaans, seeing them die.
  57  So when they were gathered and assembled together,
  58  swift-footed Achilles[13] rose and spoke among them:
  59  "Son of Atreus, now I think we shall be beaten back off our course
  60  and go home again — if we escape death at all —
  61  if war and plague together are to break the Achaeans.
  62  Come, let us ask some seer or priest,
  63  or even a reader of dreams — for a dream too is from Zeus —[14]
  64  who might tell us why Phoebus Apollo is so angry:
  65  whether he faults a vow unpaid or a hecatomb[15],
  66  in hope he may accept the savor of lambs and unblemished goats
  67  and choose to drive this ruin from us."
  68  So he spoke and sat down; and among them rose
  69  Calchas, Thestor's son, far the best of bird-readers,
  70  who knew what is, what will be, and what was before,
  71  and who had guided the Achaeans' ships to Ilion
  72  by the seercraft Phoebus Apollo gave him.
  73  With good will toward them he spoke and addressed them:
  74  "Achilles, dear to Zeus, you command me to explain
  75  the wrath of Apollo, the lord who strikes from afar.
  76  Then I will speak. But you consider, and swear to me
  77  that you will readily defend me with word and hand;
  78  for I think I shall anger a man who rules
  79  all the Argives in his great power, a man the Achaeans obey.
  80  A king is the stronger when he rages at a lesser man;
  81  even if he swallows his anger for that one day,
  82  still he keeps resentment afterward in his chest
  83  until he brings it to completion. Say, then, if you will keep me safe."
  84  Answering him spoke swift-footed Achilles:
  85  "Take heart, and speak whatever god-sign you know.
  86  For by Apollo, dear to Zeus — the god to whom you pray, Calchas,
  87  when you reveal god-signs to the Danaans —
  88  no man, while I live and see light on this earth,
  89  shall lay heavy hands on you beside the hollow ships,
  90  no man of all the Danaans — not if you name Agamemnon,
  91  who now claims to be far the best of the Achaeans."
  92  Then the blameless seer took heart and spoke:
  93  "He faults no vow unpaid, no hecatomb,
  94  but for the sake of his priest, whom Agamemnon dishonored —
  95  he did not release his daughter, would not accept the ransom —
  96  for that the far-striker has given griefs, and will give more.
  97  He will not lift this shameful ruin from the Danaans[16]
  98  until we give back to her dear father the bright-eyed girl,
  99  unbought, unransomed, and lead a holy hecatomb
 100  to Chryse. Then we might appease him and win him over."
 101  So he spoke and sat down; and among them rose
 102  the hero, Atreus' son, wide-ruling Agamemnon,
 103  in anguish: his darkened heart was brimming full
 104  of fury, and his eyes were like blazing fire.
 105  Glaring evil first at Calchas, he spoke:
 106  "Seer of evils! Never yet have you spoken a word to my good;
 107  always it is dear to your heart to prophesy evils,
 108  and no good word have you ever spoken or brought to pass.
 109  And now you stand prophesying before the Danaans
 110  that this is why the far-striker makes their griefs —
 111  because I would not take the shining ransom
 112  for the girl Chryseis, since I greatly wish
 113  to keep her at home. Indeed I prefer her to Clytemnestra,
 114  my wedded wife, since she is not her inferior
 115  in build or bearing, in mind or in any work of her hands.
 116  Even so I am willing to give her back, if that is better;
 117  I want the people safe, not perishing.
 118  But make ready a prize[17] for me at once, so that I alone
 119  of the Argives not go unprized — that would not be fitting;
 120  for you all see it: my prize goes elsewhere."
 121  Then swift-footed brilliant Achilles answered him:
 122  "Most glorious son of Atreus, greediest of all men,
 123  how shall the great-hearted Achaeans give you a prize?
 124  We know of no common store laid up anywhere;
 125  what we took when we sacked the cities has been shared out,
 126  and it is wrong to make the people gather it back again.
 127  No — give the girl up now, to the god; and we Achaeans
 128  will pay you back three and four times over, if ever Zeus
 129  grants us to sack the well-walled city of Troy."
 130  Answering him spoke the lord Agamemnon:
 131  "Do not, brave as you are, godlike Achilles,
 132  try to cheat me with cleverness; you will not slip past me or persuade me.
 133  Is this your wish — that you keep your own prize, while I
 134  sit here without one, just because you tell me to give her back?
 135  If the great-hearted Achaeans will give me a prize,
 136  suiting it to my heart, so it be worth as much — well and good;
 137  but if they will not give it, then I myself will come and take
 138  your prize, or Ajax's, or Odysseus's —
 139  seize it and carry it off. And the man I visit will be angry.
 140  But all that we can think through later.
 141  For now, come, let us drag a black ship down to the bright sea,
 142  gather rowers enough, set a hecatomb aboard,
 143  and put fair-cheeked[18] Chryseis herself
 144  on deck. And one man of counsel shall be commander —
 145  Ajax, or Idomeneus, or brilliant Odysseus,
 146  or you, son of Peleus, most terrifying of all men,
 147  to make sacrifice and appease for us the worker-from-afar."
 148  Glaring at him darkly, swift-footed Achilles spoke:
 149  "You — wrapped in shamelessness, mind set on profit!
 150  How shall any Achaean gladly obey your orders,
 151  to go on a mission or to fight men in strength?
 152  It was not for the sake of Trojan spearmen that I came
 153  to fight here: they have done nothing to me.
 154  They never drove off my cattle or my horses,
 155  never in deep-soiled Phthia, feeder of men,
 156  did they ruin my harvest — since much lies between us,
 157  shadowing mountains and the echoing sea.
 158  No: you, colossal in shamelessness, you we followed,
 159  to please you — winning honor for Menelaus and for you, dog-face[19],
 160  from the Trojans. None of that do you look at or care for.
 161  And now you threaten to take my prize from me yourself,
 162  the prize I labored hard for, which the sons of the Achaeans gave me.
 163  I never have a prize equal to yours, whenever the Achaeans
 164  sack some well-settled Trojan town;
 165  my hands carry the greater part of furious war,
 166  yet when the division comes,
 167  your prize is far the larger, and I go back to my ships
 168  with something small and dear, when I have worn myself out fighting.
 169  Now I am going to Phthia. It is far better
 170  to go home with my beaked ships. I do not intend,
 171  dishonored here, to go on piling up wealth and riches for you."
 172  Then answered him the lord of men Agamemnon:
 173  "Run, then, if your heart is set on it. I am not begging you
 174  to stay for my sake. Beside me are others
 175  who will honor me — above all Zeus the counselor.
 176  Of all the kings whom Zeus sustains, you are the most hateful to me:
 177  strife is always dear to you, and wars, and battles.
 178  If you are very strong, some god gave you that.
 179  Go home with your ships and your companions,
 180  be king over your Myrmidons. I take no account of you;
 181  your rage does not move me. But here is my threat:
 182  since Phoebus Apollo takes Chryseis from me,
 183  her I will send off with my ship and my companions —
 184  but I will come myself to your hut and take fair-cheeked
 185  Briseis, your own prize, so you may learn well
 186  how much greater I am than you, and the next man too may shrink
 187  from calling himself my equal and matching himself against me."
 188  So he spoke. And grief came over Peleus' son; the heart
 189  in his shaggy chest was split two ways:
 190  whether to draw the sharp sword from beside his thigh,
 191  scatter the men between, and kill the son of Atreus,
 192  or to check his anger and rein in his spirit.
 193  While he was turning this over in mind and heart
 194  and drawing the great sword from its sheath, Athena came
 195  from the sky. White-armed Hera had sent her,
 196  loving both men alike in her heart, and caring for both.
 197  She stood behind him and caught Peleus' son by his tawny hair,
 198  showing herself to him alone; of the others, no one saw her.
 199  Achilles was startled. He turned, and at once he knew
 200  Pallas Athena — the eyes shining terribly —
 201  and speaking winged words[20] he addressed her:
 202  "Why have you come this time, child of aegis-bearing Zeus?[21]
 203  To see the outrage of Agamemnon, Atreus' son?
 204  I will tell you this, and I think it will be accomplished:
 205  by his own arrogance he may soon lose his life."
 206  Then the goddess grey-eyed Athena[22] answered him:
 207  "I came from the sky to stop your fury — if you will listen —
 208  and white-armed Hera sent me,
 209  loving you both alike in her heart, and caring for both.
 210  Come, leave off this strife; do not draw the sword.
 211  With words, though — taunt him with what is coming, and it will come.
 212  I tell you this outright, and it will be accomplished:
 213  one day gifts three times as splendid will be laid before you
 214  because of this outrage. Hold back, and obey us."
 215  Answering her spoke swift-footed Achilles:
 216  "Goddess, a man must keep the word of you two,
 217  however angry his heart. That way is better.
 218  Whoever obeys the gods, him they hear indeed."
 219  He spoke, and checked his heavy hand on the silver hilt,
 220  and back into the sheath he drove the great sword; he did not disobey
 221  the word of Athena. And she was gone to Olympus,
 222  to the house of aegis-bearing Zeus, among the other gods.
 223  But Peleus' son once more with cutting words
 224  went at Atreus' son, and did not yet let go his anger:
 225  "Wine-sack! Dog's eyes and a deer's heart![23]
 226  Never once to arm yourself for battle with the people,
 227  never to go out in ambush with the best of the Achaeans —
 228  you have not the courage; that looks like death to you.
 229  Far better, no doubt, through the wide Achaean camp
 230  to strip his gifts from any man who speaks against you —
 231  a king who devours his own people! Because you rule nobodies;
 232  otherwise, son of Atreus, this outrage would be your last.
 233  But I will say this to you, and swear a great oath on it:
 234  by this staff — which will never again put out leaves and branches
 235  once it has left its stump in the mountains,
 236  nor bloom again, since the bronze stripped it
 237  of leaf and bark; and now the sons of the Achaeans
 238  carry it in their palms when they give judgment, those who guard
 239  the ordinances that come from Zeus — this will be a great oath:
 240  some day a longing for Achilles will come upon the sons of the Achaeans,
 241  all of them together. And then, grieved as you are, you will have no power
 242  to help, when many at the hands of man-slaughtering Hector
 243  fall dying. And you will tear the heart inside you,
 244  raging that you paid no honor to the best of the Achaeans."
 245  So spoke Peleus' son, and he dashed to the ground the staff
 246  studded with golden nails, and sat down himself,
 247  while Atreus' son raged on the other side. Then Nestor
 248  sprang up, the sweet-speaking, the clear-voiced speaker of Pylos,
 249  from whose tongue the voice ran sweeter than honey.
 250  In his time two generations of mortal men
 251  had already passed away, who once were born and raised with him
 252  in sacred Pylos, and he was king among the third.
 253  With good will toward them he spoke and addressed them:
 254  "Shame! Great grief comes upon the Achaean land.
 255  Priam would rejoice indeed, and Priam's sons,
 256  and the rest of the Trojans would be glad at heart,
 257  if they learned all this about the two of you quarreling —
 258  you who surpass the Danaans in counsel, surpass them in fighting.
 259  But listen to me: you are both younger than I am.
 260  Once, long ago, I kept company with better men
 261  even than you, and they never made light of me.
 262  Such men I have not seen since, nor shall I see —
 263  Peirithous, and Dryas shepherd of the people,
 264  Caeneus, Exadius, godlike Polyphemus,
 265  and Theseus, Aegeus' son, image of the immortals.[24]
 266  Strongest they grew of all men reared on earth;
 267  strongest they were, and with the strongest they fought,
 268  the beasts that lair in the mountains, and destroyed them terribly.
 269  With these men I kept company, come from Pylos,
 270  far away, from a distant land; for they themselves called me.
 271  And I fought as my own man, by myself. Against them no one
 272  of mortals now upon the earth could fight.
 273  And they listened to my counsels and obeyed my word.
 274  So you two listen also, since listening is better.
 275  You — do not, great man though you are, take the girl from him;
 276  let her be, as the sons of the Achaeans first gave her to him, a prize.
 277  And you, son of Peleus — do not choose to strive with a king,
 278  force against force. A scepter-holding king, to whom Zeus has given
 279  glory, has no common share of honor.
 280  If you are the stronger, and a goddess mother bore you,
 281  still he is the greater, because he rules over more.
 282  Son of Atreus, check your fury. It is I who beg you:
 283  let go your anger against Achilles, who stands, for all the Achaeans,
 284  a great wall against the evil of war."
 285  Answering him spoke the lord Agamemnon:
 286  "Yes, old man — all this you have said rightly and in due measure.
 287  But this man wants to be above all others:
 288  to hold power over all, to be king over all,
 289  to give orders to all — and there is one, I think, who will not obey.
 290  If the gods who live forever made him a spearman,
 291  do they therefore give him leave to hurl insults?"[25]
 292  Breaking in on him, brilliant Achilles answered:
 293  "Yes — for I would be called coward and nobody
 294  if I give way to you in every matter, whatever you say.
 295  Lay these commands on others; do not give orders
 296  to me. I do not think I shall obey you any longer.
 297  And I will tell you another thing — put it away in your heart:
 298  with my hands I will not fight over the girl,
 299  not with you, not with any man; you gave her, and you have taken her away.
 300  But of all else that is mine by my swift black ship,
 301  you shall carry off nothing, taking it against my will.
 302  Go on — try it, so these men too may know:
 303  in a moment your dark blood will spurt around my spear."
 304  So the two of them, fighting it out with head-on words,
 305  rose, and dissolved the assembly by the Achaean ships.
 306  Peleus' son went off to his huts and balanced ships
 307  with Menoetius' son[26] and his companions.
 308  And Atreus' son had a swift ship dragged to the sea,
 309  chose twenty rowers for her, drove aboard a hecatomb
 310  for the god, and brought fair-cheeked Chryseis
 311  and set her aboard; and resourceful Odysseus[27] went as commander.
 312  So they embarked and sailed the watery ways.
 313  And Atreus' son commanded the people to purify themselves:
 314  they washed themselves clean, and cast the washings into the sea,
 315  and offered to Apollo perfect hecatombs
 316  of bulls and goats along the shore of the barren sea[28],
 317  and the savor rose to the sky, curling in the smoke.
 318  So the camp busied itself with this. But Agamemnon
 319  did not let go the quarrel with which he had first threatened Achilles:
 320  he spoke to Talthybius and Eurybates,
 321  his two heralds and ready henchmen:
 322  "Go to the hut of Peleus' son Achilles.
 323  Take fair-cheeked Briseis by the hand and bring her.
 324  And if he will not give her up, I will come with more men
 325  and take her myself — which will be the worse for him."
 326  So he sent them off, and laid a hard command on them.
 327  Unwilling, the two went along the shore of the barren sea
 328  and came to the huts and ships of the Myrmidons.
 329  They found him beside his hut and his black ship,
 330  sitting; and Achilles took no joy in seeing them.
 331  The two, in fear and awe of the king,
 332  stood, and did not speak to him or question him.
 333  But he understood in his heart, and spoke:
 334  "Welcome, heralds, messengers of Zeus and of men.
 335  Come near. You are not to blame in my eyes, but Agamemnon,
 336  who sends you two for the girl Briseis.
 337  Come, Patroclus, sprung from Zeus — bring out the girl
 338  and give her to them to take. And let these two themselves be witnesses
 339  before the blessed gods and mortal men
 340  and before that hard king, if ever again
 341  there is need of me to beat back shameful ruin
 342  from the rest. Truly, he rages in a ruinous mind;
 343  he has no thought of seeing before and behind at once,
 344  how his Achaeans might fight safe beside the ships."
 345  So he spoke, and Patroclus obeyed his dear companion:
 346  he led fair-cheeked Briseis out of the hut
 347  and gave her to be taken. And the two went back along the Achaean ships,
 348  and the woman went with them, unwilling. Then Achilles,
 349  weeping, drew apart from his companions and sat down
 350  on the shore of the grey sea, looking out over the boundless deep[29],
 351  and stretched out his hands and prayed hard to his dear mother:
 352  "Mother, since you bore me to a short life,
 353  honor at least the Olympian owed me,
 354  Zeus who thunders on high. Now he has honored me not even a little.
 355  The son of Atreus, wide-ruling Agamemnon,
 356  has dishonored me: he has taken and keeps my prize; he seized it himself."
 357  So he spoke, shedding tears, and his lady mother heard him,
 358  sitting in the depths of the sea beside her old father.
 359  Quickly she rose from the grey salt water like mist,
 360  and sat down before him as he wept,
 361  stroked him with her hand, and spoke a word, and named him:
 362  "Child, why do you weep? What grief has come to your heart?
 363  Speak out; hide nothing; let us both know."
 364  Groaning heavily, swift-footed Achilles said:
 365  "You know. Why tell it all to one who knows?
 366  We went to Thebe, Eëtion's holy city[30],
 367  and sacked it, and brought everything here;
 368  the sons of the Achaeans divided it fairly among themselves,
 369  and for Atreus' son they picked out fair-cheeked Chryseis.
 370  Then Chryses, priest of Apollo who strikes from afar,
 371  came to the swift ships of the bronze-shirted Achaeans
 372  to free his daughter, bearing ransom past counting,[31]
 373  holding in his hands, on a golden staff, the wreaths
 374  of Apollo who strikes from afar, and he begged all the Achaeans,
 375  but the two sons of Atreus most of all, marshals of the people.
 376  Then all the other Achaeans shouted assent:
 377  respect the priest, take the shining ransom.
 378  But it did not please the heart of Atreus' son Agamemnon;
 379  he sent him off harshly, and laid a hard command on him.
 380  And the old man went back angry; and Apollo
 381  heard his prayer, since he was very dear to him,
 382  and loosed an evil shaft on the Argives. And now the people
 383  were dying thick and fast, and the god's arrows ranged
 384  everywhere through the wide Achaean camp. But a seer,
 385  knowing well, declared to us the god-word of the far-striker.
 386  At once I, first, urged appeasing the god;
 387  but rage took hold of Atreus' son, and standing up
 388  he made a threat — which now stands accomplished.
 389  The girl the bright-eyed Achaeans are sending on a swift ship
 390  to Chryse, with gifts for the lord;
 391  and the other — heralds have just now gone from my hut leading her,
 392  the daughter of Briseus, whom the sons of the Achaeans gave me.
 393  But you — if you have the power, defend your son.
 394  Go to Olympus and entreat Zeus, if ever
 395  you gladdened the heart of Zeus by word or deed.
 396  For often in my father's halls I heard you[32]
 397  declare it proudly: that you alone among the immortals
 398  warded off shameful ruin from the son of Cronos, lord of the dark cloud,
 399  that time the other Olympians wanted to bind him —
 400  Hera and Poseidon and Pallas Athena.
 401  But you came, goddess, and set him free of his bonds,
 402  quickly calling to high Olympus the hundred-handed one
 403  whom the gods call Briareus, but all men
 404  Aegaeon[33] — for he in turn is stronger than his father —
 405  and he sat down beside the son of Cronos, glorying in his splendor,
 406  and the blessed gods shrank in fear of him, and bound Zeus no more.
 407  Sit beside him and remind him of this; take his knees;
 408  see if he may be willing to help the Trojans
 409  and pen the Achaeans back among the ships' sterns and the sea,
 410  dying — so they may all have joy of their king,
 411  and even Atreus' son, wide-ruling Agamemnon, may know
 412  his blindness, that he paid no honor to the best of the Achaeans."
 413  Then Thetis answered him, letting fall her tears:
 414  "Ah my child, why did I raise you, born to sorrow?
 415  If only you could sit by the ships without tears, without pain,
 416  since your portion is short — not long at all.
 417  Now you are both quick-fated and wretched beyond all men;
 418  so it was for an evil fate I bore you in our halls.
 419  To tell your word to Zeus who delights in thunder
 420  I myself will go to snow-capped Olympus; he may listen.
 421  And you — sit now by your swift-faring ships,
 422  keep your wrath against the Achaeans, and let war go entirely.
 423  For Zeus went yesterday to Ocean, to the blameless Ethiopians[34],
 424  for a feast, and all the gods went with him.
 425  On the twelfth day he will come back to Olympus,
 426  and then I will go to Zeus's house with its bronze floor,
 427  and clasp his knees, and I think I will persuade him."
 428  So she spoke and went away, and left him there,
 429  the heart in him angry for the fair-belted woman
 430  they had taken from him by force, against his will. Meanwhile Odysseus
 431  came to Chryse leading the holy hecatomb.
 432  When they had come inside the deep harbor,
 433  they furled the sails and stowed them in the black ship,
 434  dropped the mast by the forestays into its crutch,
 435  smartly, and rowed her to her mooring with the oars.
 436  They threw out the anchor-stones and made fast the stern lines,
 437  and stepped out themselves onto the surf-line of the sea,
 438  and out came the hecatomb for Apollo who strikes from afar,
 439  and out of the seagoing ship stepped Chryseis.
 440  Then resourceful Odysseus led her to the altar
 441  and put her in her dear father's arms, and said:
 442  "Chryses, the lord of men Agamemnon sent me
 443  to bring you your child, and to offer a holy hecatomb
 444  to Phoebus for the Danaans, that we may appease the lord
 445  who has now sent the Argives griefs and groaning."
 446  So he spoke and put her in his arms, and the old man received
 447  his dear child, rejoicing. Then quickly they set up
 448  the holy hecatomb for the god in order around the well-built altar,
 449  washed their hands, and took up the barley-grains.
 450  And Chryses lifted his hands and prayed aloud for them:
 451  "Hear me, god of the silver bow, who stand guard over Chryse
 452  and holy Cilla, who rule Tenedos in strength.
 453  Once before you heard me when I prayed:
 454  you honored me, and struck the Achaean army hard.
 455  So now again fulfill this wish of mine:
 456  now beat the shameful ruin back from the Danaans."
 457  So he spoke in prayer, and Phoebus Apollo heard him.
 458  And when they had prayed and thrown the barley-grains,[35]
 459  they first drew back the victims' heads, cut their throats, and flayed them,
 460  carved out the thigh-pieces and wrapped them in fat,
 461  making a double fold, and laid raw flesh upon them.
 462  The old man burned them on split wood, and over them poured
 463  gleaming wine; beside him young men held five-pronged forks.
 464  When the thighs were burned and they had tasted the inner parts,
 465  they cut up the rest and threaded it on spits,
 466  roasted it with care, and drew it all off.
 467  Then when they had finished the work and made the meal ready,
 468  they feasted, and no heart lacked a fair share of the feast.
 469  And when they had put away desire for eating and drinking,
 470  the young men filled the mixing-bowls brim-high with drink
 471  and served all, pouring first drops in each cup for the god.
 472  So all day long they sought the god's grace with song,
 473  the young men of the Achaeans singing the beautiful paean,
 474  hymning the worker-from-afar; and he listened, and his heart was glad.
 475  And when the sun went down and darkness came on,
 476  they lay down to sleep by the ship's stern-cables;
 477  and when early-born rosy-fingered Dawn[36] appeared,
 478  then they put to sea for the wide Achaean camp,
 479  and Apollo the worker-from-afar sent them a following wind.
 480  They stepped the mast and spread the white sails,
 481  and the wind filled the belly of the sail, and around the cutwater
 482  the surging wave sang loud as the ship went on,
 483  and she ran down the waves, making her way.
 484  And when they came to the wide Achaean camp,
 485  they hauled the black ship up on the mainland,
 486  high on the sand, and set the long props under her,
 487  and scattered among the huts and ships.
 488  But he raged on, sitting by his swift-faring ships,[37]
 489  the Zeus-sprung son of Peleus, swift-footed Achilles.
 490  He never went now to the assembly where men win glory,
 491  never to war, but wasted his own heart away
 492  staying there, longing for the war-cry and for battle.
 493  But when the twelfth dawn from that day came,
 494  then the gods who live forever went to Olympus
 495  all together, Zeus leading. And Thetis did not forget the charge
 496  of her son: she rose from the swell of the sea
 497  and in the early mist went up to the great sky and Olympus.
 498  She found the wide-seeing son of Cronos sitting apart from the others
 499  on the topmost peak of many-ridged Olympus.
 500  She sat down before him, and took his knees[38]
 501  with her left hand, and with her right taking him under the chin,
 502  she spoke in supplication to lord Zeus, son of Cronos:
 503  "Father Zeus, if ever among the immortals I helped you
 504  by word or deed, grant me this wish:
 505  honor my son, who is doomed to the swiftest death of all —
 506  and now the lord of men Agamemnon
 507  has dishonored him: he has taken and keeps his prize; he seized it himself.
 508  But honor him, Olympian Zeus of the counsels:
 509  give power to the Trojans until the Achaeans
 510  pay my son his due and magnify him with honor."
 511  So she spoke. And Zeus the cloud-gatherer[39] said nothing;
 512  he sat silent a long time. And Thetis, as she had clasped his knees,
 513  so she held on, clinging close, and asked a second time:
 514  "Promise me truly now, and bow your head,
 515  or refuse — you have nothing to fear — so I may know well
 516  how far I am the most dishonored goddess of all."
 517  Greatly troubled, Zeus the cloud-gatherer answered her:
 518  "A sorry business — you will set me at feud
 519  with Hera, when she provokes me with her taunting words.
 520  Even as it is she is forever at me among the immortal gods,
 521  saying I help the Trojans in battle.
 522  Go now, go back, before Hera sees anything;
 523  these things will be my care, until I accomplish them.
 524  Look — I will bow my head to you, so you may believe:
 525  that, from me, is the greatest pledge among the immortals;
 526  for nothing of mine can be taken back, nothing proves false,
 527  nothing goes unfulfilled, once I bow my head to it."
 528  The son of Cronos spoke, and nodded with his dark brows,
 529  and the ambrosial hair rolled forward from the lord's
 530  immortal head; and he shook great Olympus.
 531  So the two took counsel and parted. She
 532  leapt into the deep sea from shining Olympus,
 533  and Zeus went to his own house. And the gods all rose
 534  from their seats before their father; not one dared
 535  stay seated as he came, but all stood up to meet him.
 536  So he sat down there on his throne. But Hera
 537  had seen, and was not fooled: silver-footed Thetis[40],
 538  daughter of the old man of the sea, had counseled with him.
 539  At once she spoke to Zeus, son of Cronos, with cutting words:
 540  "Which of the gods this time, schemer, has been counseling with you?
 541  It is always dear to you to be apart from me,
 542  deciding things in secret. Never yet have you brought yourself
 543  to tell me openly a word of what you intend."
 544  Then the father of men and gods answered her:
 545  "Hera, do not hope to know all my words:
 546  they would be hard for you, wife though you are.
 547  Whatever is fitting to hear, no one, god or man,
 548  shall know before you;
 549  but what I choose to think apart from the gods,
 550  do not pry into all that, or question me."
 551  Then the ox-eyed lady Hera[41] answered him:
 552  "Most dread son of Cronos, what a word you have spoken!
 553  Before now I have hardly asked or pried;
 554  quite at your ease you decide whatever you wish.
 555  But now I am terribly afraid at heart that she has won you over —
 556  silver-footed Thetis, daughter of the old man of the sea.
 557  In the early mist she sat beside you and took your knees,
 558  and I think you bowed your head to her, a firm pledge to honor
 559  Achilles, and to destroy many beside the Achaean ships."
 560  Answering her spoke Zeus the cloud-gatherer:
 561  "Strange one — always suspecting; I can never escape you.
 562  Yet you will have no power to do anything about it, only sit
 563  further from my heart — and that will be the colder for you.
 564  If this is as you say, then it must be my pleasure.
 565  Sit down and be silent, and obey my word,
 566  or all the gods on Olympus may be no help to you
 567  when I come nearer and lay my irresistible hands on you."
 568  So he spoke, and the ox-eyed lady Hera was afraid,
 569  and sat down in silence, bending her own heart.
 570  And through the house of Zeus the heavenly gods were troubled.
 571  Then Hephaestus, famed for craft, began to speak among them,
 572  bringing comfort to his dear mother, white-armed Hera:
 573  "A sorry business this will be, not to be borne,
 574  if you two quarrel this way over mortals
 575  and drive brawling through the gods. There will be no pleasure
 576  in the good feast, when the worse side wins.
 577  I advise my mother — though she understands it herself —
 578  to bring comfort to our dear father Zeus, so that the father
 579  not scold again and throw our feast into confusion.
 580  For if the Olympian, lord of lightning, chooses
 581  to blast us from our seats — well, he is far the strongest.
 582  No — touch him with soft words,
 583  and at once the Olympian will be gracious to us."
 584  So he spoke, and springing up he set the two-handled cup
 585  in his dear mother's hand, and said to her:
 586  "Bear up, my mother, and endure it, grieved though you are,
 587  or I may see you beaten before my eyes,
 588  dear as you are — and then, for all my grief, I will have no power
 589  to help. The Olympian is a hard one to stand against.
 590  Once before, when I was straining to defend you,[42]
 591  he caught me by the foot and flung me from the threshold of heaven:
 592  all day I fell, and with the setting sun
 593  I dropped on Lemnos, and there was little breath left in me;
 594  there the Sintian men took me up, quickly, where I had fallen."
 595  So he spoke, and the goddess white-armed Hera smiled,
 596  and smiling took the cup from her son's hand.
 597  Then, moving to the right, he poured for all the other gods,
 598  drawing sweet nectar from the mixing bowl,
 599  and unquenchable laughter rose among the blessed gods
 600  as they watched Hephaestus bustling through the halls.
 601  So all day long until the sun went down
 602  they feasted, and no heart lacked a fair share of the feast,
 603  nor of the beautiful lyre that Apollo held,
 604  nor of the Muses, who sang in answer with lovely voice.
 605  But when the bright light of the sun had set,
 606  they went each to his own house to lie down,
 607  where for each of them the famed crook-footed god,
 608  Hephaestus, had built a house with cunning skill.
 609  And Zeus the Olympian, lord of lightning, went to his own bed,
 610  where he always lay when sweet sleep came upon him;
 611  there he climbed and slept, with Hera of the golden throne beside him.

Notes

[1] (1.1) "wrath" (μῆνιν) — the poem's first word and announced theme.
    Rendered "wrath" rather than "rage" or "anger": mēnis is a sustained,
    quasi-divine grudge (elsewhere used almost solely of gods), not a flash
    of temper, and "wrath" carries that weight. See the commentary above.

[2] (1.3) "souls" (ψυχάς) — Apollonius Rhodius is reported to have read
    κεφαλάς, "heads" (apparatus/book_01.txt, Line 3). The vulgate "souls"
    is kept; "hurled heads to Hades" would make the bodies-for-dogs of the
    next lines redundant.

[3] (1.4-5) Zenodotus athetized both lines (apparatus, Line 4-5: ath. Zen);
    separately his text is reported (via Athenaeus) to have read δαῖτα,
    making the corpses "a feast" for the birds (disputed_lines.md #1).
    Translated from the vulgate (πᾶσι, "every bird"), keeping the grim
    literal image over the sardonic "banquet"; see commentary.

[4] (1.7) "lord of men" (ἄναξ ἀνδρῶν) — Agamemnon's fixed title epithet,
    always so rendered. Likewise δῖος ("heavenly, radiant") is always
    "brilliant" — of Achilles and Odysseus here, of many heroes later.
    Epithet conventions are noted at first occurrence only; the full list
    lives in translation/CONVENTIONS.md.

[5] (1.14-15) The wreaths (στέμματα) are the god's woolen fillets, carried
    on the priest's golden staff: portable proof of sacred office. Chryses
    arrives simultaneously as suppliant and as Apollo's representative,
    so Agamemnon's threat in 26-32 is sacrilege, not mere rudeness.

[6] (1.14) "who strikes from afar" (ἑκηβόλος) — first of Apollo's archer
    epithets, a family rendered consistently: ἑκηβόλος/ἑκατηβόλος and
    ἑκατηβελέτης "who strikes from afar," ἕκατος "the far-striker,"
    ἑκάεργος "the worker-from-afar."

[7] (1.17) "strong-greaved Achaeans" (ἐϋκνήμιδες Ἀχαιοί) — stock epithet
    of the army (greaves: bronze shin-guards). Note also that "Achaeans,"
    "Danaans," and "Argives" all name the same Greek host interchangeably,
    as meter requires; the translation keeps whichever the Greek uses.

[8] (1.29-31) Aristarchus athetized these three lines (apparatus, Line
    29-31: ath. Ar.) — ancient critics found Agamemnon's leering detail
    unbecoming. Kept, following the printed text; the coarseness is
    dramatically apt at this first display of his character.

[9] (1.34) "loud-roaring sea" (πολυφλοίσβοιο θαλάσσης) — famous
    onomatopoeic formula for the surf-line, rendered thus wherever it
    recurs.

[10] (1.39) "Smintheus" — obscure cult title of Apollo, in antiquity
     connected with σμίνθος "mouse"; a mouse-god (whether as sender or
     averter of vermin and pestilence) suits the plague-god invoked here.
     Left untranslated, as a cult name.

[11] (1.46-47) Zenodotus athetized these two lines (apparatus, Line 46-47:
     ath. Zen) — the clashing arrows and "he came on like nightfall."
     Kept; the simile is among the most admired in the poem.

[12] (1.55) "white-armed Hera" (λευκώλενος Ἥρη) — her fixed epithet;
     white forearms as a mark of female beauty (elite women stayed
     unsunburned).

[13] (1.58) "swift-footed Achilles" — his defining epithet. The Greek
     alternates πόδας ὠκύς ("swift as to his feet") and ποδάρκης
     ("foot-strong") by metrical convenience; both are rendered
     "swift-footed" rather than inventing a distinction English cannot
     hear.

[14] (1.63) "for a dream too is from Zeus" — athetized by Zenodotus
     (apparatus, Line 63: ath. Zen). Kept; the parenthetical
     justification is characteristic of Achilles' earnest style here.

[15] (1.65) "hecatomb" (ἑκατόμβη) — literally "a hundred oxen," in
     practice any large-scale public sacrifice (the one at 458 ff. is of
     bulls and goats, far fewer than a hundred). Kept as the standard
     English term.

[16] (1.97) For this line Zenodotus' text read instead "until he holds
     back the plague's heavy hands from the Danaans" (apparatus, Line 97).
     The vulgate, printed here, makes Apollo the subject throughout;
     the sense is not materially changed.

[17] (1.118-120) "prize" (γέρας) — the concrete share of spoils publicly
     awarded as the embodiment of a warrior's honor (τιμή). Chryseis and
     Briseis are both gera. The entire quarrel runs on this institution:
     to lose one's geras by force is to be publicly declared worthless,
     which is why Achilles calls the seizure "dishonoring" (ἀτιμάζειν).
     "Prize" is kept rigidly for γέρας throughout the translation.

[18] (1.143) "fair-cheeked" (καλλιπάρῃος) — stock beauty epithet, used in
     this book of both Chryseis and Briseis; always so rendered.

[19] (1.159) "dog-face" (κυνῶπα) — in Homeric invective the dog is the
     type of shamelessness (scavenging, fawning, no sense of decency),
     not of loyalty; hence also "dog's eyes" in Achilles' next speech
     (225). The insults are translated literally to keep their animal
     force.

[20] (1.201) "winged words" (ἔπεα πτερόεντα) — formula for direct speech
     launched at an addressee, probably from the image of a feathered
     arrow flying straight to its mark; always so rendered.

[21] (1.202) "aegis-bearing Zeus" (αἰγίοχος) — the aegis is Zeus's
     terror-inducing goat-skin storm-shield, wielded also by Athena;
     his commonest fixed epithet.

[22] (1.206) "grey-eyed Athena" (γλαυκῶπις Ἀθήνη) — γλαυκῶπις may mean
     "gleaming-eyed" or "grey/silvery-eyed," and was anciently connected
     with γλαύξ "owl," her bird. "Grey-eyed" is adopted as the
     conventional English; the gleam, not the hue, is what the scenes
     exploit (cf. 200, "the eyes shining terribly").

[23] (1.225-233) Zenodotus athetized this entire opening of Achilles'
     tirade through the beginning of the oath (apparatus, Line 225-233:
     ath. Zen) — presumably as unworthy abuse. Kept: the escalation from
     insult to formal oath-on-the-staff is the dramatic hinge of the
     assembly.

[24] (1.265) The Theseus line is absent from most manuscripts and is
     identical to Hesiod, Shield 182; it is almost certainly a later
     insertion here (apparatus, Line 265: om. vulg.). Retained, bracketed
     by no marks, since the printed text keeps it — but it is the weakest
     line of the catalogue. The "beasts that lair in the mountains" (268,
     φῆρες) are the centaurs: Nestor is recalling the Lapith-centaur war.

[25] (1.291) "give him leave to hurl insults" — the verb προθέουσιν is an
     old crux: taken here (with the project treebank) as from προτίθημι,
     "do they set it before him / license him?"; others derive it from
     προθέω, "do his insults run out ahead?" The sarcastic sense —
     prowess is god-given, abuse is not — is the same either way.

[26] (1.307) "Menoetius' son" — Patroclus, here making his first,
     silent appearance in the poem; he is named at 337.

[27] (1.311) "resourceful Odysseus" (πολύμητις) — "of much cunning
     intelligence (mētis)"; his fixed epithet, always so rendered.

[28] (1.316) "barren sea" (ἀτρυγέτοιο) — formulaic sea-epithet of
     uncertain sense, traditionally "unharvested/yielding no crop";
     "barren" is adopted as the convention.

[29] (1.350) "the boundless deep" — the printed text follows Aristarchus'
     reading ἀπείρονα ("boundless"); nearly all manuscripts have οἴνοπα,
     the famous "wine-dark sea" (apparatus, Line 350). Readers expecting
     "wine-dark" here are remembering the manuscript vulgate. Translated
     as printed, with real regret.

[30] (1.366) Thebe — a town of the Troad ruled by Eëtion, father of
     Hector's wife Andromache. Achilles sacked it; Chryseis was captured
     there (visiting, evidently, since her home is Chryse). The raid
     resurfaces in Book 6 as the source of Andromache's griefs.

[31] (1.372-379) These eight lines repeat 12-16 and 22-25 verbatim —
     normal epic technique when a character retells events — and
     Aristarchus athetized the repetition here (apparatus, Line 372-379:
     ath. hic Ar.). Kept, and translated in identical words, since
     verbatim recurrence is a deliberate feature of the style.

[32] (1.396-406) Zenodotus athetized the whole Briareus digression
     (apparatus, Line 396-406: ath. Zen) — the only surviving mention of
     this failed divine coup against Zeus. At 400 his text also named
     "Phoebus Apollo" among the conspirators instead of "Pallas Athena"
     (apparatus, Line 400). Kept as printed: the story is doing real
     work, reminding Zeus (through Thetis) of a concrete debt.

[33] (1.403-404) A rare glimpse of double naming: gods say "Briareus,"
     men say "Aegaeon." The hundred-handed giant is elsewhere a son of
     Uranus; "stronger than his father" here more likely points to a
     tradition making him Poseidon's son. Zenodotus' text expanded the
     line with a verse on his strength (apparatus, Line 404).

[34] (1.423-425) The gods' twelve-day absence feasting with the
     "blameless Ethiopians" at the world-encircling stream of Ocean is a
     standard epic device for clearing the divine stage. "For a feast"
     follows Aristarchus' reading κατὰ δαῖτα (apparatus, Line 424).

[35] (1.458-466) The standard Homeric sacrifice, described with ritual
     precision: barley thrown, head drawn back, throat cut; thigh-bones
     wrapped in a double fold of fat with raw strips laid on top are the
     god's portion, burned with wine poured over; the innards (σπλάγχνα)
     are tasted first by the participants, then the rest is spitted and
     roasted for the shared feast. The gods receive smoke and savor; the
     people, dinner.

[36] (1.477) "early-born rosy-fingered Dawn" (ῥοδοδάκτυλος Ἠώς) — the
     most famous of all Homeric formulas, marking daybreak; always so
     rendered.

[37] (1.488-492) Zenodotus athetized these five lines on Achilles wasting
     his heart in idleness (apparatus, Line 488-492: ath. Zen.). Kept:
     they are the essential bridge from quarrel to consequence, holding
     Achilles motionless while the plot moves to Olympus.

[38] (1.500-501) Thetis performs the full ritual of supplication: crouch
     before the person, clasp the knees with one hand, reach for the chin
     with the other. A completed supplication put the supplicated under
     strong obligation — hence Zeus's trapped silence at 511-512.

[39] (1.511) "Zeus the cloud-gatherer" (νεφεληγερέτα Ζεύς) — his fixed
     weather-god epithet, always so rendered.

[40] (1.538) "silver-footed Thetis" (ἀργυρόπεζα) — her fixed epithet.
     The "old man of the sea" is Nereus, her father, never named in the
     Iliad but always so designated.

[41] (1.551) "ox-eyed lady Hera" (βοῶπις πότνια Ἥρη) — her second fixed
     epithet: large, dark, liquid eyes as an archaic mark of majesty and
     beauty, possibly a fossil of older theriomorphic cult. Rendered
     literally.

[42] (1.590-594) Hephaestus' fall: Zeus once hurled him from Olympus for
     siding with Hera; he fell a whole day, landed on Lemnos, and was
     tended by the Sintians, the island's non-Greek early inhabitants.
     Lemnos was the chief historical center of Hephaestus' cult. (Book 18
     has a different, incompatible fall-story — thrown by Hera at birth;
     the poem tolerates both.)
