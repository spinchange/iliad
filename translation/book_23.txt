BOOK 23

The ghost asks for burial and one last embrace; the pyre takes
horses, dogs, and twelve men; and then — games: the army that has
been killing for nine years spends a day competing for tripods and
mares, quarreling justly, yielding handsomely, and laughing — while
the man who arranged it all watches, having no future of his own.

TRANSLATOR'S COMMENTARY

Book 23 is the Iliad's demonstration that civilization is a thing
people do on purpose, the day after the worst thing in their lives.
Its first movement is all night and fire: the Myrmidons' three
circuits of the body, the funeral feast where "blood ran in
cupfuls," Achilles' refusal to wash until the pyre is built (the
fast of Book 19 still governing his body), and the visit of the
ghost — Patroclus "in everything like himself, in stature and the
beautiful eyes and the voice," standing above the sleeper's head
with the poem's most quietly devastating reproach: "You sleep —
and you have forgotten me, Achilles" (note [4]). What the ghost
asks for is administration: burial, quickly, so the other souls
will let him across the river; and one thing more — "give me your
hand: I am weeping" — which the living man cannot deliver: the
embrace closes on nothing, and the soul goes down "like smoke...
squeaking," leaving Achilles with the poem's plainest statement of
its afterlife: "a soul and an image — but the wits are not in it
at all" (note [5]). Homer's dead are real enough to make requests
and too thin to hold.

The pyre-sequence keeps every terrible ledger the poem has opened:
the hair vowed to the river Spercheius given to the dead instead
(the vow of homecoming formally cancelled, note [7]); and the
twelve Trojan youths of the vow of 18.336-337, killed with the
narrator's own verdict attached — "and evil was the work his heart
intended" — while, two lines later, the gods quietly begin Book
24: Aphrodite keeping the dogs from Hector with rose-oil,
Apollo roofing the corpse with cloud (note [8]). The winds must be
fetched by Iris from a dinner-party to make the pyre burn; and
over the all-night fire the poem sets its most terrible simile of
grief: Achilles mourning "as a father mourns, burning the bones of
his son, a bridegroom" (note [9]).

Then the games — two-thirds of the book, and the poem's whole
social philosophy in action. Every crisis of the Iliad recurs here
in miniature, and every one is resolved: a dispute over a prize
(Antilochus's foul and Menelaus's oath-challenge) ends not in
wrath but in yielding, a young man handing over the mare and an
elder, melted "as dew softens the ears of ripening corn," handing
it back (note [21]); a spectators' quarrel (Idomeneus and Oilean
Ajax) is stopped by — of all men — Achilles, the poem's original
quarreler, now its arbiter (note [17]); and the book ends with
Agamemnon standing up for the javelin-throw and Achilles awarding
him the prize uncontested — the two men whose fight over a prize
began the epic closing it with a prize freely given (note [29]).
Around these, the poem's late tenderness is everywhere: Achilles'
only smile (at Antilochus, note [20]); Epeius's disarming
self-knowledge ("no man can be best in everything"); Nestor given
a prize for nothing but memory; the biography of a silver bowl
that once ransomed Lycaon and is now raced for at the funeral of
the man whose death doomed him (note [25]); Ajax face-down in
cattle-dung while the army "laughed at him sweetly." The dead man
gets his games; the living get their order back; and the host of
the funeral arranges prizes for a future — his own burial mound,
his own bones in the same urn — that he has already scheduled
(note [10]). It is the gentlest book of the poem, and it earns
its gentleness line by line over an open grave.

 379  they seemed forever on the point of mounting his chariot,
 380  and their breath was hot on the back and broad shoulders
 381  of Eumelus: their heads were laid right on him as they flew.
 382  And now he would have passed him, or made it a dead heat,
 383  had not Phoebus Apollo been angry with Tydeus's son
 384  and dashed the shining whip out of his hands.
 385  Then the tears of rage sprang from his eyes: he saw
 386  the mares going better still — better and better —
 387  while his own pair faltered, running without the goad.
 388  But Apollo's cheating of the son of Tydeus did not
 389  slip past Athena: she swept after the shepherd of the people,
 390  gave him back his whip, and put fury in his horses;
 391  and after Admetus's son she went in her anger
 392  and shattered — a goddess — his horses' yoke; and his mares
 393  ran out to either side of the road, and the pole slewed to the ground,
 394  and he himself was tumbled out of the car beside the wheel,
 395  and the skin was ripped from his elbows, his mouth, his nostrils,
 396  and his forehead bruised above the brows; and his eyes
 397  filled up with tears, and the strong voice stuck in his throat.
 398  And the son of Tydeus swung his single-hoofed horses past him,
 399  leaping far out in front of the rest: for Athena
 400  had put fury in his horses, and set the glory on him.
 401  And after him came Atreus's son, fair-haired Menelaus.
 402  But Antilochus called to his father's horses:[16]
 403  "Get in there, you two as well! Stretch out — as fast as you can!
 404  I am not telling you to race with those in front —
 405  the horses of war-minded Tydeus's son, to whom Athena
 406  has just now given speed, and set the glory on their driver.
 407  But catch the horses of Atreus's son — don't be left behind them! —
 408  quickly — or Aethe will pour shame over the two of you:
 409  a female! Why are you falling behind, my braves?
 410  For I will tell you this, and it will be accomplished:
 411  there will be no more care for you two at the hands of Nestor,
 412  shepherd of the people — he will cut your throats on the spot
 413  with the sharp bronze, if by your slacking we carry off the lesser prize.
 414  After them! Press hard — fast as you can!
 415  And I myself will devise it and manage it —
 416  a way to slip past in the narrow of the road. It will not escape me."
 417  So he spoke; and in dread at their master's rebuke
 418  they ran the harder for a little while; and quickly then
 419  Antilochus, staunch in battle, saw the narrows of the sunken road.
 420  There was a break in the ground, where winter-gathered water
 421  had torn away the track and hollowed the whole place deep;
 422  there Menelaus held his line, shy of fouling wheels;
 423  but Antilochus swerved his single-hoofed horses and drove
 424  outside the track, and pressed him hard, bearing in a little.
 425  And the son of Atreus was afraid, and shouted to Antilochus:
 426  "Antilochus — this is mad driving! Hold in your horses!
 427  The road is narrow here — it widens soon for passing —
 428  you'll wreck the both of us, fouling my car!"
 429  So he spoke; but Antilochus drove all the harder,
 430  laying on the goad, like a man who had not heard.
 431  As far as the swing of a discus flung from the shoulder,
 432  hurled by a young man testing the strength of his youth,
 433  so far they ran on together; and then the mares of Atreus's son
 434  dropped back: he slackened his driving by his own will,
 435  for fear the single-hoofed horses would collide in the road
 436  and overturn the well-plaited cars, and the men themselves
 437  go down in the dust for all their straining after victory.
 438  And fair-haired Menelaus called after him in rebuke:
 439  "Antilochus — no mortal man alive is more ruinous than you!
 440  Go, and be damned — we Achaeans were wrong to call you sane.
 441  But even so, you shall not carry off the prize without an oath."
 442  So he spoke, and called out to his horses:
 443  "Don't hang back on me now, don't stand there grieving!
 444  Their feet and knees will tire before yours do:
 445  the pair of them are past their youth."
 446  So he spoke; and in dread at their master's rebuke
 447  they ran the harder, and quickly closed the gap.
 448  Meanwhile the Argives, sitting in the assembly, watched[17]
 449  for the horses; and these came flying through the dust of the plain.
 450  And the first to make them out was Idomeneus, leader of the Cretans:
 451  for he sat outside the gathering, highest up, on a lookout;
 452  and hearing the voice of a driver shouting, far off as he was,
 453  he knew it; and he made out a horse, clear in the lead,
 454  chestnut over all the rest of him, except that on his forehead
 455  a white blaze was set, round as the full moon.
 456  And he stood upright, and spoke his word among the Argives:
 457  "Friends — leaders and counselors of the Argives —
 458  am I the only one who sees the horses, or do you see them too?
 459  Other horses seem to me to be in front now —
 460  another driver shows. The mares must have come to grief
 461  out on the plain — the ones that led on the way out:
 462  I saw them rounding the turn-post first,
 463  and now I cannot see them anywhere, though my eyes
 464  go searching everywhere across the Trojan plain.
 465  Did the reins escape their driver, so he could not
 466  hold them well around the post, and failed to make the turn?
 467  He was thrown out there, I think, and the chariot wrecked,
 468  and the mares bolted off the course, their hearts gone wild.
 469  But stand up and look for yourselves: I cannot
 470  make it out for certain. The man in front seems to me
 471  to be Aetolian by birth — and a lord among the Argives —
 472  the son of horse-taming Tydeus, strong Diomedes."
 473  Then swift Ajax, Oileus's son, rounded on him shamefully:
 474  "Idomeneus — why this blather before the time? The mares
 475  are far away still, sweeping the wide plain.
 476  You are not so much the youngest among the Argives,
 477  and the eyes in your head are not the sharpest lookers —
 478  yet you are always blathering. There is no need for you
 479  to blather here: better men than you are present.
 480  The mares in front are the same that led before —
 481  Eumelus's; and it is he who stands there holding the reins."
 482  And the leader of the Cretans answered him in anger:
 483  "Ajax — champion of insult, wit of straw — in all things else
 484  you are least of the Argives: your mind is a stubborn mind.
 485  Come then: let us wager a tripod or a cauldron,
 486  and let us both make Agamemnon, Atreus's son, our judge —
 487  whose mares are in front. You will learn as you pay."
 488  So he spoke; and at once swift Ajax, Oileus's son,
 489  rose up in anger to answer with hard words —
 490  and the quarrel between the two would have gone further,
 491  had not Achilles himself stood up, and said his word:
 492  "No longer answer each other with hard words,
 493  Ajax — Idomeneus — with evil words: it is not seemly.
 494  You would be indignant at another man who acted so.
 495  Sit down in the gathering, and watch
 496  for the horses: they are straining for victory, and they
 497  will be here soon enough. Then each of you can know
 498  the Argive horses — who is behind, and who in front."
 499  So he spoke — and the son of Tydeus came driving in nearly upon them,[18]
 500  laying on the whip down from the shoulder, stroke on stroke; and his horses
 501  lifted high, going fast, devouring the way.
 502  And the spatters of dust rained without pause on the driver,
 503  and the chariot, plated with gold and tin,
 504  ran hard on the heels of the quick-footed horses — and behind them
 505  the tires left hardly a trace of wheel-track
 506  in the fine dust: the pair of them flew, straining.
 507  He pulled up in the middle of the gathering, the sweat
 508  streaming to the ground from his horses' necks and chests;
 509  and he himself leapt to the ground from his shining car
 510  and leaned the whip against the yoke. Nor did strong Sthenelus
 511  delay: he seized the prize at once, eagerly,
 512  and gave the woman to his high-hearted companions to lead,
 513  and the eared tripod to carry; and he loosed the horses from the yoke.
 514  Next after him Antilochus of Neleus's line drove in his horses —[19]
 515  by craft, not speed at all, he had slipped past Menelaus;
 516  yet even so Menelaus held his swift horses close behind.
 517  As far as a horse stands from the wheel, when he strains
 518  drawing his master over the plain with the chariot,
 519  and the tips of his tail-hairs brush against the tire,
 520  for he runs close in front, and there is little space between
 521  as he sweeps the wide plain:
 522  so far was Menelaus behind blameless Antilochus —
 523  though at first he was left a discus-throw behind,
 524  he was catching him fast: the fine fury of Aethe,
 525  Agamemnon's lovely-maned mare, was rising still;
 526  and had the course run longer for the two of them,
 527  he would have passed him clean, beyond dispute.
 528  And Meriones, the good aide of Idomeneus,
 529  came in a spear-cast behind famed Menelaus:
 530  for his lovely-maned horses were the slowest of them all,
 531  and he himself the least of them at driving in a race.
 532  And Admetus's son came last of all the others,
 533  dragging his beautiful chariot, driving his horses before him.
 534  And swift-footed brilliant Achilles saw him, and pitied him,[20]
 535  and stood among the Argives and spoke winged words:
 536  "Last comes the best man, driving his single-hoofed horses.
 537  Come, let us give him a prize, as is fitting —
 538  the second; and let the son of Tydeus carry off the first."
 539  So he spoke; and they all approved what he commanded.
 540  And now he would have given him the mare — for the Achaeans approved —
 541  had not Antilochus, great-hearted Nestor's son,
 542  stood up and answered Achilles, Peleus's son, with a claim of right:
 543  "Achilles, I will be very angry with you if you accomplish
 544  this word: for you mean to take away my prize,
 545  weighing this — that his chariot came to grief, and his swift horses,
 546  and he himself a good man. But he should have prayed
 547  to the immortals: then he would not have come in last of all.
 548  If you pity him, and he is dear to your heart,
 549  there is gold in plenty in your hut, and there is bronze,
 550  and sheep and goats, and there are slave-women and single-hoofed horses:
 551  take from those, and give him afterward an even greater prize —
 552  or right now, this moment — and the Achaeans will applaud you.
 553  But the mare I will not give up. For her, let any man
 554  who wants her try me — hand to hand."
 555  So he spoke; and swift-footed brilliant Achilles smiled,
 556  delighting in Antilochus, because he was his dear companion;
 557  and in answer he spoke winged words to him:
 558  "Antilochus — if you would have me give Eumelus
 559  some other thing from my stores, that too I will do.
 560  I will give him the corslet I stripped from Asteropaeus —
 561  bronze, with a casting of shining tin
 562  run round it. It will be worth much to him."
 563  He spoke, and told Automedon, his dear companion,
 564  to bring it from the hut; and he went and brought it,
 565  and laid it in Eumelus's hands; and he received it, rejoicing.
 566  Then Menelaus also rose among them, grieved at heart,[21]
 567  angry past soothing at Antilochus. And the herald
 568  put the staff into his hand, and called the Argives
 569  to silence; and then that man like a god spoke out:
 570  "Antilochus — you who had good sense before — what have you done?
 571  You have shamed my skill and fouled my horses,
 572  throwing your own in front, which were far the worse.
 573  Come then, leaders and counselors of the Argives:
 574  judge between the two of us — and no favor either way —
 575  so no man of the bronze-shirted Achaeans ever says:
 576  'Menelaus beat Antilochus down with lies
 577  and went off leading the mare — his horses were far the worse,
 578  but he himself the greater in rank and power.'
 579  Or come — I will judge it myself; and I say no other
 580  Danaan will fault the judgment: it will be straight.
 581  Antilochus — come here, Zeus-nurtured, as the custom stands:
 582  stand in front of your horses and your chariot, take in your hand
 583  the slender whip you drove with,
 584  lay your hand on your horses, and swear by the earth-upholder,
 585  the earth-shaker, that you did not foul my car by deliberate craft."
 586  Then in his turn sound-minded Antilochus answered him:
 587  "Bear with me now: I am far younger
 588  than you, lord Menelaus — you are the elder, and the better man.
 589  You know how a young man's trespasses come about:
 590  his mind is the quicker, but his judgment thin.
 591  So let your heart endure it. The mare I won —
 592  I give her to you myself. And if you asked of me some greater thing
 593  from my own stores besides, I would choose to give it to you
 594  at once, this moment, Zeus-nurtured — rather than fall
 595  out of your favor all my days, and be wrong before the gods."
 596  So spoke great-hearted Nestor's son, and led the mare
 597  and put her into Menelaus's hands. And that man's heart
 598  was warmed like the dew about the ears of ripening corn,
 599  when the fields are bristling: so, Menelaus,
 600  was the heart in your chest warmed within you.
 601  And he spoke to him in answer winged words:
 602  "Antilochus — now I will yield to you myself, out of my anger:
 603  for you were never scattered or light of mind before.
 604  This once, youth got the better of your judgment;
 605  the next time, take care not to outscheme your betters.
 606  No other man of the Achaeans would have soothed me quickly —
 607  but you have suffered much, and labored much for my sake,
 608  you and your good father and your brother.
 609  So I will be persuaded by your plea; and the mare —
 610  mine though she is, I will give her to you — so these men too may know
 611  that my heart was never overbearing and unbending."
 612  He spoke, and gave the mare to Noëmon, Antilochus's
 613  companion, to lead away; and took for himself the shining cauldron.
 614  And Meriones took up the two talents of gold,
 615  fourth, as he had driven. But the fifth prize was left over —
 616  the two-handled bowl. Achilles gave it to Nestor,
 617  carrying it to him across the gathering of the Argives, and said:
 618  "Here, old man. Let this be a treasure laid away for you,[22]
 619  a memorial of the burial of Patroclus: for never again
 620  will you see him among the Argives. I give you this prize
 621  outright: for you will not box, and you will not wrestle,
 622  nor enter for the javelins, nor race
 623  on foot: the hard weight of age is on you now."
 624  So speaking, he laid it in his hands; and he received it, rejoicing,
 625  and speaking winged words he addressed him:
 626  "Yes, child — all this you have said aright.
 627  My limbs are no longer sound, dear friend — my feet — nor do my arms
 628  swing light from my shoulders on either side.
 629  Would that I were young, and my strength as firm
 630  as when the Epeians buried lord Amarynceus
 631  at Buprasium, and his sons set out the king's prizes.
 632  There no man proved my equal — not of the Epeians,
 633  not of the Pylians themselves, not of the great-hearted Aetolians.
 634  In boxing I beat Clytomedes, Enops's son;
 635  at wrestling, Ancaeus of Pleuron, who stood up against me;
 636  Iphiclus I outran on foot, good runner though he was;
 637  and with the spear I out-threw Phyleus and Polydorus.
 638  Only with the horses did the two sons of Actor pass me —
 639  crowding ahead by force of numbers, grudging me the victory,
 640  because the biggest prizes had been left for the horses.
 641  They were twins: one held the reins — held the reins
 642  for good and all — while the other laid on the whip.
 643  Such I was once. Now let younger men take on
 644  such labors: I must bow to grim old age —
 645  though in my day I shone among the heroes.
 646  But go: honor your companion too with funeral games.
 647  This gift I receive gladly, and my heart rejoices
 648  that you remember me always as your kindly friend, and never forget
 649  the honor with which it is fitting I be honored among the Achaeans.
 650  And may the gods, in return, grant you grace to your heart's fill."
 651  So he spoke; and the son of Peleus went back through the great
 652  crowd of Achaeans, when he had heard out all the praise of Neleus's son.
 653  Then he set out the prizes for the punishing fist-fight:[23]
 654  a hard-working mule he led out and tethered in the gathering,
 655  six years old, unbroken — the hardest kind to break;
 656  and for the beaten man he set a two-handled cup.
 657  Then he stood upright, and spoke his word among the Argives:
 658  "Son of Atreus, and you other strong-greaved Achaeans:
 659  for these we summon two men — the best there are —
 660  to put up their fists and trade blows. The man to whom Apollo
 661  grants endurance, and all the Achaeans acknowledge it,
 662  let him lead the hard-working mule away to his hut;
 663  and the beaten man carries off the two-handled cup."
 664  So he spoke; and at once a man rose up, huge and strong,
 665  skilled in fist-fighting — Epeius, Panopeus's son;
 666  and he laid a hand on the hard-working mule, and said:
 667  "Step up, whoever wants to carry off the two-handled cup:
 668  the mule — I say no other Achaean is taking her
 669  by winning with his fists. I claim to be the best.
 670  Is it not enough that I fall short in battle? There was never a way,
 671  it seems, for one man to be skilled in every work there is.
 672  For I tell you this, and it will be accomplished:
 673  I will split his skin clean open and stave his bones in.
 674  Let his next of kin stand waiting here in a body
 675  to carry him out when my fists have finished him."
 676  So he spoke; and they all fell silent, stilled.
 677  Euryalus alone stood up to face him, a man like a god,
 678  son of lord Mecisteus, Talaus's son,
 679  who once came to Thebes for the burial of fallen
 680  Oedipus, and there beat all the sons of Cadmus.
 681  And Tydeus's spear-famed son busied himself about him,
 682  cheering him with words, wanting the victory for him badly.
 683  First he threw down the girdle for him, and then
 684  he gave him the well-cut thongs of a field-ranging ox.
 685  Girded, the two men strode into the middle of the gathering,
 686  and squaring up, both at once, with their heavy hands,
 687  they fell on each other, and their heavy fists mixed.
 688  Terrible was the grinding of jaws, and the sweat ran
 689  everywhere off their limbs. And brilliant Epeius rose to it:
 690  as the other glanced for an opening, he struck him on the cheek,
 691  and he could stand no longer: there his shining limbs went down.
 692  As under the North Wind's shiver a fish leaps up
 693  on the weed-strewn shallows, and the black wave hides it again:
 694  so he leapt at the blow. But great-hearted Epeius
 695  took him in his hands and set him upright; and his own companions
 696  crowded round and led him through the gathering, feet trailing,
 697  spitting thick blood, his head lolling to one side;
 698  and they sat him down among them, wits astray,
 699  and went themselves and fetched the two-handled cup.
 700  Then the son of Peleus set out at once, and showed the Danaans,[24]
 701  a third set of prizes, for the punishing wrestling:
 702  for the winner a great tripod made to stand over fire —
 703  the Achaeans among themselves priced it at twelve oxen —
 704  and for the beaten man he put a woman in their midst,
 705  skilled in many kinds of work; they priced her at four oxen.
 706  And he stood upright, and spoke his word among the Argives:
 707  "Up — you two who will make trial of this prize."
 708  So he spoke; and then great Telamonian Ajax rose,
 709  and resourceful Odysseus stood up, the knower of advantages.
 710  Girded, the two men strode into the middle of the gathering
 711  and grappled each other arm on arm with their heavy hands,
 712  like the gable-rafters a famous builder joins
 713  in a high house, to baffle the strength of the winds.
 714  Their backs creaked under the stubborn hauling
 715  of bold hands; and the wet sweat ran down;
 716  and welts thick and many along their ribs and shoulders
 717  sprang up red with blood; and always they
 718  strained for victory, and the wrought tripod;
 719  and Odysseus could not trip him and bring him to the ground,
 720  nor could Ajax — the strong build of Odysseus held.
 721  But when they began to weary the strong-greaved Achaeans,
 722  then great Telamonian Ajax said to him:
 723  "Zeus-born son of Laertes, Odysseus of many devices:
 724  lift me — or I lift you. And the rest will be Zeus's business."
 725  So speaking he heaved him up. But Odysseus did not forget his craft:
 726  he caught him with a kick behind the knee, and unstrung his limbs,
 727  and threw him backward, and fell on his chest
 728  himself. The watching army marveled at it.
 729  Second, much-enduring brilliant Odysseus heaved in his turn,
 730  and budged him a little off the ground — but could not lift him —
 731  and hooked his knee inside; and both went down together
 732  close in each other's grip, and were fouled with dust.
 733  And now they would have sprung up and wrestled a third fall,
 734  had not Achilles himself risen, and held them back:
 735  "Strain against each other no more. Do not wear yourselves out with pain.
 736  Victory to you both. Take equal prizes
 737  and go — so the other Achaeans may compete."
 738  So he spoke; and they listened to him well, and obeyed,
 739  and wiped away the dust, and put their tunics on.
 740  Then the son of Peleus set out at once the prizes for the foot-race:[25]
 741  a mixing-bowl of silver, a worked piece: six measures
 742  it held, and in beauty it outdid all others on earth
 743  by far, since craftsmen of Sidon, the cunning-handed, made it well,
 744  and Phoenician men carried it over the misty sea,
 745  set it up in harbor, and gave it as a gift to Thoas;
 746  and Euneus, Jason's son, gave it as the price of Lycaon,
 747  Priam's son, to the hero Patroclus.
 748  This too Achilles set out as a prize for his companion —
 749  for the man who should prove the lightest on his quick feet;
 750  for the second again he set out an ox, big and heavy with fat;
 751  and a half-talent of gold he set out for the last.
 752  And he stood upright, and spoke his word among the Argives:
 753  "Up — you who will make trial of this prize."
 754  So he spoke; and at once swift Ajax rose, Oileus's son,
 755  and resourceful Odysseus stood up, and then Nestor's son
 756  Antilochus: for he beat all the young men at running.
 757  They stood in line; and Achilles showed them the goal.
 758  The course was stretched flat-out from the start; and quickly
 759  the son of Oileus shot ahead; and brilliant Odysseus drove in
 760  close behind — close as the weaving-rod to the breast
 761  of a fair-girdled woman, when she pulls it deftly in her hands,
 762  drawing the spool out along the warp, and holds the rod
 763  near her breast: so close Odysseus ran, and behind him
 764  he struck the man's footprints with his feet before the dust could settle,
 765  and brilliant Odysseus poured his breath down on his head,
 766  running lightly on and on; and all the Achaeans roared
 767  for his straining after victory, and cheered him pressing on.
 768  But when they were running the last of the course, at once
 769  Odysseus prayed in his heart to grey-eyed Athena:
 770  "Hear me, goddess: come a good helper to my feet!"
 771  So he spoke, praying; and Pallas Athena heard him,
 772  and made his limbs light — his feet, and his arms above them.
 773  And when they were on the point of darting in for the prize,
 774  then Ajax slipped at a dead run — for Athena tripped him —
 775  just where the dung lay scattered from the slaughtered bellowing
 776  oxen swift-footed Achilles had killed for Patroclus;
 777  and his mouth and nostrils filled with ox-dung.
 778  So much-enduring brilliant Odysseus took up the bowl,
 779  having come in first; and shining Ajax took the ox.
 780  And he stood there holding the horn of the field-ranging ox,
 781  spitting out dung, and spoke among the Argives:
 782  "Damn it all — the goddess hobbled my feet! — she who forever
 783  stands by Odysseus like a mother, and comes to help him."
 784  So he spoke; and they all laughed at him sweetly.
 785  And Antilochus carried off the last prize
 786  smiling, and spoke his word among the Argives:
 787  "I tell you all what you know already, friends: the immortals
 788  honor the elder generation of men, now as always.
 789  For Ajax is only a little older than I am —
 790  but this one is of an earlier stock, of earlier men:
 791  a green old age, they say, is his. And it is hard work
 792  for any Achaean to race him — except Achilles."
 793  So he spoke, and gave glory to the swift-footed son of Peleus.
 794  And Achilles answered him with his word, and said:
 795  "Antilochus — your praise shall not be spoken for nothing:
 796  I will add to your prize a half-talent of gold."
 797  So speaking, he laid it in his hands; and he received it, rejoicing.
 798  Then the son of Peleus carried into the gathering and set down[26]
 799  a long-shadowed spear, and a shield and helmet —
 800  the armor of Sarpedon, which Patroclus stripped from him.
 801  And he stood upright, and spoke his word among the Argives:
 802  "For these we summon two men — the best there are —
 803  to put on armor, take up the flesh-cutting bronze,
 804  and make trial of each other before the crowd.
 805  Whichever man first reaches the other's fair flesh
 806  and touches the inward parts through armor and dark blood,
 807  to him I will give this sword with its silver studs —
 808  a beautiful Thracian blade I stripped from Asteropaeus;
 809  and the armor — let both men carry it off in common;
 810  and we will set a good feast before them in the huts."
 811  So he spoke; and then great Telamonian Ajax rose,
 812  and up rose the son of Tydeus, strong Diomedes.
 813  And when they had armed themselves on opposite sides of the crowd,
 814  the two came together in the middle, hot for combat,
 815  glaring terribly; and awe held all the Achaeans.
 816  And when they had come close, advancing on each other,
 817  three times they lunged in, three times they charged to close quarters.
 818  Then Ajax stabbed at the balanced round shield —
 819  but did not reach the flesh: the corslet inside it held.
 820  And the son of Tydeus, over the rim of the great shield,
 821  kept reaching for his neck with the point of the shining spear.
 822  And then, in fear for Ajax, the Achaeans
 823  called on them to stop, and take equal prizes.
 824  But to the son of Tydeus the hero gave the great sword,
 825  bearing it with its scabbard and well-cut baldric.
 826  Then the son of Peleus set out a lump of self-smelted iron[27]
 827  that the huge strength of Eëtion used to throw;
 828  but him swift-footed brilliant Achilles killed,
 829  and carried the iron off in his ships with the rest of his goods.
 830  And he stood upright, and spoke his word among the Argives:
 831  "Up — you who will make trial of this prize.
 832  Though his rich fields lie very far away,
 833  the winner will have this to use for five revolving years:
 834  not for want of iron will his shepherd or his plowman
 835  go into town — this will supply them."
 836  So he spoke; and then war-staunch Polypoetes rose,
 837  and the strong might of godlike Leonteus rose,
 838  and Ajax, Telamon's son, and brilliant Epeius.
 839  They stood in order; and brilliant Epeius took the weight,
 840  whirled, and let it fly; and all the Achaeans laughed at him.
 841  Second in turn Leonteus, sprig of Ares, threw it;
 842  and third, great Telamonian Ajax flung it
 843  from his heavy hand, and passed the marks of all the others.
 844  But when war-staunch Polypoetes took up the weight —
 845  far as a herdsman sends his throwing-staff
 846  spinning away through the herds of his cattle —
 847  so far past the whole field he threw it; and the people shouted;
 848  and the companions of strong Polypoetes stood up
 849  and carried the king's prize away to the hollow ships.
 850  Then for the archers he set out the dark-blue iron:[28]
 851  ten double axes he laid down, and ten half-axes,
 852  and he set up the mast of a dark-prowed ship
 853  far off in the sands, and to it, by a thin cord,
 854  tied a trembling dove by the foot, and commanded them
 855  to shoot at her: "Whoever hits the trembling dove,
 856  let him take up all the double axes and carry them home;
 857  whoever hits the cord, and misses the bird —
 858  he is the lesser man: let him carry off the half-axes."
 859  So he spoke; and then the strength of lord Teucer rose,
 860  and up rose Meriones, the good aide of Idomeneus.
 861  They took lots and shook them in a bronze helmet,
 862  and Teucer drew first place; and at once he let fly
 863  an arrow with all his force — but did not vow to the lord
 864  a famous hecatomb of firstling lambs.
 865  So he missed the bird — Apollo grudged him that —
 866  but hit the cord beside her foot, where the bird was tied,
 867  and the bitter arrow cut the cord clean through.
 868  She darted up toward heaven; and the cord fell slack
 869  toward the earth; and the Achaeans thundered.
 870  But Meriones, in one motion, snatched the bow
 871  out of his hand — an arrow he had held ready while the other aimed —
 872  and vowed at once to Apollo who strikes from afar
 873  a famous hecatomb of firstling lambs;
 874  and high under the clouds he saw the trembling dove:
 875  there, as she circled, he shot her under the wing, full through,
 876  and the shaft passed clean out, and came back down to plant itself
 877  in the earth before Meriones's foot. And the bird
 878  settled on the mast of the dark-prowed ship,
 879  her neck drooped over, and the close-set wings went slack,
 880  and the life flew swift from her limbs, and she dropped far down
 881  from the mast; and the watching army marveled at it.
 882  And Meriones took up all ten double axes,
 883  and Teucer carried the half-axes to the hollow ships.
 884  Then, last, the son of Peleus carried into the gathering[29]
 885  a long-shadowed spear and an unfired cauldron worked with flowers,
 886  worth an ox; and the javelin-throwers stood up:
 887  up rose the son of Atreus, wide-ruling Agamemnon,
 888  and up rose Meriones, the good aide of Idomeneus.
 889  And among them spoke swift-footed brilliant Achilles:
 890  "Son of Atreus — we know how far you surpass all others,
 891  how much you are the best in power and in the throw:
 892  take this prize with you to the hollow ships,
 893  and let us give the spear to the hero Meriones —
 894  if your own heart is willing: for that is what I urge."
 895  So he spoke; and the lord of men Agamemnon did not refuse.
 896  Achilles gave the bronze spear to Meriones; and the hero
 897  handed his prize, the beautiful cauldron, to Talthybius the herald.

NOTES

[1] (23.1-16) The Myrmidons mourn mounted: three circuits of
    the body with horses and chariots — cavalry honors, the
    war-machine itself made to grieve — with Thetis stirring
    "the desire of lament" (γόου ἵμερος: grief in Homer is an
    appetite, roused, fed, and satisfied). Achilles' ruling is
    the book's charter: "that is the dead man's due" (γέρας
    θανόντων) — the prize of the dead, in the same word the
    living quarreled over in Book 1.

[2] (23.17-25) The vow reported to its beneficiary: 22-23
    repeat the wording of 18.336-337 (register kept), and
    Hector is laid "face-down in the dust beside the bier" —
    the outrage arranged like a grave-good. The poem keeps
    Achilles' promise-keeping and its horror in one frame:
    he is exact with the dead on both sides of hate.

[3] (23.26-58) The funeral feast — "everywhere around the
    corpse the blood ran in cupfuls" — and the refusal to
    wash: the fast of Book 19 (19.208-210, 305-308) still
    governs Achilles' body; he yields to the "hateful feast"
    but swears off water "before I lay Patroclus on the fire."
    His oath contains the book's quiet self-knowledge: "never
    a second time will such grief reach my heart, while I am
    among the living" — true both ways, as he intends it and
    as the poem knows it: there is not much "while" left.

[4] (23.59-92) The ghost. Patroclus comes "in everything like
    the man himself" — stature, the beautiful eyes, the voice,
    even the clothes — and opens with the gentlest rebuke in
    epic: "You sleep — and you have forgotten me, Achilles."
    His requests are a dead man's administration: burial now,
    because the other souls bar him from crossing the river
    (the poem's one glimpse of the afterlife's waiting-room);
    the hand ("I am weeping"); and the shared urn — grounded
    in the biography only now revealed: Patroclus came to
    Peleus's house as a child-killer, a boy who slew the son
    of Amphidamas "angry over the dice — a child's act; I did
    not mean it." The gentlest man in the poem was a refugee
    from his own temper: gentleness, the lines quietly say,
    was something he built. Aristarchus athetized 92 (the
    golden amphora, "which your lady mother gave you" —
    Dionysus's gift to Thetis in the later tradition;
    Aeschines' text lacked it), but the request stands: one
    vessel, two friends, the arrangement the Odyssey (24.74)
    confirms was kept.

[5] (23.93-108) The failed embrace: Achilles reaches, "and
    grasped nothing: the soul, like smoke, was gone under the
    earth, squeaking." His waking cry is the plainest
    statement of Homeric eschatology: "so there is, even in
    the house of Hades, a soul and an image (ψυχὴ καὶ
    εἴδωλον) — but the wits (φρένες) are not in it at all."
    (Aristophanes athetized the line as too doctrinal; it is
    the shape of the whole scene.) The dead persist as form
    without force — enough to be loved, too little to be
    held: the exact measure of what the living owe them,
    which is everything this book does.

[6] (23.109-140) The wood-train — Meriones's logistics, the
    mules, the felled oaks "with a great crashing" — ends at
    the shore where Achilles plans "the great mound for
    Patroclus — and for himself": his own funeral entering
    the arrangements as a fact needing no comment. The army
    heaps the corpse with cut hair; Achilles carries the
    head — chief mourner's station, as Thetis held his
    (18.71).

[7] (23.141-154) The Spercheius lock: hair grown long for a
    homecoming vow — Peleus's fifty rams at the river's
    sources — cut now and given to the dead instead: "since
    I am not going home to my dear fatherland." The vow of
    return is formally transferred to the grave; no line in
    the book cancels Achilles' future more quietly. He says
    it looking at the sea — the road home, declined.

[8] (23.161-192) The pyre, a hundred feet each way, and its
    freight: fat-wrapped corpse, honey and oil, four horses,
    two of the nine table-dogs — and the twelve Trojan youths
    of the vow, butchered with the narrator's own verdict
    attached: "and evil was the work his heart intended"
    (κακὰ δὲ φρεσὶ μήδετο ἔργα). The poem does not flinch
    from human sacrifice and does not condone it: one clause
    of judgment, unique in the epic, set exactly here. And
    then, within eight lines, Book 24 quietly begins:
    Aphrodite keeping the dogs from Hector "by day and by
    night," the oil of roses, Apollo's sheltering cloud —
    the gods already voting against the rage they have
    stopped assisting.

[9] (23.193-232) The winds must be asked: Iris fetches Boreas
    and Zephyrus from a banquet (declining a seat — she is
    expected at the Aethiopians': divine society continuing
    around the edges of grief), and they burn the pyre all
    night while Achilles pours wine and calls the soul. Over
    it, the poem's most terrible simile of mourning: "As a
    father mourns, burning the bones of his son, a
    bridegroom" — the worst grief the Greek imagination
    could name, a child dead at the threshold of marriage —
    given to a friend for a friend, and doubled by the fact
    that the mourner's own father will soon have precisely
    this grief and no body to burn.

[10] (23.236-257) The white bones "of their kindly companion"
    (ἐνηέος — the dead man's adjective) into the golden
    vessel "until I myself am hidden in Hades"; the mound
    deliberately modest, "then afterward the Achaeans may
    build it wide and high — you who are left behind me":
    Achilles designs his own tomb as an afterthought to his
    friend's, assigning the enlargement to survivors he has
    already stopped counting himself among. The funeral is
    double throughout; only one of the dead is present.

[11] (23.257-271) The pivot of the book: "But Achilles held
    the army there" — grief converted, by decree, into
    games. The prize-list is the Iliad's economy in
    miniature: women, tripods, mares, cauldrons, gold,
    iron — the same currencies men died over, now to be
    won by rule.

[12] (23.271-286) Achilles' abdication: his immortal horses
    would win, so he withdraws — and the reason is their
    mourning: they stand with manes trailing the ground for
    "the gentle one" (ἠπίου), who washed them and oiled
    their manes. Patroclus's epithet-world (kindly, gentle)
    persists in the memory of animals; the horses that wept
    at 17.426-440 are still in mourning, and their grief
    disqualifies the field's best entry.

[13] (23.287-351) Nestor's sermon on μῆτις — "by cunning,
    far more than by force, is the woodcutter better; by
    cunning the helmsman... by cunning charioteer outdoes
    charioteer" — the poem's most explicit praise of
    intelligence over strength, delivered to a son whose
    horses are slowest. The turn-post itself is the book's
    emblem: "either it is the grave-mark of a man dead long
    ago, or it was set for a turning-post by men before our
    time; and now Achilles has made it the goal" — a
    monument whose meaning is already lost, reused as
    sport: what tombs become, in the long run the poem
    knows all about. The driving instructions (lean left,
    goad the right-hand horse, graze the post with the hub)
    are the oldest coaching manual in literature.

[14] (23.352-373) The start: lots drawn (Antilochus first —
    the inside track his tactics will need), Phoenix as
    umpire "to keep the running in memory and report the
    truth," and the break — dust like cloud or stormwind,
    manes streaming, the cars now hugging earth, now
    airborne, "and each man's heart was pounding in his
    hunger for victory." The poem's joy in speed, absent
    since the chariots ran for Hector's life, is given back
    clean.

[15] (23.373-402) The gods play the race as they played the
    war: Apollo dashes the whip from Diomedes (still angry
    from Book 5, perhaps — or just partisan); Athena
    returns it and breaks Eumelus's yoke — the best
    horseman skinned across mouth and elbows in the dust.
    Divine interference, which cost Patroclus his life and
    Hector his, here costs a man a mare and his skin: the
    same machinery at play-scale, safe at last.

[16] (23.402-447) Antilochus's foul: the speech to his
    horses (threatening them with Nestor's knife —
    charioteer's hyperbole; "Aethe will pour shame over
    you: a female!"), the sunken narrows, and the swerve
    that forces Menelaus to yield or wreck. The narrator
    is exact: Menelaus dropped back "by his own will," the
    prudence of the elder buying both men's safety — and
    his shout ("you shall not carry off the prize without
    an oath") files the case the assembly will hear. The
    young man drives "like a man who had not heard":
    Nestor's cunning-lecture, over-learned in one
    generation.

[17] (23.448-499) The spectators' quarrel — Idomeneus's
    sharp-eyed call (the chestnut with the moon-round
    blaze), Oilean Ajax's gratuitous insult, the proposed
    wager with Agamemnon as judge — is Book 1 in a teacup:
    two proud men, a prize, an arbiter. And it is stopped
    by Achilles — "it is not seemly... You would be
    indignant at another man who acted so" — the poem's
    original quarreler now its peacemaker, speaking to
    others the sentence nobody managed to speak to him.
    The reformation is complete and completely unremarked:
    the poem lets you notice it yourself.

[18] (23.499-514) Diomedes wins — whip-strokes "down from
    the shoulder," the gold-and-tin car running the horses'
    heels down, wheel-tracks barely printed in the fine
    dust (speed measured by what it fails to leave), and
    the cool gesture at the end: the whip leaned against
    the yoke. Sthenelus takes the woman and the tripod:
    Book 5's team, intact, at play.

[19] (23.514-533) The placings, with the poem's own verdict
    on Antilochus: "by craft, not speed at all" (κέρδεσιν,
    οὔ τι τάχει γε). The tail-hairs-brushing-the-tire
    figure measures Menelaus's closing surge; Meriones a
    spear-cast back; and last, dragging his wreck, "the
    best man" — Eumelus, whose excellence the race no
    longer records, which is why the next scene has to.

[20] (23.534-566) Justice, round one: Achilles proposes
    second prize for Eumelus (pity ranking worth above
    result), and Antilochus objects on procedure — the
    mare is won; compensate from your own stores — with
    the challenge-tag of a man standing on right ("let any
    man who wants her try me — hand to hand"). And
    Achilles smiled — his only smile in the Iliad —
    "delighting in Antilochus, because he was his dear
    companion." The word ἑταῖρος, freighted with all the
    book's grief, spent here on the living: the smile is
    the poem's first evidence that Achilles can still be
    reached. Eumelus gets Asteropaeus's corslet; everyone
    is paid; nobody is wronged.

[21] (23.566-613) Justice, round two — the poem's model
    trial. Menelaus rises "grieved at heart," takes the
    herald's staff (procedure!), and offers two courses:
    judgment by the leaders, "and no favor either way," or
    — better — the self-executing oath: stand before your
    horses, whip in hand, touch the team, and swear by the
    earth-upholder, the earth-shaker, that the foul was not
    deliberate. Antilochus folds precisely at the oath —
    he will not perjure — with the young man's confession
    for all time: "his mind is the quicker, but his
    judgment thin" — and hands over the mare with his whole
    inheritance in one sentence rather than "be wrong
    before the gods." And Menelaus melts — the dew-on-the-
    ripening-corn simile, the gentlest in the poem, for
    the untensing of an angry heart — and gives the mare
    back. Compare Book 1: a prize, a grievance, an
    assembly — and there, wrath; here, yielding met by
    yielding. This scene is the Iliad showing what the
    quarrel of Achilles and Agamemnon should have been.

[22] (23.615-652) Nestor's prize for nothing: the leftover
    bowl, given "a memorial of the burial of Patroclus:
    for never again will you see him among the Argives."
    The old man's answer is his career in miniature — the
    games for Amarynceus at Buprasium, four victories
    named, and the one defeat: the Molione twins who beat
    him "by force of numbers... one held the reins — held
    the reins for good and all — while the other laid on
    the whip" (the doubled phrase is the Greek's own, an
    old man's groove in the telling). His thanks name the
    thing the whole book trades in: "my heart rejoices
    that you remember me always as your kindly friend"
    (ἐνηέος — Patroclus's word, 252). Honor, in the end,
    is being remembered kindly: the games' true currency,
    and the poem's.

[23] (23.653-700) The boxing: Epeius's magnificent boast
    with its disarming core — "Is it not enough that I
    fall short in battle? There was never a way for one
    man to be skilled in every work there is" — the most
    honest self-assessment in the epic (this is the man
    who will build the wooden horse: his skill was
    coming). Euryalus's pedigree is a funeral-games
    résumé — he won at the burial of "fallen Oedipus" at
    Thebes, the Iliad's only notice of Oedipus, dead at
    home and buried with games. One counter-punch ends
    it; the fish-leap simile under Boreas's shiver lands
    him; and Epeius "took him in his hands and set him
    upright" — courtesy inside violence, the book's whole
    ethic in one gesture.

[24] (23.700-740) The wrestling, with the poem's price-list
    attached: the tripod worth twelve oxen; the woman
    "skilled in many kinds of work," worth four. The Iliad
    states its world's arithmetic without comment, and no
    reader is meant to pass it in silence: this is the
    economy the whole war runs on. Ajax and Odysseus lock
    like "gable-rafters a famous builder joins"; two falls
    — Odysseus's knee-trick, then the failed lift and
    mutual tumble — and Achilles calls it: "Victory to
    you both." Force and cunning, the poem's two great
    modes, wrestle to a draw and are sent to dinner.

[25] (23.740-798) The foot-race, and the bowl with a
    biography: Sidonian work, Phoenician freight, guest-
    gift to Thoas, and then — the price of Lycaon, paid by
    Euneus to Patroclus. The object that once bought
    Lycaon's life (21.40-41) is now the prize at the
    funeral of the man it was paid to, raced for while
    Lycaon lies in the river. Objects in Homer remember
    what men would rather not. The race itself is the
    book's comedy: Odysseus running so close he treads
    Ajax's prints "before the dust could settle," breathing
    on his head (with the weaving-woman simile — the
    poem's most domestic measure of distance); the prayer;
    Athena's trip; Ajax face-down in the sacrificial
    ox-dung, spitting, with the complaint that convulses
    the army: "she stands by Odysseus like a mother!" —
    "and they all laughed at him sweetly" (ἡδύ). Laughter,
    which this poem has mostly given to gods and scorners,
    comes back to men, sweet, at a funeral. Antilochus's
    graceful last-place speech ("the immortals honor the
    elder generation... hard work to race him — except
    Achilles") wins a half-talent bonus for pure charm.

[26] (23.798-826) The armed duel — for Sarpedon's armor,
    Patroclus's greatest spoil (16.663-665) — is the one
    event that edges too close to the real thing: first
    blood "through armor and dark blood" for
    Asteropaeus's Thracian sword. Ajax and Diomedes
    close "glaring terribly; and awe held all the
    Achaeans"; Diomedes keeps reaching over the shield-rim
    for the neck — the exact stroke that killed Hector —
    and the Achaeans, "in fear for Ajax," stop it. Play
    that can kill is called before it does: the community
    knows where the line is now. (Aristophanes' text
    varied the victory-terms; Aristarchus athetized 806
    and 810; 824-825 were athetized too — antiquity was
    uneasy exactly where the game is uneasy.)

[27] (23.826-849) The weight-throw: the prize is the
    implement — Eëtion's self-smelted iron lump, war-booty
    from Andromache's father's city, now five years'
    supply for a winner's shepherd and plowman: iron
    passing from a king's sport through sack and spoil to
    farm-tools — the poem's whole material history in one
    object. Polypoetes's cast, measured by a herdsman's
    throwing-staff "spinning away through the herds," wins
    it for the ships.

[28] (23.850-883) The archery: a dove on a cord at the
    mast-head. Teucer shoots without vowing the hecatomb
    — "Apollo grudged him that" — and cuts the cord;
    Meriones, arrow already in hand, vows, and takes the
    bird under the wing as she circles the clouds. Piety
    as marksmanship's missing variable, scored like the
    contest it is; and the poem's last death — after
    Hector, after the twelve — is a dove's, drooping her
    neck on the mast "and the life flew swift from her
    limbs." The Iliad's killing ends in miniature,
    feathered, at a game.

[29] (23.884-897) The close: Agamemnon stands up for the
    javelin — and Achilles awards him the prize without a
    throw: "we know how far you surpass all others...
    take this prize" — with the spear to Meriones. The
    poem's first quarrel was these two men over a prize;
    its last prize passes between them ungrudged,
    unthrown-for, by the loser of Book 1 now presiding in
    perfect courtesy. Agamemnon hands the cauldron to
    Talthybius — the herald who once came to Achilles'
    hut to take Briseis (1.320-348) now receives, from
    Agamemnon's own hands, a gift born of Achilles'
    graciousness. The circle shuts without a word of
    comment: the games have done what nine years of war
    could not. All that remains is a corpse behind the
    hut, and a father across the plain — Book 24.
