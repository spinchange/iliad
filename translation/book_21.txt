BOOK 21

Half the rout is driven into the river, and the river fights back:
Lycaon's bared throat, Asteropaeus's two spears, Scamander rising
against the man who has choked him with corpses — water against
fire, gods against gods — until Troy's gates gape for the routed,
and Apollo, wearing a man's face, runs Achilles out of the way.

TRANSLATOR'S COMMENTARY

Book 21 is the wildest book of the Iliad — the one where the
fighting bursts every frame that holds it: ransom, river-banks,
even the difference between the elements. It opens at the ford of
Xanthus (the lines repeat 14.433-434, where the stunned Hector was
laid at this same crossing: the river of Trojan refuge becomes the
trap), where Achilles cuts the rout in two, drives half into the
water, and wades in after them with a sword — locusts before a
grass-fire, fish before a dolphin. He takes twelve boys alive for
Patroclus's pyre, collecting the vow of 18.336-337, and then meets
Lycaon.

The Lycaon scene (34-135) is the moral center of the second half
of the poem. The poem gives him the fullest biography of any minor
figure — caught at night cutting fig-shoots for chariot-rails,
sold to Lemnos, ransomed, home eleven days, and on the twelfth
delivered by "the god" back into the same hands — precisely so
that his death will be the death of the old economy: Achilles had
sold him once, because "sparing was dearer to my heart"; now
"Fool — offer me no ransom." What follows the refusal is the most
quoted consolation in Greek literature, spoken with the spear
raised: "So, friend — you die too. Why lament it so? Patroclus
died, and he was a far better man than you... over me too stand
death and strong fate: there will be a dawn, or an evening, or a
midday..." (note [6]). The word is φίλος — friend — and it is not
irony: it is the fellowship of the doomed, which is the only
fellowship Achilles has left. Lycaon opens his arms and takes the
sword; the body goes to the fish, with a taunt that cancels every
funeral rite the poem holds dear — and the river begins to be
angry.

Asteropaeus, who fights with a spear in each hand and draws the
only blood Achilles loses in the Iliad, dies over the argument his
corpse then illustrates: rivers against Zeus. Achilles' boast
descends the ladder — Zeus beats rivers; not even Achelous, "not
even the great strength of deep-flowing Ocean, from whom all
rivers and all the sea and all springs and deep wells flow," can
face the thunderbolt. Zenodotus's text omitted the Ocean line,
crowning Achelous the first of waters — the oldest recorded Greek
quarrel about cosmology, sitting in our apparatus (note [10]).
Then the river itself has had enough: "my lovely streams are full
of corpses... and you kill and kill." The battle with Scamander
(233-382) is the poem's one war between a man and an element, and
it strips Achilles to something no enemy has seen: running,
outrun, wailing to Zeus that his mother lied, dreading the
"wretched death" of a boy swept down a gully — death by water,
without glory, without a grave. The river's threat makes that
explicit in the poem's bitterest mock-burial: sand and shingle
heaped so deep "the Achaeans will not know where to gather his
bones... he will need no heaped tomb" (note [16]). Rescue comes as
plot: Poseidon and Athena, hand in hand, dictate the rest of the
epic — pen the Trojans, kill Hector, take the glory — and Hera
throws Hephaestus at the water. Fire drinks the plain; the
riverside trees burn by name; eels tumble; the god of water
surrenders while boiling "like a cauldron melting the lard of a
fatted hog."

Then the Theomachy the poem has owed us since Book 20 — and it is
played as comedy under one laughing spectator ("Zeus heard,
sitting on Olympus, and his own heart laughed for joy"). Athena
fells Ares with a boundary-stone and Aphrodite with a fist; Hera
boxes Artemis's ears with her own bow; Hermes politely forfeits to
Leto; Artemis ends in Zeus's lap in tears. Only one exchange is in
earnest: Poseidon reminds Apollo of Laomedon's cheat — the wall
built for a wage never paid, the threatened ear-lopping — and
Apollo refuses combat with the most devastating thing any god says
about us in the poem: mortals are not worth a fight, "who like the
leaves now flourish in their blaze... and now wither heartless
away" — Glaucus's leaf-simile of 6.146 returned from above, as
contempt (note [24]). The gods go home; Achilles remains — smoke
of a burning city — and the book ends with Priam opening the
gates, Agenor's lonely stand (the soliloquy where a frightened man
discovers that Achilles too "has one life"), and Apollo in
Agenor's shape running the fastest man alive across the plain,
"always just a little ahead," while everything that is left of
Troy's army pours inside the walls — everything except the man
who will be standing outside them when the next book begins.

 130  Not even the river will save you — the fair-flowing,
 131  silver-eddying river you have honored so long with slaughtered bulls,

 141  the son of Pelegon, whom wide-flowing Axius
 142  begot on Periboea, eldest of the daughters

 151  Unhappy are the fathers whose sons face my fury!"

 159  he fathered Pelegon, famed with the spear; and of Pelegon

 221  Come, let it be. Horror takes me, leader of the people."

 263  so the wave of the current forever caught Achilles,
 264  fast as he was: gods are stronger than men.

 344  the many corpses that crowded it — Achilles' killing;
 345  and the whole plain was dried, and the shining water stopped.
 346  As when in late summer the North Wind quickly dries
 347  a new-watered orchard, and the man who tends it is glad:

 349  the corpses. Then he turned his all-shining flame on the river.
 350  The elms burned, and the willows, and the tamarisks;
 351  the lotus burned, and the rushes, and the galingale,
 352  which grew in abundance around the river's beautiful streams;
 353  and the eels and the fish were in torment down the eddies,
 354  tumbling this way and that through the beautiful streams,
 355  worn down by the blast of resourceful Hephaestus.
 356  And the strong river burned, and spoke a word, and named him:[19]
 357  "Hephaestus! — no one of the gods can stand against you,
 358  and I will not fight you, flaming with fire as you are.
 359  Leave off the strife. As for the Trojans — let brilliant Achilles
 360  drive them out of their city this instant. What are strife and rescue to me?"
 361  He spoke, burning in the fire, and his beautiful streams were seething.
 362  As a cauldron boils within, hurried by a great fire,
 363  melting down the lard of a fatted hog,
 364  bubbling up on every side, with dry split wood beneath it:
 365  so his beautiful streams blazed with the fire, and the water boiled,
 366  and would not flow onward, but stood checked: the breath
 367  of mighty, wise Hephaestus wore him down. And to Hera,
 368  begging hard, he spoke winged words:
 369  "Hera, why has your son fallen on my current, to torment it
 370  out of all others? Surely I am not so much to blame
 371  as all the rest of them — all the helpers of Troy.
 372  I will stop — I promise it — if you command it;
 373  but let him stop as well. And I will swear this oath besides:
 374  never to ward the evil day away from the Trojans,
 375  not even when all Troy is burning in ravening fire —
 376  burning, and the warlike sons of the Achaeans light it."
 377  Now when the goddess white-armed Hera heard that,
 378  at once she called to Hephaestus, her dear son:
 379  "Hephaestus — hold, my glorious child. It is not seemly
 380  to batter an immortal god this way for the sake of mortals."
 381  So she spoke; and Hephaestus quenched the unearthly fire,
 382  and the wave ran back down the beautiful streams.
 383  And when the fury of Xanthus was beaten, those two[20]
 384  rested — for Hera held them back, angry as she was.
 385  But on the other gods fell strife, heavy and grinding,
 386  and the hearts in their chests blew two ways at once;
 387  and they fell together with a great crash, and the wide earth rang,
 388  and the great heaven trumpeted around them. And Zeus heard it,
 389  sitting on Olympus; and his own heart laughed
 390  for joy, when he saw the gods coming together in strife.
 391  Then they stood apart no longer. Ares began it —[21]
 392  the shield-piercer: first he sprang at Athena,
 393  bronze spear in hand, and flung the word of insult:
 394  "Why, dog-fly — why do you drive the gods together in strife
 395  with your gale of daring, your great heart egging you on?
 396  Do you not remember when you set Diomedes, Tydeus's son,
 397  on me to wound me — and took the spear yourself, in plain sight,
 398  and drove it straight at me, and tore my beautiful flesh?
 399  Now, I think, you will pay me for all you have done."
 400  So speaking, he stabbed at the tasseled aegis —
 401  the terrible aegis that not even Zeus's thunderbolt breaks:
 402  there blood-stained Ares stabbed her with his long spear.
 403  But she, giving ground, caught up a stone in her thick hand —
 404  a stone that lay on the plain, black, jagged, and huge,
 405  which men of former times had set to be a field's boundary-marker.
 406  With it she hit rushing Ares in the neck, and unstrung his limbs.
 407  Seven acres he covered, falling; and his hair was fouled with dust,
 408  and his armor clattered around him. And Pallas Athena laughed,
 409  and triumphing over him she spoke winged words:
 410  "Baby fool — you have never yet reckoned how much stronger
 411  I claim to be, that you match your fury against mine.
 412  So pay in full your mother's curses — the Erinyes
 413  she wishes on you in her anger, because you deserted
 414  the Achaeans and defend the arrogant Trojans."
 415  So speaking, she turned her shining eyes away.
 416  And Aphrodite, daughter of Zeus, took Ares by the hand[22]
 417  and led him off, groaning thick and fast, barely gathering his spirit.
 418  But when the goddess white-armed Hera noticed her,
 419  at once she spoke winged words to Athena:
 420  "Shame! Unwearied one, child of aegis-bearing Zeus —
 421  there goes the dog-fly again, leading Ares, bane of mortals,
 422  out of the deadly war through the chaos. After her!"
 423  So she spoke; and Athena sped after, glad at heart,
 424  and rushing on Aphrodite she struck her on the breast
 425  with her thick hand; and there her knees went slack, and her own heart.
 426  So the two of them lay on the earth that feeds many,
 427  and Athena, triumphing, spoke winged words:
 428  "Such may they all be now — all the helpers of the Trojans
 429  when they fight the armored Argives:
 430  just so bold, and just so hardy, as Aphrodite
 431  coming to second Ares, and facing my fury.
 432  Then long ago we should have rested from the war,
 433  with the well-built citadel of Ilion sacked."
 434  So she spoke; and the goddess white-armed Hera smiled.
 435  And the lord earth-shaker addressed Apollo:[23]
 436  "Phoebus — why do we two stand apart? It is not fitting,
 437  when the others have begun. The greater shame if we go back
 438  fightless to Olympus, to the bronze-floored house of Zeus.
 439  Begin: you are the younger born. It would not be right
 440  for me, since I was born first and know more things.
 441  Baby fool — how mindless a heart you have! Do you not even
 442  remember all we two suffered around Ilion,
 443  alone of the gods, when we came from Zeus and drudged
 444  a whole year for haughty Laomedon at a stated wage,
 445  and he gave the orders, and we served?
 446  I built the Trojans their wall around the city —
 447  wide, and very beautiful, so the city might be unbreakable —
 448  while you, Phoebus, herded the shambling twist-horned cattle
 449  in the spurs of wooded, many-folded Ida.
 450  But when the glad seasons brought around the term
 451  of our hire, then he robbed us of all our wage by force —
 452  terrifying Laomedon — and sent us off with threats.
 453  He threatened to bind our feet, and our hands above them,
 454  and ship us for sale to far-off islands;
 455  and he vowed to lop the ears from both of us with bronze.
 456  So back we went, the two of us, with rancor in our hearts,
 457  in anger for the wage he promised and did not pay.
 458  And it is his people you now favor — instead of trying,
 459  with us, to make the arrogant Trojans perish
 460  root and branch, evilly, with their children and honored wives!"
 461  Then in turn the lord Apollo, the worker-from-afar, answered him:[24]
 462  "Earth-shaker — you would say I was unsound of mind
 463  if I fought against you for the sake of mortals —
 464  pitiful things, who like the leaves at one time
 465  flourish in their blaze, eating the fruit of the plowland,
 466  and at another wither heartless away. No — quickly,
 467  let us rest from battle. Let them do their own fighting."
 468  So speaking he turned away: for he shrank in awe
 469  from trading blows with his father's brother.
 470  But his sister rebuked him bitterly — the lady of wild beasts,[25]
 471  Artemis of the wilds — and flung the word of insult:
 472  "So you run, worker-from-afar! — and hand Poseidon the whole
 473  victory, and give him glory for nothing!
 474  Baby fool — why do you carry that bow, useless as wind?
 475  Never again let me hear you in our father's halls
 476  boasting, as before among the immortal gods,
 477  that you would fight Poseidon face to face."
 478  So she spoke; and Apollo the worker-from-afar answered nothing;
 479  but the honored wife of Zeus, in anger,
 480  reviled the shooter of arrows with words of insult:
 481  "And how is it you are eager, shameless bitch, to stand
 482  against me? I am a hard match for your fury,
 483  bow-carrier though you are — you whom Zeus made a lion
 484  to women, and gave you leave to kill whichever you choose.
 485  Better, surely, to slaughter beasts on the mountains,
 486  and wild deer, than to fight by force with your betters.
 487  But if you want a lesson in war — so you may know well
 488  how much stronger I am, when you match your fury against mine —"
 489  She spoke, and with her left hand caught both the other's hands
 490  at the wrist, and with her right stripped the bow from her shoulders,
 491  and with the bow itself, smiling, she boxed her about the ears
 492  as she twisted this way and that; and the swift arrows spilled out.
 493  And the goddess fled from under her, weeping, like a dove
 494  that flies from a hawk into a hollow rock,
 495  a cleft — for it was not her portion to be caught:
 496  so she fled weeping, and left her bow and arrows where they lay.
 497  And to Leto spoke the courier, the slayer of Argus:[26]
 498  "Leto, I will not fight you — no: it is a hard thing
 499  to trade blows with the wives of cloud-gatherer Zeus.
 500  With all my heart's goodwill: boast among the immortal gods
 501  that you overcame me with your strong force."
 502  So he spoke; and Leto gathered up the curved bow
 503  and the arrows, fallen this way and that in the swirl of dust,
 504  and taking her daughter's bow and arrows she went back.
 505  And the girl came to Olympus, to the bronze-floored house of Zeus,[27]
 506  and sat down weeping on her father's knees,
 507  the ambrosial robe trembling around her; and her father
 508  the son of Cronos drew her to him, and asked, laughing sweetly:
 509  "Who of the sons of Heaven, dear child, has treated you so —
 510  for nothing — as if you had been caught doing wrong in the open?"
 511  And the fair-crowned goddess of the clamorous hunt answered him:
 512  "Your wife battered me, father — white-armed Hera,
 513  from whom strife and quarrel cling to the immortals."
 514  So the two of them spoke such things to one another.[28]
 515  And Phoebus Apollo went down into holy Ilion:
 516  for the wall of the well-built city was on his mind —
 517  lest the Danaans sack it that day, beyond its fate.
 518  The other everlasting gods went to Olympus,
 519  some in anger, and some in high triumph,
 520  and sat down beside the father of the black cloud. But Achilles
 521  went on killing the Trojans — the men and their single-hoofed horses.
 522  As when smoke rises and reaches the wide heaven
 523  from a burning city — the wrath of the gods has sent it up,
 524  and it lays labor on all, and grief on many:
 525  so Achilles laid labor and grief on the Trojans.
 526  And old Priam stood on the god-built tower,
 527  and made out gigantic Achilles: and there before him
 528  the Trojans were being driven pell-mell in rout, and no defense
 529  was left. He cried out, and came down from the tower,
 530  to rouse the famed gate-keepers along the wall:
 531  "Hold the gates spread open in your hands, until the army
 532  comes into the city in its rout: for here is Achilles
 533  close by, driving them. Now, I think, comes disaster.
 534  But the moment they are gathered inside the wall and breathing,
 535  close the tight-fitted double doors again:
 536  I dread that ruinous man — that he may leap inside the wall."
 537  So he spoke; and they loosed the gates and thrust back the bars,
 538  and the gates, spread wide, made a light of safety. And Apollo
 539  sprang out to meet the rout, to beat destruction from the Trojans.
 540  And they, straight for the city and the high wall,
 541  parched with thirst and caked with dust, came fleeing
 542  out of the plain; and he pressed hard behind them with the spear:
 543  raging madness held his heart, and he strained for glory.
 544  Then the sons of the Achaeans would have taken high-gated Troy,[29]
 545  had not Phoebus Apollo stirred up brilliant Agenor —
 546  Antenor's son, a man blameless and strong.
 547  Into his heart he threw courage, and himself stood
 548  beside him, to beat away the heavy hands of death,
 549  leaning on the oak tree, wrapped in thick mist.
 550  Now when Agenor caught sight of Achilles, sacker of cities,
 551  he stood fast; but the heart in him churned dark as he waited,
 552  and troubled, he spoke to his own great-hearted spirit:
 553  "Ah me! If I run from strong Achilles
 554  the way the rest are driven, panicked, in rout —
 555  he will catch me all the same, and butcher me for the coward I am.
 556  But if I leave these men to be driven before him —
 557  before Achilles, Peleus's son — and run on my own feet
 558  another way, off from the wall, toward the Ileian plain,
 559  until I reach the spurs of Ida and get into the brush —
 560  then at evening, when I have bathed in the river
 561  and cooled the sweat from me, I could come back to Ilion...
 562  But why does my own heart argue these things with me?
 563  What if he sees me swinging wide of the city toward the plain,
 564  and comes after at a sprint, and runs me down on his quick feet?
 565  Then there will be no more escaping death and the death-spirits:
 566  he is too strong — strong beyond all mankind.
 567  But what if, in front of the city, I stand and face him?
 568  His flesh too, surely, can be cut by the sharp bronze;
 569  in him is one life, no more; and mortal, men say
 570  he is — though Zeus, Cronos's son, keeps handing him the glory."
 571  So speaking, braced and gathered, he waited for Achilles,[30]
 572  and the brave heart in him strained toward war and battle.
 573  As a leopard comes out of deep brush
 574  to face the huntsman, and her heart neither
 575  fears nor thinks of flight when she hears the baying —
 576  for even if he is quicker, and stabs or hits her with a cast,
 577  still, run through on the very spear, she does not leave off
 578  her valor, until she closes with him or goes down:
 579  so the son of proud Antenor, brilliant Agenor,
 580  refused to run before he had put Achilles to the test.
 581  He held the balanced round shield out before him,
 582  aimed at him with his spear, and shouted loud:
 583  "No doubt you hoped in your heart, shining Achilles,
 584  to sack the city of the proud Trojans this very day.
 585  Baby fool — there is grief yet to be suffered over her:
 586  inside her we are many, and men of valor,
 587  who will guard Ilion, standing in front of our dear parents
 588  and wives and sons. It is you who will meet your doom here —
 589  terrifying as you are, and so daring a fighter."
 590  He spoke, and let the sharp javelin fly from his heavy hand,
 591  and hit him on the shin below the knee — no miss:
 592  and around it the greave of new-forged tin
 593  screamed terribly; and the bronze sprang back
 594  from where it struck, and did not pass: the god's gifts held it.
 595  And the son of Peleus went for godlike Agenor
 596  in his turn — but Apollo would not let him win the glory:
 597  he snatched Agenor away, and hid him in thick mist,
 598  and sent him quietly out of the war, to go his way.
 599  Then by a trick he kept the son of Peleus from the army:[31]
 600  likened in every point to Agenor himself, the worker-from-afar
 601  stood before his feet — and Achilles ran hot after him.
 602  And while he chased him across the wheat-bearing plain,
 603  turning him along the deep-eddying river Scamander
 604  as he ran just a little ahead — for by that cunning Apollo
 605  kept him beguiled, always hoping to catch him on his quick feet —
 606  meanwhile the rest of the routed Trojans, in a mass,
 607  came glad to the city, and the town was filled with their crowding.
 608  Nor did they dare wait for one another any longer
 609  outside the city and the wall, to learn who had escaped
 610  and who had died in the fighting: in they poured, headlong,
 611  every man whose feet and knees could save him.

NOTES

[1] (21.1-16) The opening repeats 14.433-434 exactly (our
    English kept): "the ford of the fair-flowing river, eddying
    Xanthus, whom immortal Zeus begot" — the crossing where the
    stunned Hector was laid and revived in Book 14. The river
    of Trojan refuge becomes the killing-pen: Achilles splits
    the rout, Hera walls one half behind mist, and the other
    half goes into the water. The locust simile knows what is
    coming three hundred lines early: men driven into a river
    by fire is precisely how the book will end for the river
    himself.

[2] (21.17-33) The spear left leaning on the tamarisks: sword
    work — butcher's work — is what the moment wants. The
    dolphin simile makes the water itself predatory ground.
    Then the poem's coldest logistics: twelve young men taken
    alive "blood-price for the death of Patroclus" — the vow of
    18.336-337 collected on schedule, the victims led out
    "dazed, like fawns," bound with the belts of their own
    tunics. They will be paid out at 23.175-176. Between vow
    and payment the poem never lets them speak.

[3] (21.34-48) Lycaon's dossier — the fullest biography any
    minor victim receives, given precisely so we know the whole
    weight of what is about to be refused: the night raid, the
    fig-shoots cut for chariot-rails (a boy doing farm-work in
    an orchard war), the sale on Lemnos to Euneus son of Jason
    (the fleet's wine-merchant, 7.467-469), the ransom by
    Eëtion of Imbros, Arisbe, the escape home. "Eleven days he
    delighted his heart among his dear ones; and on the twelfth
    the god threw him once more into the hands of Achilles."
    The symmetry is exact and awful: eleven days of resurrection,
    and then the machinery resumes.

[4] (21.49-63) Achilles' soliloquy over the unarmed man plays
    the return as ghost-story: "the great-hearted Trojans I
    have killed will rise again out of the murky dark." The
    joke — spear as experiment: "so I may see... whether he
    will come back equally from down there" — is the aristeia's
    humor: death-dealing grown so routine it wants variety.
    Under it, the poem's physics: the sea did not hold him;
    "the life-giving earth... holds down even the strong" will.

[5] (21.64-96) The supplication tableau: one hand on the knees,
    one gripping the spear itself — clinging to the weapon to
    keep it out of his own body. Lycaon's claims are, in order:
    the suppliant-bond; table-fellowship ("at your table first
    I ate the grain of Demeter" — the captive fed by his captor
    becomes his guest); the ransom record (a hundred oxen paid,
    three hundred to free him); the fate-arithmetic of the
    twelfth dawn; and last, the desperate genealogical wedge —
    born of Laothoe, not Hecuba: "I am not from the same womb
    as Hector." He offers every category the heroic world
    honors. The answer voids the categories.

[6] (21.97-113) The refusal and the consolation, the poem's
    ethical summit. "Fool — offer me no ransom": the economy
    that ran Books 1-9 (and Lycaon's own first capture —
    "before Patroclus met his day of destiny, sparing Trojans
    was dearer to my heart") is formally closed. Then the turn
    from wrath to metaphysics — "So, friend, you die too"
    (ἀλλὰ φίλος θάνε καὶ σύ) — and the terms of the
    consolation: Patroclus was better, and died; "do you not
    see what I am — how beautiful, how big?" — and I die too:
    "a dawn, or an evening, or a midday." The killer comforts
    his victim with their equality before death, and means it;
    φίλος is not sarcasm but the only fellowship left to a man
    who has accepted his own sentence. No passage of the poem
    was more quoted in antiquity as the summary of the human
    condition.

[7] (21.114-135) Lycaon's assent: knees slack, spear released,
    both hands spread — a posture between surrender and
    embrace — and the sword at the collarbone. Then the
    anti-funeral: slung into the river with a taunt that
    negates each rite in turn — no mother's laying-out, no
    lament; instead fish licking the wound "uncaring" and
    darting up "under the black ripple to eat the white fat of
    Lycaon" (the body already imagined as carcass-meat). The
    closing insult is cultic: the river you honored "so long
    with slaughtered bulls, drowning single-hoofed horses
    alive in his eddies" (live-horse sacrifice to rivers is
    real archaic practice) will not save you. Aristophanes
    athetized 130-135 — and Didymus suspected Aristarchus
    agreed — but the lines are the hinge: this speech is what
    the river cannot forgive, and the next six hundred lines
    grow out of it.

[8] (21.136-160) Asteropaeus: grandson of the river Axius,
    eleven days at Troy (to Lycaon's twelve — the book counts
    its doomed newcomers), with Xanthus himself putting fury
    in his chest: the river's first counter-move is a champion
    of river-blood. The challenge-exchange is a diptych with
    Book 20's: where Aeneas gave sixteen generations, the
    Paeonian gives a river and a spear — "Axius, who sends the
    most beautiful water over the earth."

[9] (21.161-183) The double cast — "for he was a two-handed
    fighter" (περιδέξιος): one spear into the shield's gold,
    the other grazing Achilles' right elbow — the only blood
    Achilles sheds in the entire poem, and a river's grandson
    draws it. The duel then turns on the ash spear stuck in
    the bluff: three heaves, and on the fourth attempt —
    trying to snap the spear of Aeacus' line — the sword takes
    him. He dies at the belly like Polydorus, bowels on the
    ground, "gasping"; the register's darkness-formula closes
    his eyes.

[10] (21.184-199) The victory-boast is a theology lesson:
    "It is a hard thing to strive with the children of
    Cronos's mighty son — even for the son of a
    river." Zeus's line beats water's line; and the ladder
    runs up: "not even Achelous the king is his equal, not
    even the great strength of deep-flowing Ocean, from whom
    all rivers flow and every sea, and all the springs, and
    the deep wells." Zenodotus and Megaclides omitted line
    195 (Pausanias saw such texts), which makes Achelous —
    not Ocean — the greatest of waters: the two readings
    preserve the oldest Greek dispute about the primal water.
    The vulgate keeps Ocean as source of all things and
    Achelous as king of rivers; this translation keeps the
    vulgate.

[11] (21.200-210) The corpse in the shallows: "around him the
    eels and the fish were busy, tearing at the fat of his
    kidneys" — Lycaon's threatened fate enacted on the next
    body within eighty lines. Then seven Paeonians in a line
    — the plain catalogue-deaths that mark an aristeia
    running at full mechanical speed — until the river
    speaks.

[12] (21.211-232) The river's protest is the book's ecological
    center: "my lovely streams are crammed full of corpses,
    and I can no longer pour my current through to the bright
    sea... and you kill, and kill." The god of the landscape
    pleads not for Troy but for his own nature — a river's
    function is to flow, and slaughter has clogged it.
    Achilles' answer grants the venue-change and nothing
    else, and states the poem's remaining program in one
    line: "stand against Hector face to face, whether he
    breaks me or I break him." The river then rebukes Apollo
    with Zeus's sunset commission — the day-clause that has
    governed the poem since 11.194 — as if to say: someone
    must hold the line till dark, and the archer is idle.

[13] (21.233-271) The attack. The river fights as water
    fights: not blows but volume — the surge that shoves
    corpses ashore ("bellowing like a bull") while hiding
    the living in his eddies (the river as shepherd of his
    people, doing with water what Apollo does with mist);
    the wave on the shield; the uprooted elm that takes the
    whole bank with it and bridges the stream. Achilles —
    for the first time since Book 1 — runs. The eagle simile
    keeps his dignity ("strongest of winged things at once,
    and swiftest"); the irrigation simile takes it away
    magnificently: the cosmic terror measured by a gardener
    whose channel outruns him, mattock in hand — the most
    domestic image in the Iliad set against its most
    elemental combat, closed by the flattest of maxims:
    "gods are stronger than men."

[14] (21.272-283) The despair-prayer. Achilles wails (ᾤμωξεν)
    — the verb of the wounded — that no god will save him,
    and that "my own mother beguiled me with lies": the
    promised death "under the wall... by the swift arrows of
    Apollo" (exactly the death he will in fact receive,
    22.359-360 — the complaint certifies the prophecy). The
    horror is genre-specific: to drown is to die without a
    victor, without a grave-mound, without kleos — "like a
    swineherd's boy swept away by a gully he was crossing in
    a storm." The greatest of heroes measures his death
    against the smallest imaginable one; Odysseus, drowning,
    makes the same calculation (Od. 5.306-312).

[15] (21.284-297) The rescue is pure dramaturgy: Poseidon and
    Athena "likened to men," taking his hand — the pledge-
    gesture — and dictating the remainder of the epic: not
    fated to die by water; pen the Trojans; kill Hector;
    return to the ships; "we give you the glory to carry
    off." (Seleucus athetized 290-292, and the Cretan
    edition omitted them.) The plot of Books 21-22 is
    handed over in a handclasp.

[16] (21.298-327) Scamander summons Simoïs — "dear brother"
    — for the war of the waters: springs opened, gullies
    roused, "a huge clamor of timbers and stones." The
    threat-speech is the book's dark masterpiece: armor
    "deep beneath the mere... wrapped in slime," the man
    rolled in sand under "shingle past counting," and the
    perfect inversion of epic burial: "the Achaeans will not
    know where to gather his bones... There his burial-mound
    will stand ready-made: he will need no heaping of a tomb."
    Book 23 will build Patroclus (and eventually Achilles) the
    greatest funeral in poetry; the river offers its exact
    negation — burial as disappearance, the tomb no one can
    find. For a hero, that is not death but erasure.

[17] (21.328-341) Hera's counter-stroke calls the match-card
    of 20.73-74 by name: "it was against you we always
    reckoned eddying Xanthus matched in battle." Her plan is
    a coordinated burn: Hephaestus on the trees and the
    river, herself rousing Zephyrus and the white South Wind
    from the sea to drive the flame onto the Trojans. Her
    warning — "do not let him turn you back at all with soft
    words or with threats" — repeats Apollo's coaching of
    Aeneas (20.108-109): even gods need to be told not to
    listen.

[18] (21.342-355) Fire eats the book's own scenery. First the
    corpses — the dead of Achilles' killing get from
    Hephaestus the cremation the river denied them: the
    aristeia's litter burned off the plain. The drying simile
    answers the irrigation simile: there, water outran its
    gardener; here, the North Wind dries the new-watered
    orchard "and the man who tends it is glad." Then the
    riverside flora burns by name — elms, willows, tamarisks
    (where the spear leaned at line 18), lotus, rushes,
    galingale — and the eels and fish that ate Asteropaeus
    tumble in the boil. The book's justice is elemental and
    complete.

[19] (21.356-382) The surrender: "no one of the gods can
    stand against you... What are strife and rescue to me?"
    — the god of the landscape resigning the war effort
    mid-sentence. The cauldron simile is the poem at its
    most audaciously domestic: a god's agony rendered as
    kitchen-work, "melting down the lard of a fatted hog...
    with dry split wood beneath." And the oath he offers
    Hera is her own: 374-376 repeat, word for word, the
    Troy-burning clause she recited at 20.315-317 (renderings
    kept identical) — the river buys his life by swearing
    the destruction of the city he rose to save. Hera's
    stand-down order states the Theomachy's official rule —
    "not seemly to batter an immortal god for the sake of
    mortals" — which every god then proceeds to break.

[20] (21.383-390) The gods fall on each other, "the wide
    earth rang, and the great heaven trumpeted" — and the
    audience is named: "Zeus heard, sitting on Olympus; and
    his own heart laughed for joy, when he saw the gods
    coming together in strife." This is the show he
    commissioned at 20.23 ("where I can watch and delight my
    mind"), now delivered. The gods cannot die; therefore
    their war is comedy, and the poem plays it as such — the
    laughter of Zeus is the genre-marker for the next
    hundred lines, while beneath it the mortal war goes on
    being fatal.

[21] (21.391-415) Ares vs Athena, the grudge-match of Book 5:
    his "dog-fly" and the memory of Diomedes' guided spear;
    her weapon a boundary-stone "which men of former times
    had set to be a field's boundary-marker" — the goddess
    of civic order felling the war-god with the marker of
    agreed limits (what Ares tramples, in every sense).
    Seven acres of fallen god, dusty hair, a laugh, and the
    taunt: pay "your mother's curses — the Erinyes she
    wishes on you" for changing sides. The register's
    formulas — "blood-stained Ares," "unstrung his limbs" —
    do their fixed work.

[22] (21.416-434) Aphrodite leading Ares off by the hand is
    Book 5 again (she led him wounded then too), and Hera's
    "there goes the dog-fly again" hands Athena her second
    knockdown: one blow to the breast, and the pair lie
    together "on the earth that feeds many." Athena's boast
    — had all Troy's helpers been so brave, "long ago we
    should have rested from the war, with the well-built
    citadel of Ilion sacked" — is the counterfactual that
    exposes the whole Theomachy: divine partisanship is what
    keeps the war long. Hera smiles; the smile is the scene's
    verdict.

[23] (21.435-460) The one serious challenge: Poseidon to
    Apollo, elder to younger ("Begin: you are the younger
    born" — dueling etiquette among immortals), grounded in
    the poem's deepest Trojan backstory: the year of bonded
    labor under Laomedon, Poseidon building the wall "wide,
    and very beautiful, so the city might be unbreakable"
    (the very fortification this war beats against), Apollo
    herding cattle on Ida; the wage refused, the threats —
    binding, sale to far islands, ears lopped with bronze.
    Troy's royal house cheated two gods and threatened them
    with mutilation and slavery: the city's doom has a
    contract-law history, and Poseidon's indignation — "it
    is his people you now favor!" — is the poem's last
    rehearsal of it.

[24] (21.461-469) Apollo's refusal is the great verdict on
    mankind from above: fighting a fellow-god "for the sake
    of mortals — pitiful things, who like the leaves at one
    time flourish in their blaze, eating the fruit of the
    plowland, and at another wither heartless away." This is
    Glaucus's leaf-simile (6.146-149) — there, a man's
    hard-won consolation ("as the generation of leaves, so
    the generations of men"); here, a god's reason for
    indifference. The same image carries humility from below
    and contempt from above: no two lines in the poem
    measure the distance between gods and men more exactly.
    He turns away out of respect — for his uncle, not for
    us. (471 and 475-477 were athetized by Aristarchus.)

[25] (21.470-496) Artemis pays for her brother's prudence:
    her taunt ("why do you carry that bow, useless as
    wind?") draws Hera — and the queen of heaven disciplines
    the huntress like an unruly girl: wrists caught in one
    hand, the bow stripped and used to box her ears,
    "smiling" as she does it, the arrows spilling. Hera's
    speech first defines her — "Zeus made you a lion to
    women, and gave you leave to kill whichever you choose"
    (Artemis as the sender of women's sudden deaths) — then
    demotes her: "better to slaughter beasts on the
    mountains than to fight by force with your betters." The
    dove-into-the-rock-cleft simile turns the archeress into
    her own quarry; the bow lies where it fell.

[26] (21.497-504) Hermes to Leto — the Theomachy's shortest
    match and its wittiest: an immediate, courteous forfeit
    ("it is a hard thing to trade blows with the wives of
    cloud-gatherer Zeus") with the winnings handed over in
    advance: "boast among the immortal gods that you
    overcame me with your strong force." Leto, unbloodied,
    gathers her daughter's scattered arrows — the one
    maternal gesture in a battle of gods.

[27] (21.505-513) The end of the divine war: Artemis in
    Zeus's lap, the ambrosial robe trembling, and the father
    "laughing sweetly" as he asks who hurt her "as if you
    had been caught doing wrong in the open." Her answer
    indicts the household: "Your wife battered me... from
    whom strife and quarrel cling to the immortals." The
    Theomachy that began with a cosmic quake ends as a
    family scene in the living room — which is the poem's
    point: for the deathless, war can only ever be domestic
    comedy. Below, meanwhile, it is Books 22-24.

[28] (21.514-543) Apollo alone stays at his post, entering
    Troy "lest the Danaans sack it that day, beyond its
    fate" — the god now guarding the same limit Zeus feared
    for at 20.30. The burning-city simile makes Achilles
    into the thing he is about to cause ("the wrath of the
    gods has sent it up" — he is that wrath); and Priam
    appears in his end-game role for the first time: the
    old man on the tower, ordering the gates held open in
    men's hands until the rout is in, then barred — "I
    dread that ruinous man — that he may leap inside the
    wall." The gates "spread wide, made a light of safety"
    (φάος — the register's word for rescue): the same doors
    that will stand between Hector and his life in Book 22.

[29] (21.544-570) Agenor — one man, stiffened by Apollo,
    between Achilles and the open gates. His soliloquy in
    the registered frame ("troubled, he spoke to his own
    great-hearted spirit") is the infantryman's complete
    decision-tree: flight with the crowd (caught and
    butchered "for the coward I am"); solo flight to Ida's
    brush — with the poem's most humane detail, the
    fugitive's daydream of an evening bath in the river,
    "cooled the sweat from me" — rejected on pure
    footspeed-arithmetic; then the discovery on which the
    rest of the poem stands: "His flesh too, surely, can be
    cut by the sharp bronze; in him is one life, no more;
    and mortal, men say he is." Every man who chooses to
    stand against Achilles must first say this catechism;
    Hector will say it, and its refutations, at 22.99-130.

[30] (21.571-598) The leopard simile — she comes out of
    deep brush at the huntsman and "run through on the very
    spear, she does not leave off her valor, until she
    closes with him or goes down": doomed courage as the
    poem's highest animal image, spent here on a secondary
    hero to set the standard Hector will be measured
    against. Agenor's cast hits the shin — "the greave of
    new-forged tin screamed terribly" — and Hephaestus's
    armor turns its first blow in combat exactly where
    Hephaestus promised marvel rather than protection
    (18.466-467): it gives both. Apollo snatches Agenor
    into mist before the counterstroke — the third man so
    saved from Achilles in two books.

[31] (21.599-611) The decoy: Apollo "likened in every point
    to Agenor" runs, and the fastest man alive chases a god
    across the wheat-bearing plain, "always hoping to catch
    him on his quick feet" — Achilles' one weapon against
    Hector, his speed, played against him to bleed the
    pursuit of its object. The trade is a city's remnant
    for one man's glory: while he chases nothing, Troy
    pours inside, "glad" — and the book's last lines are
    the rout's arithmetic, without a single name: no one
    dared wait "to learn who had escaped and who had died
    in the fighting: in they poured, headlong, every man
    whose feet and knees could save him." One man is not
    inside. Book 22 opens with Hector standing in front of
    the gates.
