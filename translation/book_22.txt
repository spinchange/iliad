BOOK 22

One man is left outside the wall. His father begs from the tower,
his mother bares her breast; he debates, runs, is run down past
the washing-places of the old peace; the scales dip, Athena lies,
the ash spear finds the throat above the stolen armor's collar —
and a woman heating bath-water learns why the tower is wailing.

TRANSLATOR'S COMMENTARY

Everything in the poem has narrowed to this: one man outside the
wall, "penned by deadly fate" at the Scaean Gates, and the whole
apparatus of the epic — parents, gods, landscape, army, similes —
arranged around the last hour of Hector. The book is built as
three concentric scenes: the pleading (Priam and Hecuba from the
tower); the running (three circuits of Troy); the killing and its
consequences — and at each stage the poem does the thing only it
would dare: it makes the terror specific. Priam does not simply
fear death; he sees his own house-dogs — "the dogs I fed at my
table, to keep my doors" — drinking his blood in the doorway, and
states the poem's cruelest law of decorum: a young man dead in
battle is seemly, "all things are beautiful on a dead young man";
it is the mauled grey head of an old one that is "the most
pitiful thing there is for wretched mortals" (note [3]). Hecuba
answers with the bared breast that once quieted him — the body's
own argument (note [4]). Neither works, because Hector's real
audience is neither parent: it is Polydamas ("who will be the
first to lay reproach on me"), and behind him the nameless "worse
man" of the streets. Shame, not courage, holds him at the gate —
his soliloquy admits it in so many words — until Achilles is
close, and then shame does what shame does under enough pressure:
it collapses, and the best man of Troy runs (notes [6], [8]).

The chase (136-213) is the greatest sustained passage of the poem,
and its power is in what the runners pass: the lookout, the wind-
blown fig, and the two springs of Scamander — one steaming, one
ice-cold — "and there beside them are the wide washing-troughs,
beautiful, of stone, where the Trojans' wives and their beautiful
daughters used to wash their glistening clothes — in the old
days, in peace, before the sons of the Achaeans came." Domestic
memory set flush against the fact: "they ran for the life of
Hector the horse-tamer" (note [9]). Zeus proposes rescue; Athena
answers him with the same rebuke Hera used over Sarpedon
(179-181 = 16.441-443, kept verbatim): the gods themselves have a
form-line for this argument, and it always ends the same way. The
dream-simile — pursuer who cannot catch, quarry who cannot pull
away — was athetized by Aristarchus and kept here (note [11]);
the golden scales dip; Apollo leaves; and Athena, wearing the
face and "unwearying voice" of the brother Hector loves, commits
the coldest act any god performs in the poem (note [13]).

The duel is short because it was never a duel: it is the
supplication theme played to its end. Hector offers a compact
about corpses; Achilles answers with wolves and lambs — "there
are no faithful oaths between lions and men" — and, when the
throat is pierced and the dying man begs again, with the wish he
could "hack your flesh away and eat it raw" (notes [15], [20]).
Hector dies prophesying — Paris and Phoebus Apollo at the Scaean
Gates — exactly as Patroclus died prophesying Hector's own death,
and the soul-flight couplet repeats 16.856-857 verbatim, as the
Greek does: the poem's two great deaths are one death, twice
(note [21]). What follows — the ankle-thongs, the dragging, the
head "that was once so beautiful" in its own country's dust — the
narrator marks with the plainest sentence available: "Zeus gave
him to his enemies to defile in his own fatherland."

And then the poem does what only the Iliad does: it leaves the
victor, and goes inside. Priam rolling in dung, already planning
the journey of Book 24 ("He too has a father like me — Peleus");
Hecuba's lament; and Andromache — who has heard nothing — at the
loom, weaving flowers into a purple cloak, ordering the cauldron
heated "for Hector, for his return out of the fighting. Poor
innocent." She reaches the wall in time to see the dragging, and
her fainting fling scatters the headdress Aphrodite gave her on
her wedding day — the marriage undone garment by garment — before
the great widow's lament, with the athetized orphan-passage (the
child at the feast, lips wetted, palate dry) defended in note
[31], and the last quiet horror: the fire she will feed with his
unused clothes, "no use to you: you will not lie in them." The
book ends on the women's answering wail — the sound Book 24 will
finally be allowed to finish.

   4  came up close under the wall, shields tilted on shoulders.

   8  "Why, son of Peleus, do you chase me on your quick feet —
   9  you a mortal, and I a deathless god? Not even yet
  10  have you known me for a god: how unrelenting your fury is!
  11  And the Trojans you routed — no thought for that labor now?
  12  They are penned in the town, while you swerve away out here.
  13  You cannot kill me: I am not a man marked out for death."
  14  Then, deeply troubled, swift-footed Achilles answered him:
  15  "You have robbed me, worker-from-afar — deadliest of all gods —
  16  turning me away from the wall. Many more of them
  17  would have bitten the earth before they got to Ilion!
  18  Now you have stolen great glory from me, and saved them
  19  cheaply: you had no vengeance to fear afterward.
  20  I would pay you back — I! — if only I had the power."
  21  So speaking, he set off for the city, high-hearted,[2]
  22  stretched out like a prize-winning horse with his chariot,
  23  one that runs at full reach, easily, over the plain:
  24  so Achilles worked his feet and knees, and they were quick.
  25  And it was old Priam whose eyes first made him out,
  26  streaking across the plain, blazing like the star
  27  that rises at harvest-time, and its rays shine clear
  28  among many stars in the milking-dark of night —
  29  the star men call by name the Dog of Orion.
  30  Brightest of all it is; and it is made an evil sign,
  31  and brings great fever down on wretched mortals:
  32  so shone the bronze on his chest as he ran.

  34  raising them high, and shouted down, crying aloud,
  35  begging his dear son. But he, before the gates,
  36  stood fast, hot beyond turning to fight Achilles;
  37  and the old man called to him piteously, hands outstretched:
  38  "Hector — do not wait for that man, dear child![3]
  39  Not alone, with no one by you — or you will meet your doom quickly,
  40  broken by the son of Peleus: for he is far the stronger.
  41  Merciless! I wish the gods loved him just as much
  42  as I do: quickly enough the dogs and vultures would eat him
  43  where he lay — and this terrible grief would leave my heart.
  44  He has emptied me of many sons, and good ones,
  45  killing them, or selling them off to far-lying islands.
  46  Even now there are two — Lycaon and Polydorus —
  47  whom I cannot see among the Trojans penned in the town,
  48  the sons Laothoe bore me, a queen among women.
  49  If they are alive somewhere in the camp, then we will
  50  ransom them with bronze and gold: it is there, inside —
  51  old Altes, the famous name, gave plenty with his daughter.
  52  But if they are dead already, and in the house of Hades,
  53  that is grief to my heart and their mother's — we who bore them.
  54  To the rest of the people the grief will be briefer —
  55  so long as you do not die too, broken by Achilles.
  56  Come inside the wall, my child — so you may save
  57  the men and women of Troy; do not hand the great glory
  58  to the son of Peleus, and be stripped of your own dear life.
  59  And pity me — the wretch, the ill-fated, who can still feel —
  60  whom the father, Cronos's son, on the threshold of old age
  61  will wither out in hard fate, with my eyes full of evils:
  62  sons destroyed, daughters dragged away captive,
  63  store-chambers ransacked, and little children
  64  dashed against the ground in the terrible slaughter,
  65  and my sons' wives dragged off under the deadly hands of Achaeans.
  66  And me last of all — at my own front door the dogs
  67  will tear me raw, when someone with the sharp bronze
  68  has stabbed or speared the life out of my limbs —
  69  the dogs I fed at my table, to keep my doors,
  70  who will drink my blood, and sprawl, their hearts run mad,
  71  in my own doorway. For a young man it is all seemly:
  72  killed in war, torn by the sharp bronze,
  73  to lie there — everything is beautiful on him, dead, whatever shows.
  74  But when the dogs defile the grey head and the grey beard
  75  and the naked shame of an old man slain,
  76  that is the most pitiful thing there is for wretched mortals."
  77  So the old man spoke, and pulled at his grey hair with his hands,
  78  tearing it from his head; but he could not move the heart of Hector.
  79  And his mother in her turn wailed, letting her tears fall,[4]
  80  loosening the fold of her gown; with the other hand she held out her breast,
  81  and letting her tears fall she spoke winged words:
  82  "Hector, my child — respect this. And pity me —
  83  me — if ever I gave you this breast, and it stilled your crying.
  84  Remember that, dear child. Fight that man of death
  85  from inside the wall — do not stand out there to meet him,
  86  merciless one! For if he kills you, I shall never
  87  mourn you laid on a bed, dear green shoot I bore —
  88  nor will your wife of the rich dowry — but far away from us both,
  89  by the ships of the Argives, the quick dogs will eat you."
  90  So the two of them, weeping, called to their dear son,
  91  begging over and over; but they could not move the heart of Hector.
  92  He stood fast, as gigantic Achilles came on nearer.
  93  And as a mountain snake at his hole waits for a man,[5]
  94  fed full of evil poisons, and the terrible anger has entered him,
  95  and he glares dreadfully, coiling around his hole:
  96  so Hector, holding his fury unquenchable, would not give way,
  97  his shining shield propped on the jut of the tower;
  98  and troubled, he spoke to his own great-hearted spirit:
  99  "Ah me! If I go inside the gates and the wall,[6]
 100  Polydamas will be the first to lay reproach on me —
 101  he who commanded me to lead the Trojans back to the city
 102  on that ruinous night, when brilliant Achilles rose.
 103  I would not listen. It would have been far better if I had.
 104  Now — since I have destroyed the army by my own recklessness —
 105  I am ashamed before the Trojan men and the trailing-robed
 106  Trojan women: that some worse man than I may say:
 107  'Hector trusted his own strength, and destroyed the army.'
 108  So they will talk. And then it would be far better for me
 109  to meet Achilles man to man, and kill him, and come home —
 110  or die at his hands myself, with honor, in front of the city.
 111  Or what if — if I set down the bossed shield
 112  and the heavy helmet, and lean the spear on the wall,
 113  and go out, just myself, to meet blameless Achilles,
 114  and promise him Helen, and all her treasure with her —
 115  everything, all that Alexandros in his hollow ships
 116  brought home to Troy, the beginning of the quarrel —
 117  to give to the sons of Atreus to lead away; and for the Achaeans,
 118  to divide with them everything else this city holds;
 119  and after, I would take the elders' oath for the Trojans
 120  to hide nothing, but to split it all in two —
 121  all the wealth the lovely town keeps locked within it —
 122  but why does my own heart argue these things with me?
 123  I might come to him, suppliant — and he would show no pity,
 124  no respect at all: he would kill me naked as I stood,
 125  just like a woman, once I had put off my armor.
 126  There is no way now, from an oak or from a rock,
 127  to murmur with him softly — the way a girl and a young man,
 128  a girl and a young man, murmur softly with each other.
 129  Better to drive together in strife — and quickly.
 130  Let us see to which of us the Olympian hands the triumph."
 131  So he weighed it, waiting. And Achilles came near him,[7]
 132  the equal of Enyalius, the war-god of the flashing helm,
 133  shaking the Pelian ash over his right shoulder,
 134  terrible; and the bronze around him flashed with a light
 135  like blazing fire, or the sun as it rises.
 136  And trembling took Hector when he saw him. He could bear it[8]
 137  no longer there: he left the gates behind, and ran in terror;
 138  and the son of Peleus sprang after, trusting his quick feet.
 139  As a falcon in the mountains, lightest of winged things,
 140  swoops easily after a trembling dove:
 141  she flees away under him, and he close upon her, shrieking
 142  sharp, lunges again and again, and his heart drives him to take her:
 143  so Achilles flew straight for him, hot; and Hector fled
 144  under the wall of the Trojans, working his knees fast.
 145  Past the lookout-place, past the wind-blown wild fig,[9]
 146  always out from under the wall, along the wagon-road
 147  they sped; and they came to the two fair-flowing springs,
 148  where the twin sources of eddying Scamander leap up.
 149  One flows with warm water, and steam all around it
 150  rises off it, as if from a burning fire;
 151  the other runs out even in summer as cold as hail
 152  or freezing snow, or ice that forms from water.
 153  And there beside them are the wide washing-troughs,
 154  beautiful, of stone, where their glistening clothes
 155  were washed by the Trojans' wives and their beautiful daughters —
 156  in the old days, in peace: before the sons of the Achaeans came.
 157  There the two of them ran past, one fleeing, one behind pursuing:
 158  a brave man fled in front — but a far better one pursued him —
 159  fast: since it was no beast of sacrifice, no ox-hide,
 160  they strove for — the prizes given for men's running:
 161  they ran for the life of Hector, the tamer of horses.
 162  As when prize-winning single-hoofed horses wheel round the turn-posts
 163  at full gallop, and a great prize is set out —
 164  a tripod, or a woman — in the games for a man who has died:
 165  so the two of them whirled three times around Priam's city
 166  on their quick feet. And all the gods looked on.
 167  And the father of men and gods began speaking among them:[10]
 168  "Shame — a man that I love, chased around the wall
 169  before my own eyes! My heart is grieving for Hector,
 170  who has burned the thigh-cuts of many oxen for me
 171  on the peaks of folded Ida, and other times again
 172  on the city's height; and now brilliant Achilles
 173  hunts him on quick feet around the city of Priam.
 174  Come, gods — consider it, take counsel:
 175  shall we save him from death? Or now, at last,
 176  break him — brave though he is — under Achilles, Peleus's son?"
 177  Then answered him the goddess grey-eyed Athena:
 178  "Father of the bright bolt, of the black cloud — what a word!
 179  A man — a mortal, long since doomed to his fate —
 180  and you mean to release him back out of harsh-sounding death?
 181  Do it. But we other gods will not all approve you."
 182  Then in answer spoke to her cloud-gatherer Zeus:
 183  "Take heart, Tritogeneia, dear child. I do not speak
 184  in full earnest, and to you I mean to be kind.
 185  Do as your purpose is. Hold back no longer."
 186  So speaking, he urged on Athena, eager already,
 187  and she went darting down from the peaks of Olympus.
 188  And swift Achilles pressed Hector without pause, driving him.[11]
 189  As when a hound in the mountains starts the fawn of a deer
 190  out of its bed, and courses it through glens and gorges;
 191  and even if it goes to ground, cowering under a thicket,
 192  the hound tracks it out, running steadily, till he finds it:
 193  so Hector could not shake the swift-footed son of Peleus.
 194  As often as he set himself to dash for the Dardanian gates,
 195  to get in under the strong-built towers,
 196  hoping the men above might defend him with their missiles,
 197  so often Achilles headed him off and turned him back
 198  toward the plain, keeping himself always on the city side.
 199  And as in a dream a man cannot catch one who runs —
 200  the one cannot escape him, nor the other catch —
 201  so he could not run him down, nor the other get away.
 202  And how could Hector have slipped the death-spirits of death,
 203  had not Apollo — for the last and latest time — come near him,
 204  to rouse his strength in him, and his quick knees?
 205  And brilliant Achilles shook his head to the army: no —
 206  he would not let them throw their bitter missiles at Hector,
 207  lest some thrower win the glory, and he come second.
 208  But when they came the fourth time round to the springs,[12]
 209  then it was that the father stretched out his golden scales,
 210  and set in them two death-spirits of death that lays men low —
 211  one for Achilles, one for Hector, tamer of horses —
 212  and grasping the middle he raised it. And Hector's day of doom
 213  sank down, and went to Hades; and Phoebus Apollo left him.
 214  And the goddess grey-eyed Athena came to the son of Peleus,[13]
 215  and standing close she spoke winged words:
 216  "Now at last, shining Achilles, dear to Zeus, I hope
 217  the two of us bring the Achaeans great glory at the ships,
 218  breaking Hector, however insatiable for battle.
 219  Now there is no escaping us any longer — not for him,
 220  not though Apollo the worker-from-afar should suffer agonies for it,
 221  rolling and rolling at the feet of father Zeus of the aegis.
 222  Now stop, and catch your breath. I will go
 223  and win this man over to fight you face to face."
 224  So spoke Athena; and he obeyed, and rejoiced at heart,
 225  and stood there, leaning on his bronze-bladed ash.
 226  And she left him, and came up with brilliant Hector,
 227  likened to Deiphobus in body and unwearying voice;
 228  and standing close she spoke winged words:
 229  "Old friend — how hard swift Achilles is pressing you,
 230  hunting you on quick feet around the city of Priam!
 231  Come — let us make our stand, and hold him off together."
 232  Then answered her great Hector of the flashing helmet:
 233  "Deiphobus — you were always dearest to me before this
 234  of all the brothers Hecuba and Priam bore;
 235  and now I mean to honor you in my heart still more,
 236  who dared for my sake, when your eyes saw me,
 237  to come outside the wall — while the others stay within."
 238  Then answered him the goddess grey-eyed Athena:
 239  "Old friend — yes: father and lady mother begged me hard,
 240  each in turn, clasping my knees, and my companions around them,
 241  to stay inside — so hard they are all trembling.
 242  But the heart in me was worn away with grieving.
 243  Now — straight at him, and let us fight; and no
 244  sparing of spears: so we may learn whether Achilles
 245  kills us both and carries our bloody armor
 246  to the hollow ships — or is broken by your spear."
 247  So spoke Athena, and led him on by her cunning.
 248  And when they had come close, advancing on each other,[14]
 249  the first to speak was great Hector of the flashing helmet:
 250  "No more running from you, son of Peleus — not now, as before,
 251  when I fled three times round the great city of Priam, and dared not
 252  stand to your coming. Now my heart has risen and set me
 253  to stand against you face to face. I will kill, or be killed.
 254  But come — let us swear here by the gods: for they
 255  will be the best witnesses, and the guardians of covenants.
 256  I will not defile you — not monstrously — if Zeus grants me
 257  the endurance, and I take your life:
 258  no, when I have stripped your famed armor, Achilles,
 259  I will give your body back to the Achaeans. And you do the same."
 260  Then, glaring darkly, swift-footed Achilles answered him:[15]
 261  "Hector — you past forgiving — talk to me of no covenants.
 262  As between lions and men there are no faithful oaths,
 263  and wolves and lambs do not carry hearts in concord,
 264  but think evil against each other all the way through:
 265  so there is no way for me and you to be friends, and between us
 266  there will be no oaths — not before one or the other falls
 267  and gluts with his blood Ares, the fighter under the ox-hide shield.
 268  Remember every kind of valor you own: now above all hours
 269  you must be a spearman, and a daring fighter.
 270  There is no way out for you any longer: this instant Pallas Athena
 271  breaks you under my spear. Now, in one payment, you settle
 272  all my grief for my companions — the men you killed, raging with your spear."
 273  He spoke, and poised and hurled his long-shadowed spear.[16]
 274  But shining Hector was watching it, and dodged:
 275  he saw it coming and crouched, and the bronze spear flew over
 276  and stuck in the earth. And Pallas Athena snatched it up
 277  and gave it back to Achilles — hidden from Hector, shepherd of the people.
 278  And Hector called across to the blameless son of Peleus:
 279  "You missed! So after all, godlike Achilles, you never
 280  learned my doom from Zeus — though you claimed to know it.
 281  You are a glib one, a very thief with your words —
 282  hoping I would forget my fury and valor for fear of you.
 283  You will plant no spear in my back as I run:
 284  as I charge you straight on, drive it through my chest —
 285  if the god has given you that. Now dodge my bronze
 286  in your turn — and may you carry all of it away in your flesh!
 287  The war would be lighter for the Trojans
 288  with you destroyed. You are their greatest bane."
 289  He spoke, and poised and hurled his long-shadowed spear,
 290  and hit the shield of the son of Peleus, mid-center — no miss —
 291  but the spear glanced far off the shield. And Hector was angry
 292  that the swift cast had left his hand for nothing,
 293  and stood there hanging his head: he had no second ash spear.
 294  He shouted then, calling to white-shielded Deiphobus —
 295  a long spear was what he asked — but Deiphobus was nowhere near him.
 296  And Hector understood inside himself, and said:[17]
 297  "So. The gods have called me deathward after all.
 298  I thought the hero Deiphobus stood beside me;
 299  but he is inside the wall, and Athena has tricked me.
 300  Now evil death is close to me — not far off now,
 301  and no way out. So this, long since, was the true pleasure
 302  of Zeus, and of Zeus's son who strikes from afar, who before
 303  defended me gladly. And now fate overtakes me.
 304  Then let me not die without a fight, without glory —
 305  no: having done some great thing first, for men to come to hear of."
 306  So speaking, he drew the sharp sword[18]
 307  that hung along his flank, heavy and huge,
 308  gathered himself, and swooped — like the high-flying eagle
 309  that drops to the plain through the lowering clouds
 310  to snatch a soft lamb or a cowering hare:
 311  so Hector swooped, shaking his sharp sword.
 312  And Achilles charged to meet him, his heart crammed full
 313  of savage fury; over his chest he held the cover of his shield,
 314  beautiful, intricate; and he nodded with the shining helmet,
 315  four-horned; and around it tossed the beautiful plumes
 316  of gold that Hephaestus had set thick along the crest.
 317  And as a star moves among stars in the milking-dark of night —
 318  Hesperus, the most beautiful star that stands in heaven —
 319  so the light went from the keen point that Achilles
 320  balanced in his right hand, purposing evil for brilliant Hector,
 321  searching the beautiful flesh for where it would yield the most.
 322  Now all the rest of his flesh the bronze armor held —[19]
 323  the beautiful armor he stripped from the might of Patroclus, killed —
 324  but there showed, where the collarbones part neck from shoulders,
 325  the gullet — where the destruction of a life is quickest.
 326  There, as he charged, brilliant Achilles drove with the spear,
 327  and the point went through and out the soft neck —
 328  yet the ash, heavy with bronze, did not sever the windpipe,
 329  so that he could still speak, and trade words with him.
 330  He crashed down in the dust; and brilliant Achilles cried in triumph:
 331  "Hector — no doubt you thought, while you stripped Patroclus,
 332  that you were safe; and I, far off — you gave me no thought.
 333  Fool: far from him, a helper — and a far greater one —
 334  was left behind at the hollow ships: I,
 335  who have unstrung your knees. You the dogs and the birds
 336  will drag and defile. Him the Achaeans will bury."
 337  Then, his strength nearly gone, Hector of the flashing helmet:[20]
 338  "I beg you — by your life, by your knees, by your parents:
 339  do not let the dogs devour me by the ships of the Achaeans.
 340  Take the bronze — take gold in plenty —
 341  the gifts my father and my lady mother will give you;
 342  and give my body back to go home, so that the Trojans
 343  and the Trojans' wives may grant me, dead, my portion of fire."
 344  Then, glaring darkly, swift-footed Achilles answered him:
 345  "Dog. Do not beg me by knees, or by parents.
 346  I only wish the fury and the heart in me would drive me
 347  to hack away your flesh and eat it raw myself — for what you have done —
 348  as surely as there is no man alive who will keep the dogs
 349  from your head: not if they bring here and weigh out
 350  ransom ten times over — twenty times — and promise more;
 351  not if Priam, Dardanus's descendant, bids them buy you
 352  for your own weight in gold — not even so shall your lady mother
 353  lay on a bed and mourn the son she bore.
 354  No: the dogs and the birds will share you out — all of you."
 355  Then, dying, Hector of the flashing helmet said to him:[21]
 356  "I know you now — looking at you. There was never a chance
 357  I would persuade you: the heart in your chest is iron.
 358  Only take care, now, that I not become the gods' curse
 359  upon you — on that day when Paris and Phoebus Apollo
 360  destroy you, brave as you are, at the Scaean Gates."
 361  Even as he spoke, the end that is death covered him;
 362  and his soul, flying from his limbs, went down to the house of Hades,
 363  wailing its fate, leaving its manhood and its youth.
 364  And brilliant Achilles spoke to him — dead already:
 365  "Die. My own death-spirit I will accept whenever
 366  Zeus wills to accomplish it, and the other immortal gods."
 367  He spoke, and pulled the bronze spear out of the corpse[22]
 368  and set it aside, and stripped the bloody armor
 369  from the shoulders. And the other sons of the Achaeans ran up around him,
 370  and gazed at the stature and the marvelous beauty
 371  of Hector; and not one man stood by him without a stab.
 372  And thus would one of them say, with a glance at his neighbor:
 373  "Well now — softer to the touch, this Hector, easier handling
 374  than when he burned the ships with blazing fire!"
 375  So a man would say — and step in close, and wound him.
 376  Now when swift-footed brilliant Achilles had stripped him,[23]
 377  he stood among the Achaeans and spoke winged words:
 378  "Friends — leaders and counselors of the Argives —
 379  since the gods have granted us the breaking of this man,
 380  who did more harm than all the rest together,
 381  come: let us circle the city under arms and probe it,
 382  and learn what mind the Trojans have in them now —
 383  whether they will abandon the high town, with this man fallen,
 384  or are set on holding it, though Hector is no more.
 385  But why does my own heart argue these things with me?
 386  By the ships lies a dead man — unwept, unburied —
 387  Patroclus. I will not forget him, not while I
 388  am among the living, and my own knees have their spring.
 389  And if in the house of Hades men forget their dead,
 390  even there I will remember my dear companion.
 391  Now, singing the paean, young men of the Achaeans,
 392  let us go back to the hollow ships, and take this man.
 393  We have won great glory: we have killed brilliant Hector —
 394  the man the Trojans prayed to through their city, as to a god."
 395  He spoke; and he devised foul work against brilliant Hector.[24]
 396  Behind both feet he pierced the tendons through,
 397  from heel to ankle, and threaded straps of oxhide,
 398  and bound them to his chariot, and left the head to drag;
 399  and lifting the famed armor into the car, he mounted
 400  and lashed the horses on; and they flew, nothing loath.
 401  And the dust rose off the dragged man; and his dark hair
 402  spread wide around him; and in the dust lay all that head
 403  that was so beautiful before; but now Zeus gave him
 404  to his enemies, to be defiled in his own fatherland.
 405  So all his head was fouled with dust. And now his mother[25]
 406  tore her hair, and flung the shining veil
 407  far from her, and screamed aloud at the sight of her child;
 408  and his dear father groaned piteously, and around them the people
 409  were seized with screaming and with wailing through the city.
 410  It was most like this — as if the whole of
 411  beetling Ilion, top to bottom, were smouldering in fire.
 412  The people could barely hold the old man, frantic
 413  in his grief to go out by the Dardanian gates.
 414  He begged them all, groveling in the dung,
 415  calling on each man by his name:
 416  "Let be, friends — for all your care of me, let me alone
 417  go out of the city and come to the ships of the Achaeans.
 418  I will beg this man — this reckless doer of violence —
 419  if somehow he may respect my years, and pity
 420  old age. For he too has a father — such a one as I am:
 421  Peleus, who begot him and reared him to be a bane
 422  for the Trojans — and beyond all men he has laid grief on me:
 423  so many of my sons he has killed in their green prime.
 424  Yet for all of them I do not mourn so much — grieved as I am —
 425  as for one, whose sharp loss will carry me down into Hades:
 426  Hector. I wish he had died in my arms!
 427  Then the two of us could have glutted ourselves with weeping and wailing —
 428  the mother who bore him — born to sorrow — and I myself."
 429  So he spoke, weeping; and the citizens groaned in answer.
 430  And among the women of Troy, Hecuba led the thick lament:[26]
 431  "My child — I am destroyed. Why should I live now, in this horror,
 432  with you dead? — you who were, night and day,
 433  my boast through the city, and a blessing to all,
 434  to the men and women of Troy through the town, who greeted you
 435  like a god: for you were their great glory too,
 436  while you lived. Now death and fate have overtaken you."
 437  So she spoke, weeping. But the wife of Hector had not yet[27]
 438  heard anything: no true messenger had come to her
 439  to tell her that her husband stood alone outside the gates.
 440  She was weaving at her loom, deep in the high house,
 441  a double cloak, purple, sprinkling in flowers of many colors;
 442  and she had called through the house to her lovely-braided handmaids
 443  to set a great tripod over the fire, so there would be
 444  a warm bath for Hector, for his return out of the fighting.
 445  Poor innocent: she did not know that far from all baths
 446  grey-eyed Athena had broken him under the hands of Achilles.
 447  Then she heard the shrieking and the wailing from the tower;
 448  and her limbs whirled where she stood, and the shuttle fell out of her hand,
 449  and she called again to her lovely-braided slave-women:
 450  "Here — two of you, come with me. I must see what has happened.
 451  I heard the voice of my honored mother-in-law, and in me
 452  my own heart leaps to my mouth, and beneath me my knees
 453  are frozen: something evil is close to the children of Priam.
 454  May the word stay far from my ear! — but I am terribly
 455  afraid that brilliant Achilles has cut off my bold Hector,
 456  alone, from the city, and driven him toward the plain,
 457  and has ended it — that grievous pride of his
 458  that always held him: he would never stay back in the crowd of men,
 459  but ran far out in front, yielding first place to no one."
 460  So speaking she rushed through the hall like a madwoman,[28]
 461  her heart slamming; and her maids went with her.
 462  But when she reached the tower and the crowd of men,
 463  she stopped on the wall, staring — and saw him:
 464  dragged in front of the city, and the quick horses
 465  dragging him, uncaring, toward the hollow ships of the Achaeans.
 466  Then black night came down and covered her eyes,
 467  and she toppled backward, and gasped her spirit out.
 468  And off her head she flung the shining head-dress —
 469  the frontlet, and the cap, and the woven band,
 470  and the veil that golden Aphrodite gave her
 471  on the day Hector of the flashing helmet led her
 472  out of Eëtion's house, when he had brought bride-gifts past counting.
 473  Around her, thick, stood her husband's sisters and his brothers' wives,
 474  and held her up among them, dazed to the point of death.
 475  But when she caught her breath, and the spirit gathered again in her chest,[29]
 476  with bursting sobs she spoke among the women of Troy:
 477  "Hector — I am wretched. To one fate, then, we were born,
 478  the two of us: you in Troy, in the house of Priam,
 479  and I in Thebe, under wooded Placus,
 480  in the house of Eëtion — who reared me when I was little,
 481  the doomed man, the ill-doomed girl. I wish he had never begotten me.
 482  Now you go down to the house of Hades under the hidden places
 483  of the earth, and you leave me in hateful mourning,
 484  a widow in your halls. And the boy is just a baby still,
 485  whom we bore, you and I — the two ill-fated. No help
 486  will you be to him, Hector — you are dead — nor he to you.
 487  For even if he escapes the Achaeans' war of many tears,[30]
 488  still there will be nothing for him ever after but labor
 489  and sorrows: other men will strip away his fields.
 490  The day of orphaning makes a child companionless:
 491  his head is bowed to everyone; his cheeks are wet with tears;
 492  and in his need the boy goes up to his father's friends,
 493  tugging at one man's cloak, another's tunic;
 494  and of those who pity him, one holds out his cup — a moment:
 495  he wets his lips; his palate he does not wet.
 496  And a boy with both parents living beats him from the feast,
 497  striking him with his fists, jeering at him with insults:
 498  'Get out — just as you are! No father of yours dines with us.'
 499  And in tears the boy goes up to his widowed mother —
 500  Astyanax: who before, on his own father's knees,
 501  would eat only marrow and the rich fat of sheep;
 502  and when sleep came over him, and he left off his child's play,
 503  he would slumber in a bed, in the arms of his nurse,
 504  in soft bedding, his heart brimful of good things.
 505  But now he will suffer and suffer, missing his dear father —
 506  Astyanax, 'the lord of the city,' as the Trojans call him
 507  by name: for you alone guarded their gates and their long walls.
 508  Now, by the beaked ships, far away from your parents,[31]
 509  the writhing worms will eat you, when the dogs have had
 510  their fill of you — naked: though here in your halls lies clothing,
 511  fine-woven and lovely, made by the hands of women.
 512  All of it — all — I will burn now in blazing fire:
 513  no use to you: you will not lie in it.
 514  Let it be your glory in the eyes of the men and women of Troy."
 515  So she spoke, weeping; and the women wailed in answer.

NOTES

[1] (22.1-20) The frame: Troy drinking and drying its sweat on
    the battlements — safety made physical — while one man
    stands outside, "shackled" by fate (πέδησεν: the poem's
    verb for the trap already sprung). Apollo's revelation ends
    the decoy-chase of 21.599-611, and his taunt — "you cannot
    kill me: I am not a man marked out for death" — states the
    book's whole geometry: gods are unkillable, so all the
    afternoon's fury must land on the one man who is not.
    Achilles' reply — "I would pay you back, if only I had the
    power" — is the closest any mortal in the poem comes to
    declaring war on heaven.

[2] (22.21-32) The Dog Star simile. Priam sees Achilles as
    Sirius — "brightest of all... and it is made an evil sign,
    and brings great fever down on wretched mortals": the most
    beautiful light in the sky, read as pestilence. It is the
    book's signature: brilliance and disaster in a single body,
    seen by an old man from a wall. (Achilles' own armor makes
    the same double sign at 134-135 — fire, or sunrise.)

[3] (22.38-76) Priam's plea moves from policy to the body. The
    center is Lycaon and Polydorus — "whom I cannot see among
    the Trojans penned in the town" — the audience knowing what
    the father does not: Book 20 and 21 have already answered
    him. The close is the most terrible passage spoken by any
    parent in the poem: the vision of his own scavenging — the
    table-dogs, "my door-keepers," drinking his blood at his own
    threshold — governed by the law he states with dreadful
    calm: on a young man killed in war "everything is beautiful,
    dead, whatever shows" (πάντα καλά); but dogs at the grey
    head and beard and "the naked shame of an old man slain —
    that is the most pitiful thing there is for wretched
    mortals." Tyrtaeus rebuilt the lines for Spartan war-song;
    here they are not exhortation but a father's terror.

[4] (22.79-89) Hecuba's argument is the breast itself —
    λαθικηδέα μαζόν, "the breast that stills care" — motherhood
    presented as evidence: "if ever I gave you this breast...
    remember that." Both parents end at the same image (the
    dogs, the unmourned bed) because both know what corpse-
    treatment this war has come to; the poem has spent four
    books teaching it.

[5] (22.93-98) The snake simile: coiled at his hole, "fed full
    of evil poisons," glaring — the only simile in the poem
    that makes a hero venomous. It renders exactly what shame
    has done to Hector: courage held not as flame (his usual
    fire-similes) but as toxin — swallowed anger waiting at a
    threshold it cannot abandon.

[6] (22.99-130) The soliloquy. Its logic deserves tracing:
    (1) Polydamas was right on the ruinous night (18.249-313 —
    "whose counsels were ruinous" said the narrator; Hector now
    says it of himself: "I have destroyed the army by my own
    recklessness"); (2) therefore the city's eyes — down to
    "some worse man than I" — are unbearable: shame (αἰδέομαι)
    holds him outside; (3) the fantasy of total surrender —
    Helen, her treasure, half the city's wealth under oath —
    rehearsed in loving legal detail and cut off by the fixed
    register line ("but why does my own heart argue these
    things with me?" = 21.562): he would be killed "naked...
    just like a woman"; (4) the strangest and most human turn
    in the poem — "There is no way now, from an oak or from a
    rock, to murmur with him softly — the way a girl and a
    young man, a girl and a young man, murmur softly with each
    other." The doubled phrase (the Greek repeats it too) is
    courtship remembered at the door of death: no more
    beginning-talk; only the ending. So: "Let us see to which
    of us the Olympian hands the triumph."

[7] (22.131-135) Achilles arrives as the war-god ("the equal
    of Enyalius"), the Pelian ash over his shoulder, bronze
    like "blazing fire, or the sun as it rises" — the Dog Star
    of 26-31 come to ground-level. The poem has spent two
    similes making him light; the third thing light does,
    after shining and warming, is blind.

[8] (22.136-144) "And trembling took Hector... he left the
    gates behind, and ran." The poem does not soften it: the
    snake bolts from its hole; the best man of Troy, having
    chosen to stand through ninety lines of deliberation, is
    undone in one look. The falcon-and-dove simile gives the
    chase its physics (the shriek, the repeated lunges) and
    its verdict: from here to the springs Hector is prey.

[9] (22.145-166) The run past the landmarks — lookout, wild
    fig, wagon-road — to the double springs of Scamander (one
    steaming, one ice-cold) and the stone washing-troughs
    "where the Trojans' wives and their beautiful daughters
    used to wash their glistening clothes — in the old days,
    in peace, before the sons of the Achaeans came." No
    passage in Homer does more with less: the whole lost
    civilian world folded into laundry, set at the exact spot
    where its defender is being run down. Then the narrator's
    cold accounting — "a brave man fled in front, but a far
    better one pursued" — and the great inversion of the
    games: they run as at funeral games, "a tripod, or a
    woman" for prize — "but they ran for the life of Hector,
    the tamer of horses." (Some ancient copies added a plus-
    line at 158; the apparatus records it.) Book 23 will run
    real races, for real tripods, around this dead man's
    pyre.

[10] (22.167-187) The divine council in miniature: Zeus's
    grief for a pious man ("who has burned the thigh-cuts of
    many oxen for me") and the proposal to save him — answered
    by Athena with the same three lines Hera used over
    Sarpedon (179-181 = 16.441-443, kept verbatim): a mortal
    "long since doomed" cannot be un-doomed without cosmic
    scandal; "Do it. But we other gods will not all approve
    you." The rescue-debate has become a form-letter, and
    both times the father yields — adding here, with terrible
    mildness, that he "does not speak in full earnest." Zeus's
    love is real; it is simply not the kind that saves.

[11] (22.188-207) Three similes of unclosable distance: the
    hound and the fawn (tracking "steadily, till he finds
    it"); Achilles heading Hector off from the Dardanian
    gates "keeping himself always on the city side" — the
    pursuit as sheep-dogging, the runner herded away from
    rescue; and the dream — "as in a dream a man cannot catch
    one who runs — the one cannot escape him, nor the other
    catch." Aristarchus athetized the dream-lines as
    undignified; they are the passage's genius — the one
    simile in Homer taken not from the world but from the
    experience of nightmare, where both runners labor and
    neither moves. Achilles' head-shake to the army (no
    missiles — "lest some thrower win the glory, and he come
    second") keeps the kill private: this death has an owner.

[12] (22.208-213) The scales. "Then the father stretched out
    his golden scales" — as at 8.69-74 for the armies, now
    for two men — "and Hector's day of doom sank down, and
    went to Hades; and Phoebus Apollo left him." Two clauses,
    no comment: the god who saved him at 20.443, who roused
    his knees at 22.204, departs like an advocate leaving a
    courtroom at verdict. Fate in the Iliad is exactly this
    scene: not a decree from above the gods, but the moment
    the gods stop arguing.

[13] (22.214-247) Athena's masterpiece of cruelty. To
    Achilles she is plain ("Now stop, and catch your breath.
    I will go and win this man over"); to Hector she is
    Deiphobus — the dearest brother, "in body and unwearying
    voice" — with a fabricated biography of devotion (the
    parents' clasped knees, "the heart in me was worn away
    with grieving") engineered to convert the fugitive's
    shame into hope. Hector's answer — "I mean to honor you
    in my heart still more" — is the sound of a man being
    loved back into a trap. The narrator closes the scene
    with the plainest possible verdict: "So spoke Athena,
    and led him on by her cunning" (κερδοσύνῃ). Nothing else
    any god does in the poem is this personal.

[14] (22.248-259) Hector's compact: he has stopped running —
    "I will kill, or be killed" — and offers the war's last
    treaty, with the gods as "witnesses and guardians of
    covenants": the winner strips the armor and returns the
    body. It is the decent minimum of heroic war, the norm
    the whole Patroclus-sequence has been shredding; and he
    offers it to the one man in the poem for whom it is now
    unthinkable.

[15] (22.260-272) The refusal, in natural law: "As between
    lions and men there are no faithful oaths, and wolves
    and lambs do not carry hearts in concord... there will
    be no oaths — not before one or the other falls and
    gluts with his blood Ares, the fighter under the ox-hide
    shield" (the register's phrase, 5.289 = 20.78 — now at
    its terminus). Achilles has left the covenant-species;
    he says so in genus terms. The taunt's ledger — "in one
    payment, you settle all my grief" — makes Hector the
    invoice for every companion dead since Book 16.

[16] (22.273-295) The exchange of casts, rigged: Achilles
    misses — and Athena returns the spear "hidden from
    Hector, shepherd of the people" (the title never crueler
    than here, applied to the deceived). Hector's speech at
    the false parity — "You missed!... You will plant no
    spear in my back as I run: drive it through my chest as
    I charge" — is magnificent precisely because it is
    obsolete: he thinks he is in a duel; he is in an
    execution with an intermission. His own cast hits
    mid-shield, true — against the shield of Hephaestus.
    Then: "he had no second ash spear," and Deiphobus "was
    nowhere near him."

[17] (22.296-305) The recognition. "So. The gods have called
    me deathward... Athena has tricked me. Now evil death is
    close to me — not far off now, and no way out." Four
    lines from deception to acceptance, and then the
    sentence on which Hector's whole meaning rests: "Then
    let me not die without a fight, without glory — no:
    having done some great thing first, for men to come to
    hear of" (ἐσσομένοισι πυθέσθαι — for those-to-be to
    learn). Cheated by heaven, out of weapons, out of
    brothers, he reorganizes in four heartbeats around the
    only future left: the story. It is the clearest
    statement in Greek of what heroism is for.

[18] (22.306-321) The last charge: Hector's eagle-stoop with
    the sword (the predator-simile returned to him for his
    final act — he dies attacking); Achilles' counter-charge
    behind the shield, the four-horned helmet, and Hesperus
    — "the most beautiful star that stands in heaven" — as
    the light of the spear-point "purposing evil." The
    evening star: the day-clause of Zeus's plan, the sunset
    Hector was granted through, now rising as the gleam on
    the weapon that ends him.

[19] (22.322-330) The gap in the armor: Hector dies through
    Achilles' own stolen gear — "the beautiful armor he
    stripped from the might of Patroclus" — pierced at the
    one place it leaves open, the collarbones' notch, "the
    gullet — where the destruction of a life is quickest."
    The borrowed-armor chain (16.793-804; Zeus's comment at
    17.198-208) closes with precise irony: Patroclus's
    disguise becomes Hector's coffin, and its known gap the
    door. The spear spares the windpipe "so that he could
    still speak" — the poem granting itself the dying
    dialogue it needs, and saying so.

[20] (22.331-354) Triumph and plea. Achilles' first speech
    is the ledger (you thought you were safe; "I... have
    unstrung your knees"), with the sentence-pair the whole
    corpse-war rests on: "You the dogs and the birds will
    drag and defile. Him the Achaeans will bury." Hector,
    "his strength nearly gone," begs by life, knees,
    parents — the full suppliant formula — for burial at
    any price; and the answer passes beyond the human: "I
    only wish the fury and the heart in me would drive me
    to hack away your flesh and eat it raw myself." Not
    even Zeus's scale of ransom — Hector's weight in gold —
    would move him. The wish to eat the enemy is the
    aristeia's terminal reading: Achilles has argued
    himself, by grief, to the edge of what the poem's world
    can call a man. Book 24 exists because the poem cannot
    leave him there.

[21] (22.355-366) The dying prophecy and the double death.
    "I know you now... the heart in your chest is iron" —
    then the curse-warning: "that I not become the gods'
    wrath (μήνιμα) upon you — on that day when Paris and
    Phoebus Apollo destroy you, brave as you are, at the
    Scaean Gates" — naming the god, the man, and the place
    (Xanthus's "a god and a man," 19.416-417, now
    specified; the gates are the very ones behind him).
    Then 361-363 repeat 16.855-857 verbatim, as the Greek
    repeats them — the soul-flight couplet, "wailing its
    fate, leaving its manhood and its youth": Patroclus and
    Hector go down through the same three lines, killer
    echoing killed. And Achilles answers the corpse with
    18.115-116, kept verbatim: "Die. My own death-spirit I
    will accept whenever Zeus wills to accomplish it" —
    the acceptance he spoke to his mother, restated over
    the man whose death guarantees his own.

[22] (22.367-375) The stabbing-circle: the Achaeans running
    up to admire "the stature and the marvelous beauty of
    Hector — and not one man stood by him without a stab."
    The anonymous soldier's joke — "softer to the touch,
    this Hector, than when he burned the ships" (line 375
    was athetized in antiquity) — is the war's ugliest
    line, and true to armies: the terror of months paid
    out on a body that can no longer object.

[23] (22.376-394) Achilles' speech breaks in half exactly
    where his mind does: strategy (probe the city — will it
    fall now?) collapsing mid-sentence into the register's
    self-interruption ("But why does my own heart argue
    these things with me?" = 122 = 21.562 — the third use
    binds victor to victim: Hector's phrase, in Achilles'
    mouth, within four hundred lines). What replaces
    strategy is Patroclus — "unwept, unburied... And if in
    the house of Hades men forget their dead, even there I
    will remember my dear companion" — memory outlasting
    death itself. Then the paean-couplet ("we have killed
    brilliant Hector — the man the Trojans prayed to...
    as to a god"), athetized by Aristarchus, kept here:
    the poem needs the army's song to set against what
    the next line does.

[24] (22.395-404) The outrage, in procedural detail: tendons
    pierced heel to ankle, oxhide straps, the head left to
    drag; the dust; "his dark hair spread wide around him;
    and in the dust lay all that head that was so beautiful
    before." The narrator's judgment is one clause wide and
    bottomless: "Zeus gave him to his enemies, to be
    defiled in his own fatherland" (ἑῇ ἐν πατρίδι γαίῃ) —
    the last three words doing what no exclamation could.
    ἀεικέα ἔργα, "foul work": the poem names the deed and
    does not look away; its whole final book will be the
    undoing of these ten lines.

[25] (22.405-429) Troy's grief begins with a simile of the
    end: the city's wailing "most like this — as if the
    whole of beetling Ilion, top to bottom, were smouldering
    in fire." Troy burns in the poem only here — in a
    comparison: with Hector dead, the fall is now a fact of
    grammar, needing no narration. Priam in the dung,
    begging to go out to the ships, announces Book 24
    entire: "I will beg this man... He too has a father —
    such a one as I am: Peleus." The wish — "I wish he had
    died in my arms! Then the two of us could have glutted
    ourselves with weeping" — makes grief itself the feast
    (κορεσσάμεθα: the verb of eating one's fill): the only
    satiety left to the old.

[26] (22.430-436) Hecuba's lament is civic: Hector as
    "boast" and "blessing," greeted "like a god" — the
    exact echo of the army's paean-line at 394, spoken now
    from the other wall: what the Achaeans sang as trophy,
    the mother sings as loss. Between the two uses, the
    poem holds its balance.

[27] (22.437-446) The most devastating dramatic irony in
    epic: Andromache "had not yet heard anything," weaving
    flowers into a purple cloak — making beauty — and
    ordering the cauldron heated "for Hector, for his
    return out of the fighting. Poor innocent (νηπίη): she
    did not know that far from all baths grey-eyed Athena
    had broken him under the hands of Achilles." The bath
    for the living that becomes the bath for the dead (the
    washing of corpses) hovers unspoken; the narrator's
    νηπίη — the word for doomed unknowing children and
    soldiers all poem long — is spent here on its most
    undeserving bearer.

[28] (22.447-474) The recognition sequence, staged in the
    body before the mind: the shuttle drops; the heart "to
    my mouth"; knees frozen; her exact guess about his
    "grievous pride... he would never stay back in the
    crowd of men" (she knew him better than his own
    soliloquy did); the run "like a madwoman"; the sight
    from the wall — the horses dragging him "uncaring"
    (ἀκηδέστως — the word for funeral-neglect); night on
    her eyes. Then the fall's one gesture: the head-dress
    flung off — frontlet, cap, woven band, "and the veil
    that golden Aphrodite gave her on the day Hector of
    the flashing helmet led her out of Eëtion's house."
    Widowhood performed as un-wedding: the marriage undone
    garment by garment in the sight of the city, at the
    news, before a word of lament is possible.

[29] (22.475-486) The lament's opening: "To one fate, then,
    we were born, the two of us" — Troy and Thebe, Priam's
    house and Eëtion's, joined in a single doom (her whole
    Book 6 biography compressed: the father who reared her
    "when I was little," dead at Achilles' hands with all
    her brothers — this is the second household Achilles
    has emptied for her). Its center is the child: "No
    help will you be to him, Hector — you are dead — nor
    he to you": mutual uselessness as the measure of
    death, between father and infant son.

[30] (22.487-507) The orphan-passage, athetized by
    Aristarchus (487-499) as beneath the moment — kept
    here, because its smallness is the point. Andromache's
    grief goes microscopically social: the seized fields;
    the bowed head and wet cheeks; the boy tugging at
    cloaks for charity; the friend's cup lent "a moment:
    he wets his lips; his palate he does not wet" — the
    most exactly observed line about charity in ancient
    poetry; the beating at the feast ("No father of yours
    dines with us"). Against it, the pampered past — the
    marrow, the sheep's fat, the nurse's arms — and the
    name that has become a taunt: Astyanax, "lord of the
    city... for you alone guarded their gates and their
    long walls." She is wrong about his future — the
    tradition kills him at the sack, thrown from these
    walls — and the poem lets her terrible modest fears
    stand as the mercy of not knowing worse.

[31] (22.508-515) The close: worms and dogs at the naked
    body "far away from your parents" — while at home the
    clothes lie "fine-woven and lovely, made by the hands
    of women." Her one remaining act is the fire: to burn
    the unused garments — "no use to you: you will not lie
    in it — let it be your glory (κλέος) in the eyes of the
    men and women of Troy." Kleos, the poem's grand word —
    what Achilles traded his life for, what Hector died
    reorganizing himself around — is here a widow burning
    shirts: glory reduced to, and rescued as, a household
    rite performed in the absence of a body. "So she spoke,
    weeping; and the women wailed in answer" — the same
    close as Briseis's lament (19.301-302, wording kept):
    the poem ends its greatest book on the sound it trusts
    most, the antiphony of grieving women — which is also
    how, two books from now, the whole Iliad will end.
