BOOK 3

The duel for Helen: Paris against Menelaus, the view from the wall,
and Aphrodite's rescue.

TRANSLATOR'S COMMENTARY

Book 3 reads like the war's first year transplanted into its tenth: a duel
between the two men the whole war is about, a truce, and Helen naming the
Achaean commanders for Priam as though he had never seen them in nine years
of siege. Ancient and modern critics alike noticed the chronological
strain; the poem plainly does not care, and the translation does not try to
smooth it. What the book actually does is introduce the war's cause and
cast in the most economical way possible, and it is built almost entirely
out of formal, ceremonial speech — challenge, herald's summons, oath,
prayer, curse. Those registers are rendered with deliberate stiffness (the
oath-language especially), because Book 9's embassy and later truce scenes
will reuse them; the conventions are logged in translation/CONVENTIONS.md.

One idiom is kept literal as a matter of policy: ὅρκια τάμνειν, "to cut
oaths" — from cutting the victims' throats — is translated "cut the sworn
oaths" throughout, not softened to "swear a treaty." The whole oath scene
(hair distributed to the chief men, wine poured as the curse names the
perjurers' spilled brains, the lambs' throats cut with the same knife) is
a single ritual gesture, and the verb carries it.

Paris is called both Ἀλέξανδρος and Πάρις by the poem, with Ἀλέξανδρος
predominating; the translation reproduces whichever name the Greek uses in
each line rather than normalizing to "Paris" (see note [2]). Helen's voice
is this book's discovery: her self-lacerating "dog-face that I am" (note
[13]), the elders' hushed astonishment, and the confrontation in which she
— alone of mortals in the Iliad — talks back to a god to her face and is
crushed into obedience (notes [22]-[24]). Aristarchus athetized both that
confrontation and her bitter speech to Paris; Zenodotus rewrote the line
in which Aphrodite carries her a chair. The ancient editors were visibly
uncomfortable with exactly the scenes a modern reader values most, and the
notes record each intervention while the translation keeps the vulgate
text intact.

The apparatus also preserves, at line 302, four "plus verses" found in a
Ptolemaic papyrus but absent from the medieval manuscripts — a live
specimen of the pre-Aristarchean fluidity of the text (note [19]).

   1  Now when they were marshaled, each company with its leaders,
   2  the Trojans came on with clamor and a cry, like birds —
   3  like the clamor of cranes that rises against the sky,
   4  when they flee the winter and the measureless rain
   5  and fly clamoring toward the streams of Ocean,
   6  bearing slaughter and doom to the Pygmy men,[1]
   7  and out of the morning air they carry their evil strife —
   8  but the Achaeans came on in silence, breathing fury,
   9  hearts set on standing by one another.
  10  As when the South Wind pours mist on the peaks of a mountain,
  11  no friend to shepherds, better than night to the thief,
  12  and a man sees only as far as a stone's throw —
  13  so the dust rose swirling under their feet
  14  as they came on; and very swiftly they crossed the plain.

  15  And when they were close, advancing on one another,
  16  godlike Alexandros stood out as the Trojans' champion,[2]
  17  with a leopard skin on his shoulders, and curved bow
  18  and sword; and shaking two spears headed with bronze[3]
  19  he challenged all the best of the Argives
  20  to fight him man to man in dread combat.
  21  But when Menelaus dear to Ares caught sight of him[4]
  22  coming out in front of the throng with long strides,
  23  then like a lion delighted to light on a great carcass,
  24  finding a horned stag or a wild goat
  25  when he is starving — he devours it greedily even though
  26  the swift dogs and the sturdy young hunters are driving at him —
  27  so Menelaus was delighted to see godlike Alexandros
  28  with his own eyes; for he thought to make the offender pay.
  29  At once he leapt from his chariot to the ground in his armor.
  30  But when godlike Alexandros saw him
  31  appear among the front fighters, his heart was struck cold,
  32  and he shrank back into the crowd of his companions, dodging death.
  33  As a man who has seen a snake in a mountain glen
  34  starts back and away, and trembling seizes his limbs,
  35  and he retreats, and pallor takes his cheeks,
  36  so back into the throng of the proud Trojans
  37  slipped godlike Alexandros, in dread of Atreus' son.
  38  And Hector saw him, and raked him with shaming words:
  39  "Paris — evil Paris — beauty and nothing more, woman-mad, seducer:
  40  I wish you had never been born, or had died unmarried.
  41  That is what I would wish, and it would be far better
  42  than to have you a disgrace like this, a thing others look at sideways.
  43  How the long-haired Achaeans must be cackling —
  44  they thought a prince was champion because a fine
  45  look is on him; but there is no strength in his heart, no fight.
  46  Were you really such a man when in the seagoing ships
  47  you sailed the deep with your trusted companions gathered,
  48  and mixed with strangers, and carried off a beautiful woman
  49  from a far land, a bride of spearmen —
  50  a great grief to your father, to the city, and to all the people,
  51  a joy to our enemies, a hanging of your own head?
  52  And will you not stand up to Menelaus dear to Ares?
  53  You would find out what kind of man it is whose wife in her bloom you keep.
  54  The lyre would be no help to you then, nor the gifts of Aphrodite,
  55  that hair and that fine look, when you went down in the dust.
  56  The Trojans are cowards indeed — otherwise long since
  57  you would have put on a shirt of stone for all the evil you have done."[5]
  58  And godlike Alexandros answered him:
  59  "Hector, you rake me justly, not more than justly.
  60  Your heart is always unwearying, like an axe
  61  driven through timber by a man's swing when he skillfully
  62  cuts out a ship-beam, and the iron strengthens his stroke:
  63  so unshaken is the spirit in your chest.
  64  But do not throw in my face the lovely gifts of golden Aphrodite.
  65  Not to be flung away are the gods' glorious gifts —
  66  whatever they themselves give; no man could take them by choosing.
  67  Now, if you want me to fight and do battle:
  68  make the other Trojans sit down, and all the Achaeans,
  69  and put me in the middle with Menelaus dear to Ares
  70  to fight for Helen and all her possessions.
  71  Whichever of us wins and proves the stronger,
  72  let him take all the wealth, and the woman, and lead them home;
  73  and the rest of you, cutting friendship and sworn oaths,
  74  may you live on in deep-soiled Troy, and they return
  75  to horse-pasturing Argos and Achaea of the beautiful women."
  76  So he spoke, and Hector rejoiced greatly to hear the word,
  77  and he went into the middle and held back the Trojan lines
  78  gripping his spear by the middle; and they all sat down.
  79  But the long-haired Achaeans kept shooting at him,
  80  aiming with arrows and casting stones,
  81  until the lord of men Agamemnon called out loud and long:
  82  "Hold, Argives! Do not shoot, young men of the Achaeans!
  83  Hector of the flashing helmet stands ready to say something."
  84  So he spoke, and they held from fighting and fell silent,
  85  quickly. And Hector spoke between the two armies:
  86  "Hear from me, Trojans and strong-greaved Achaeans,
  87  the word of Alexandros, for whose sake this quarrel rose.
  88  He bids the other Trojans and all the Achaeans
  89  lay down their fine armor on the earth that feeds so many,
  90  while he in the middle, with Menelaus dear to Ares,
  91  fights alone for Helen and all her possessions.
  92  Whichever of them wins and proves the stronger,
  93  let him take all the wealth, and the woman, and lead them home;
  94  and the rest of us — let us cut friendship and sworn oaths."
  95  So he spoke, and they all went still in silence.
  96  And among them spoke Menelaus, good at the war-cry:
  97  "Hear me now also. The pain of this comes closest
  98  to my heart; and I intend that they part at last,
  99  Argives and Trojans, since you have suffered many evils
 100  for the sake of my quarrel, and Alexandros who began it.
 101  For whichever of the two of us death and fate are prepared,
 102  let him die; and the rest of you part, and quickly.
 103  Bring two lambs — one white ram, one black ewe,
 104  for Earth and Sun; and we will bring another, for Zeus.[6]
 105  And lead down the strength of Priam, so he may cut the oaths
 106  himself, since his sons are overbearing and not to be trusted —
 107  that no one by transgression may do violence to the oaths of Zeus.
 108  The minds of younger men are always blowing in the wind;
 109  but where an old man is present, he looks before and behind
 110  at once, how the best may come of it for both sides."[7]
 111  So he spoke, and Achaeans and Trojans rejoiced,
 112  hoping to be done with the misery of war.
 113  They reined the chariots back into the lines, and stepped down,
 114  and stripped off their armor and laid it on the ground
 115  close together — there was little field between the armies.
 116  And Hector sent two heralds to the city
 117  quickly, to fetch the lambs and to summon Priam;
 118  and the lord Agamemnon sent Talthybius off
 119  to the hollow ships, and ordered him to bring
 120  a lamb; and he did not disobey brilliant Agamemnon.

 121  Now Iris went as messenger to white-armed Helen,
 122  in the likeness of her sister-in-law, the wife of Antenor's son —
 123  the one Antenor's son the lord Helicaon had married,
 124  Laodice, loveliest of the daughters of Priam.
 125  She found her in the hall: she was weaving a great web,
 126  a double cloak of purple,[8] and working into it the many struggles
 127  of the horse-taming Trojans and bronze-shirted Achaeans,
 128  which for her sake they suffered at the hands of Ares.
 129  Standing close, swift-footed Iris spoke:
 130  "Come here, dear bride, to see wonderful doings
 131  of the horse-taming Trojans and bronze-shirted Achaeans.
 132  They who before this carried tearful war at each other
 133  in the plain, ravenous for deadly battle —
 134  now they sit in silence, and the war has stopped;
 135  they lean on their shields, and the long spears are stuck in the earth.
 136  But Alexandros and Menelaus dear to Ares
 137  will fight for you with their long spears;
 138  and you will be called the dear wife of the man who wins."
 139  So speaking, the goddess put sweet longing into her heart
 140  for her first husband, and her city, and her father and mother.
 141  At once she veiled herself in shining white linen
 142  and went out of her chamber, letting fall a soft tear —
 143  not alone: two handmaids followed with her,
 144  Aethra, Pittheus' daughter,[9] and ox-eyed Clymene.
 145  And quickly they came to where the Scaean Gates stood.[10]

 146  Now those around Priam — Panthous and Thymoetes,
 147  Lampus and Clytius and Hicetaon, scion of Ares,
 148  and Ucalegon and Antenor, wise men both —
 149  sat as elders of the people at the Scaean Gates,
 150  finished with war now because of age, but speakers
 151  of quality, like cicadas that in the woods
 152  perch on a tree and send out their lily-thin voice:[11]
 153  such were the leaders of the Trojans sitting on the tower.
 154  And when they saw Helen coming up onto the tower,
 155  softly they spoke winged words to one another:
 156  "No shame that Trojans and strong-greaved Achaeans
 157  have suffered pain so long for a woman like this one.
 158  She is terribly like the immortal goddesses to look at.
 159  But even so — such as she is, let her go home in the ships,
 160  and not be left here, a grief to us and our children after."
 161  So they spoke; but Priam called Helen to him:[12]
 162  "Come here, dear child, and sit in front of me,
 163  to see your first husband and your kin and your friends —
 164  you are not to blame in my eyes: the gods are to blame,
 165  who set upon me this tearful war with the Achaeans —
 166  and to tell me the name of that gigantic man there,
 167  who that Achaean is, so tall and strong.
 168  There are others taller by a head, indeed;
 169  but a man so beautiful I have never yet seen with my eyes,
 170  nor so majestic. He looks like a king."
 171  And Helen answered him, brilliant among women:
 172  "You are dear to me, dear father-in-law, and dread to me too.
 173  How I wish vile death had pleased me on the day I followed
 174  your son here, leaving my bridal chamber and my kinsmen
 175  and my own dear daughter and the sweet company of girls my age.
 176  But that was not to be; and so I waste away with weeping.
 177  But I will tell you what you ask me and inquire.
 178  That man is Atreus' son, wide-ruling Agamemnon,
 179  both things at once: a good king and a strong spearman.
 180  And he was brother-in-law — to me, dog-face that I am[13] — if that ever was."
 181  So she spoke, and the old man marveled at him, and said:
 182  "Fortunate son of Atreus, child of destiny, blessed of fortune —
 183  how many young Achaean men are subject to you!
 184  Once I went into Phrygia of the vines,
 185  and there I saw Phrygian men in their multitudes, with their glancing horses,
 186  the hosts of Otreus and godlike Mygdon,
 187  encamped then along the banks of Sangarius;
 188  for I too, as an ally, was counted among them
 189  on the day the Amazons came, the match of men.[14]
 190  But even they were not so many as the bright-eyed Achaeans."
 191  Second, the old man saw Odysseus, and asked:
 192  "Come, tell me of this one too, dear child — who he is.
 193  Shorter by a head than Agamemnon, Atreus' son,
 194  but broader in the shoulders and chest to look at.
 195  His armor lies on the earth that feeds so many,
 196  and he himself ranges the ranks of men like a bellwether ram —
 197  yes, I liken him to a thick-fleeced ram
 198  that moves through a great flock of white ewes."
 199  And Helen, sprung of Zeus, answered him:
 200  "That is Laertes' son, resourceful Odysseus,
 201  raised in the land of Ithaca — rocky as it is —
 202  a man who knows every kind of trick and close-woven plan."
 203  And wise Antenor said to her in answer:
 204  "Lady, that word of yours is exactly true.
 205  For brilliant Odysseus came here once before,
 206  on an embassy about you,[15] with Menelaus dear to Ares;
 207  and I was their host: I welcomed them in my halls,
 208  and learned the build of both, and their close-woven plans.
 209  Now when they mixed with the assembled Trojans,
 210  standing, Menelaus overtopped him with his broad shoulders,
 211  but with both seated, Odysseus was the more majestic.
 212  And when they wove their words and their plans before us all,
 213  Menelaus, certainly, spoke fluently —
 214  few words, but very clear: he was no man of many words,
 215  no rambler — though he was in fact the younger.
 216  But whenever resourceful Odysseus rose to speak,
 217  he would just stand there, eyes fixed down on the ground,
 218  not moving the scepter backward or forward
 219  but holding it stiff, like a man who knows nothing;
 220  you would have called him some sulking churl, a plain fool.
 221  But when he let the great voice out of his chest,
 222  and the words came like snowflakes in winter,
 223  then no other mortal could rival Odysseus.
 224  Then we did not marvel so much at how Odysseus looked."
 225  Third, the old man saw Ajax, and asked:
 226  "And who is that other Achaean man, tall and strong,
 227  towering over the Argives in head and broad shoulders?"
 228  And Helen of the trailing robe answered, brilliant among women:
 229  "That is gigantic Ajax, wall of the Achaeans.
 230  And on the other side Idomeneus stands among the Cretans
 231  like a god, and the Cretan captains are gathered around him.
 232  Often Menelaus dear to Ares was his host
 233  in our house, whenever he came from Crete.
 234  And now I see all the other bright-eyed Achaeans,
 235  whom I could well recognize and name their names;
 236  but two marshals of the people I cannot see —
 237  Castor breaker of horses and Polydeuces the great boxer,
 238  my own brothers, whom one mother bore with me.
 239  Either they did not follow from lovely Lacedaemon,
 240  or they followed here in the seagoing ships
 241  but now are not willing to enter the battle of men,
 242  fearing the shame and the many reproaches that are mine."
 243  So she spoke. But the life-giving earth already held them under,[16]
 244  there in Lacedaemon, in their dear fatherland.

 245  Now the heralds were carrying through the city the gods' oath-offerings,
 246  two lambs, and wine that gladdens the heart, fruit of the field,
 247  in a goatskin bag; and the herald Idaeus carried
 248  a gleaming mixing-bowl and cups of gold;
 249  and he stood beside the old king and roused him:
 250  "Rise, son of Laomedon. The chief men are calling —
 251  of the horse-taming Trojans and the bronze-shirted Achaeans —
 252  for you to come down to the plain, to cut the sworn oaths.
 253  For Alexandros and Menelaus dear to Ares
 254  will fight with their long spears for the woman;
 255  and woman and wealth shall go with the winner,
 256  while the rest, cutting friendship and sworn oaths,
 257  we should live on in deep-soiled Troy, and they return
 258  to horse-pasturing Argos and Achaea of the beautiful women."
 259  So he spoke, and the old man shuddered; but he told his companions
 260  to yoke the horses, and they promptly obeyed.
 261  Priam mounted and drew the reins back taut,
 262  and beside him Antenor mounted the beautiful chariot,
 263  and through the Scaean Gates they drove the swift horses toward the plain.
 264  And when they had come among the Trojans and Achaeans,
 265  they stepped from the chariot onto the earth that feeds so many
 266  and walked into the space between Trojans and Achaeans.
 267  At once the lord of men Agamemnon rose,
 268  and resourceful Odysseus rose; and the proud heralds
 269  brought together the gods' oath-offerings, and in the bowl
 270  mixed the wine, and poured water over the kings' hands.
 271  And Atreus' son drew with his hand the knife
 272  that always hung beside the great sheath of his sword,
 273  and cut hairs from the lambs' heads; and then
 274  the heralds gave them out to the chief men of the Trojans and Achaeans.
 275  And Atreus' son prayed aloud among them, lifting his hands:
 276  "Father Zeus, ruling from Ida, most glorious, greatest;
 277  and Sun, who see all things and hear all things;
 278  and Rivers, and Earth, and you two below who punish
 279  the dead, whoever has sworn a false oath:[17]
 280  be witnesses; guard the sworn oaths.
 281  If Alexandros kills Menelaus,
 282  let him keep Helen and all her possessions,
 283  and we will go home in the seagoing ships;
 284  but if tawny-haired Menelaus kills Alexandros,
 285  then the Trojans are to give back Helen and all her possessions,
 286  and pay the Argives whatever recompense is fitting,
 287  such as will be remembered among men still to come.
 288  And if Priam and Priam's sons refuse
 289  to pay me that recompense when Alexandros has fallen,
 290  then I will fight on for the payment owed,
 291  staying right here, until I reach the end of the war."
 292  He spoke, and cut the lambs' throats with the pitiless bronze,
 293  and laid them down on the ground gasping,
 294  the life going out of them: the bronze had taken their strength.
 295  Then drawing wine from the bowl into the cups
 296  they poured it out, and prayed to the gods who are forever;
 297  and thus would one of the Achaeans and Trojans say:
 298  "Zeus most glorious, greatest, and you other immortal gods:
 299  whichever side first does harm against the oaths,
 300  may their brains run on the ground as this wine runs,
 301  theirs and their children's, and their wives be subdued to other men."[18]
 302  So they spoke; but the son of Cronos would not yet bring it to pass.[19]
 303  And Priam, Dardanus' descendant, spoke his word among them:
 304  "Hear from me, Trojans and strong-greaved Achaeans.
 305  I am going back to windswept Ilion,
 306  since I cannot yet bear to watch with my own eyes
 307  my dear son fighting Menelaus dear to Ares.
 308  Zeus, I suppose, knows — and the other immortal gods —
 309  for which of the two the end of death is fated."
 310  He spoke — a man like a god — and set the lambs in the chariot,
 311  and mounted himself, and drew the reins back taut,
 312  and beside him Antenor mounted the beautiful chariot;
 313  and back again to Ilion the two returned.

 314  Then Hector, Priam's son, and brilliant Odysseus
 315  first measured out the ground, and then
 316  took lots and shook them in a bronze-fitted helmet,
 317  for which of the two should cast his bronze spear first.
 318  And the hosts prayed, and lifted their hands to the gods,
 319  and thus would one of the Achaeans and Trojans say:
 320  "Father Zeus, ruling from Ida, most glorious, greatest:
 321  whichever of the two brought these troubles on both peoples,
 322  grant that he go down, dead, into the house of Hades,
 323  and that friendship and sworn oaths be ours."
 324  So they spoke; and great Hector of the flashing helmet shook the lots,
 325  looking away behind him; and Paris's lot leapt quickly out.
 326  Then the men sat down in ranks, where each man's
 327  high-stepping horses and patterned armor lay.
 328  And he girded the fine armor about his shoulders —[20]
 329  brilliant Alexandros, husband of lovely-haired Helen.
 330  First he set the greaves along his shins,
 331  beautiful ones, fitted with silver ankle-guards;
 332  next he girt about his chest the corslet
 333  of his brother Lycaon — and it fitted him;
 334  and about his shoulders he slung the silver-studded sword,
 335  a sword of bronze, and then the shield, great and heavy;
 336  and on his strong head he set the well-made helmet
 337  with its horsehair crest — and terribly the plume nodded above him —
 338  and took up a stout spear, fitted to his grip.
 339  And in the same way warlike Menelaus put on his gear.

 340  And when they had armed on either side of the throng,
 341  they strode into the space between Trojans and Achaeans,
 342  glaring terribly; and amazement held the watchers,
 343  the horse-taming Trojans and the strong-greaved Achaeans.
 344  They took their stand near each other on the measured ground,
 345  shaking their spears, in fury at one another.
 346  Alexandros first hurled his long-shadowed spear[21]
 347  and struck the shield of Atreus' son, balanced every way;
 348  but the bronze did not break through: its point was bent back
 349  in the strong shield. Second, Atreus' son Menelaus
 350  rose with his bronze, with a prayer to father Zeus:
 351  "Zeus, lord: grant me to punish the man who wronged me first —
 352  brilliant Alexandros — and break him under my hands,
 353  so that many a man may shudder, even among men yet to be born,
 354  at doing evil to a host who has offered him friendship."
 355  He spoke, and balancing his long-shadowed spear he hurled it
 356  and struck the shield of Priam's son, balanced every way.
 357  Through the shining shield the heavy spear went,
 358  and through the richly worked corslet it drove;
 359  and straight on beside his flank the spear sheared through
 360  the tunic — but he had swerved aside, and dodged black doom.
 361  Then Atreus' son drew the silver-studded sword,
 362  rose up, and struck the ridge of his helmet; but around it
 363  the sword shattered in three and four pieces, and fell from his hand.
 364  And Atreus' son groaned, looking into the wide sky:
 365  "Father Zeus, no other god is more destroying than you!
 366  I thought I had made Alexandros pay for his wickedness,
 367  and now the sword is broken in my hands, and the spear
 368  flew from my grip for nothing, and I have not hit him."
 369  He spoke — and springing on him he seized the horsehair helmet
 370  and swung round and dragged him toward the strong-greaved Achaeans;
 371  and the richly stitched strap was strangling the soft throat under it,
 372  the chin-strap drawn tight beneath his jaw.
 373  And now he would have dragged him off and won glory past telling,
 374  had not Aphrodite, Zeus's daughter, seen it sharply:
 375  she broke the strap — leather of an ox felled by force —
 376  and the helmet came away empty in his heavy hand.
 377  The hero sent it spinning away toward the strong-greaved Achaeans
 378  with a whirl, and his trusted companions gathered it up;
 379  and back again he sprang, raging to kill
 380  with his bronze spear. But Aphrodite snatched Paris away —
 381  easily, as a god can — and hid him in thick mist,
 382  and set him down in his fragrant, scented chamber.

 383  Then she went herself to summon Helen; she found her
 384  on the high tower, with the women of Troy thick around her.
 385  With her hand she plucked at her nectar-sweet robe and shook it,
 386  and spoke to her in the likeness of an old woman,
 387  a wool-comber of long ago, who used to work fine wool
 388  for her when she lived in Lacedaemon, and whom she dearly loved;
 389  in her likeness brilliant Aphrodite spoke:
 390  "Come here. Alexandros calls you home.
 391  There he is, in the bedchamber, on the turned bed,
 392  gleaming in his beauty and his clothes. You would not say
 393  he had come from single combat — rather that he was going
 394  to a dance, or sitting down just now come from dancing."
 395  So she spoke, and stirred the heart in Helen's breast;
 396  and when she noticed the surpassing beauty of the goddess's throat
 397  and the breasts of desire and the flashing eyes,
 398  awe took her,[22] and she spoke a word, and named her:
 399  "Strange goddess — why are you so set on cheating me like this?
 400  Will you carry me somewhere still farther on, to one of the fine
 401  cities of Phrygia or lovely Maeonia,
 402  if some mortal man is dear to you there too?
 403  Is it because Menelaus has beaten brilliant Alexandros
 404  and wants to lead hateful me home again —
 405  is that why you stand beside me now, weaving your tricks?
 406  Go sit beside him yourself. Renounce the paths of the gods;
 407  never again turn your feet back to Olympus,
 408  but fret over him forever, and guard him,
 409  until he makes you his wife — or else his slave.
 410  I will not go to him — that would be a thing for anger —
 411  to serve that man's bed. The women of Troy
 412  would all mock me afterward; and I have griefs past counting in my heart."
 413  And in anger brilliant Aphrodite answered her:
 414  "Do not provoke me, hard woman — or I may quit you in my anger
 415  and hate you as extravagantly as I now love you,
 416  and devise bitter hatreds between both sides,
 417  Trojans and Danaans alike — and you would perish miserably."
 418  So she spoke, and Helen sprung of Zeus was afraid,
 419  and she went in silence, wrapped in her shining white robe,
 420  unnoticed by all the Trojan women; and the goddess led.
 421  When they came to the beautiful house of Alexandros,
 422  the handmaids turned quickly to their work,
 423  and she went to the high-roofed bedchamber, brilliant among women.
 424  And laughter-loving Aphrodite[23] took a chair for her —
 425  a goddess! — carried it, and set it facing Alexandros;[24]
 426  there Helen sat, daughter of aegis-bearing Zeus,
 427  her eyes turned away, and she lashed her husband with her word:
 428  "You came back from the fight. How I wish you had died there,
 429  broken by the strong man who was my first husband.
 430  You used to boast, before, that you were the better man —
 431  stronger than Menelaus dear to Ares in force and hand and spear.
 432  Go on then, challenge Menelaus dear to Ares
 433  to fight you again face to face. — But no; I myself
 434  advise you to stop: do not fight tawny-haired Menelaus
 435  man against man, do not battle him,
 436  like a fool — or you may soon go down under his spear."[25]
 437  And Paris answered her with his word:
 438  "Woman, do not lash my heart with hard reproaches.
 439  This time Menelaus has won — with Athena's help;
 440  another time it will be I. There are gods on our side too.
 441  Come, let us take our joy in love, in bed together.
 442  Never has desire so wrapped itself around my heart —
 443  not even when I first carried you off from lovely Lacedaemon
 444  and sailed away in the seagoing ships,
 445  and on the island of Kranae[26] mixed with you in love and bed —
 446  as I love you now, and sweet longing takes hold of me."
 447  He spoke, and led the way to bed; and his wife followed.
 448  So the two lay together on the corded bed.

 449  But Atreus' son ranged through the throng like a wild beast,
 450  hunting for a glimpse of godlike Alexandros.
 451  And no one of the Trojans or their famed allies
 452  could show Alexandros to Menelaus dear to Ares;
 453  not out of friendship, surely, would they have hidden him, had anyone seen him:
 454  for he was hated by them all like black death.
 455  And among them spoke the lord of men Agamemnon:
 456  "Hear me, Trojans and Dardanians and allies.
 457  Victory clearly belongs to Menelaus dear to Ares.
 458  Give up, then, Argive Helen and the possessions with her,
 459  and pay whatever recompense is fitting,
 460  such as will be remembered among men still to come."
 461  So spoke Atreus' son, and the rest of the Achaeans gave assent.

Notes

[1] (3.3-7) The cranes migrating to Ocean's stream to war on the Pygmy
    men — the oldest surviving reference to the Pygmy legend. The simile's
    real work is the contrast it frames: the Trojans advance with clamor
    like birds, the Achaeans in disciplined silence (8-9) — a recurring
    characterization of the two armies.

[2] (3.16) "Alexandros" — the poem's usual name for Paris (Πάρις appears
    only occasionally, e.g. 325, 437). The translation reproduces
    whichever name the Greek uses in each line rather than normalizing.
    Hector's opening insult at 39, Δύσπαρι ("Dys-Paris," ill-Paris), is
    rendered "Paris — evil Paris."

[3] (3.18-20) A rare case of overlapping athetesis by both major
    Alexandrian editors: Zenodotus rejected 18-20, Aristarchus separately
    19-20 (apparatus). The bother was partly Paris's arsenal — leopard
    skin, bow, sword, and two spears is theatrical overload. Kept: the
    over-dressed entrance is exactly in character.

[4] (3.21) "dear to Ares" (ἀρηΐφιλος) — Menelaus's standing epithet in
    this book; his other is "tawny-haired" (ξανθός, 284). Both always so
    rendered.

[5] (3.57) "a shirt of stone" (λάϊνον χιτῶνα) — i.e. death by public
    stoning, the community's punishment for the offense Paris committed;
    the idiom is kept literal.

[6] (3.103-104) The victims are matched to the oath-witnesses: a white
    ram for the Sun, a black ewe for Earth, a third lamb from the
    Achaeans for Zeus. Priam must swear in person because oaths bind the
    swearer alone — and his sons are "not to be trusted" (106): the war
    began with Paris violating a solemn bond.

[7] (3.108-110) Aristarchus athetized these three generalizing lines on
    young men's flightiness (apparatus, Line 108-110). Kept; the
    sententiousness suits Menelaus's careful legalism in this speech.

[8] (3.125-128) Helen weaving the battles "which for her sake they
    suffered" — the poem's most self-referential image: she is weaving
    the subject-matter of the Iliad while living inside it. The
    Alexandrian editors read "a double cloak of purple" (πορφυρέην, as
    printed); most manuscripts have "shimmering" (μαρμαρέην) — the
    apparatus records the split (Line 126).

[9] (3.144) Aethra, Pittheus' daughter, is in myth the mother of Theseus,
    made Helen's slave when the Dioscuri sacked Athens's territory to
    recover the girl Theseus had abducted — a story alien to Homer, and
    ancient critics condemned the line as an Athenian interpolation
    (Aristarchus athetized it; apparatus, Line 144). Translated as
    printed; the name is either a fossil of that lost epic tradition or
    a coincidence ancient readers over-read.

[10] (3.145) The Scaean Gates: Troy's main gate facing the plain and the
     battlefield, the fixed stage-set for departures and watching in this
     poem (and, in tradition beyond it, the place of Achilles' death).

[11] (3.151-152) The old counselors chirp like cicadas with their "lily-
     thin voice" (ὄπα λειριόεσσαν — literally "lily-like," delicate as a
     petal): age has left them song without strength. One of the poem's
     most admired domestic similes.

[12] (3.161-244) The teichoscopia ("viewing from the wall"): Priam, in
     the war's tenth year, asks Helen to identify Agamemnon, Odysseus,
     and Ajax as though the enemy were new to him. Critics ancient and
     modern have noted the impossibility; dramatically it is the
     audience's own introduction to the Achaean captains, staged through
     the eyes of the two people most implicated in the war.

[13] (3.180) "dog-face that I am" (κυνώπιδος) — Helen turns on herself
     the poem's harshest shame-insult (cf. Achilles to Agamemnon, Book 1
     note [19]). Her self-lacerations ("hateful me," 404; cf. 173-176)
     are unique in the poem and are rendered at full strength.

[14] (3.189) The Amazons, "the match of men" (ἀντιάνειραι) — one of only
     two Iliad references to them (cf. 6.186); Priam fought against them
     on the Sangarius in his youth. In post-Homeric epic they come to
     Troy as allies — here they are still simply a memory of a hard war.

[15] (3.205-224) Antenor recalls the pre-war embassy: Odysseus and
     Menelaus came to Troy to demand Helen back before hostilities — a
     tradition the Iliad otherwise passes over. His sketch of Odysseus's
     oratory (the stiff stance, eyes down, then words "like snowflakes
     in winter") became the classical archetype of deceptive-looking
     eloquence.

[16] (3.243-244) The famous ironic close of the teichoscopia: Helen
     scans the plain for her brothers Castor and Polydeuces, imagining
     them ashamed of her — "but the life-giving earth already held them
     under." The narrator states their death with the epithet φυσίζοος
     ("life-giving") set against the grave: kept in its full contradiction.
     Homer knows nothing (or says nothing) of their later shared
     immortality.

[17] (3.276-279) The oath-witnesses: Zeus of Ida, the all-seeing Sun,
     the Rivers and Earth, "and you two below who punish the dead" —
     probably Hades and Persephone (some ancients understood the
     Erinyes). Perjury is the one sin explicitly punished after death
     in the Iliad.

[18] (3.298-301) The curse enacts itself in the ritual: as the wine is
     poured out, so may the perjurers' brains pour on the ground. "Their
     wives be subdued to other men" completes the symmetry — the war is,
     precisely, about a wife taken by another man.

[19] (3.302) A Ptolemaic papyrus preserves four extra lines here — Zeus
     thundering in answer and planning woes for both sides — absent from
     the medieval manuscripts (apparatus, Line 302; cf. disputed_lines.md
     #14 on Ptolemaic "plus verses"). A live specimen of how fluid the
     text still was before the Alexandrian standardization. The vulgate's
     laconic "the son of Cronos would not yet bring it to pass" is
     translated; the papyrus expansion is recorded here.

[20] (3.328-338) The poem's first full arming type-scene, in the fixed
     order that recurs whenever a hero arms: greaves, corslet, sword,
     shield, helmet, spear(s). Later arming scenes (Agamemnon in Book
     11, Patroclus in 16, Achilles in 19) elaborate the same skeleton;
     it will not be re-footnoted. Zenodotus athetized 334-335
     (apparatus). Paris borrows his brother Lycaon's corslet — the
     bowman owns none.

[21] (3.346) "long-shadowed spear" (δολιχόσκιον ἔγχος) — the fixed
     epithet of the thrown spear, always so rendered.

[22] (3.396-418) Aristarchus athetized this whole exchange (apparatus,
     Line 396-418) — Helen seeing through the disguise, defying the
     goddess to her face ("Go sit beside him yourself... his wife — or
     else his slave"), and Aphrodite's naked threat. No other mortal in
     the Iliad speaks to a god like this; the scene lays bare what
     Aphrodite's "gifts" cost the one who receives them. Kept entire.

[23] (3.424) "laughter-loving Aphrodite" (φιλομειδὴς Ἀφροδίτη) — her
     fixed epithet, always so rendered.

[24] (3.424-425) A goddess carrying a chair for a mortal woman —
     service so startling the translation lets the narrator's shock show
     ("a goddess!" renders the emphatic θεά). Zenodotus rewrote 423-426
     into a single innocuous line, "and she herself went and sat opposite
     lord Alexandros" (apparatus, Line 423-426) — flattening exactly the
     scandal that gives the gesture its force: Aphrodite is herself
     procuring, and stoops to it.

[25] (3.428-436) Helen's greeting to the rescued Paris — a death-wish,
     a taunt, and a mid-sentence collapse from "challenge him again" to
     "no — you would die." Aristarchus athetized 432-436 (apparatus).
     Kept: the broken movement of the speech is the psychology.

[26] (3.445) Kranae ("Rocky Isle") — the island where Paris and Helen
     first slept together on the flight from Sparta; possibly a proper
     name, possibly just "a rocky island." Ancient guesses ranged from
     Cythera to an islet off the Laconian coast later renamed Helene.
