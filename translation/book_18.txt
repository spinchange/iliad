BOOK 18

The news reaches Achilles: the scream, the choice of death — and on
the anvil of Hephaestus a whole world of peace, hammered onto the
shield he will carry to kill Hector.

TRANSLATOR'S COMMENTARY

Book 18 is built from three scenes of making and unmaking: a man
unmade by four lines of news; a night of vows over a washed body;
and a god at his anvil making a world. The unmaking first: Antilochus
delivers the catastrophe in the poem's most economical sentence —
"Patroclus lies dead... naked; and Hector of the flashing helmet has
the armor" — and Achilles pours soot on his head and lies down in
the dust "mighty in his mightiness," the epitaph-line of Cebriones
(16.776) settling on a living man, while Antilochus holds his hands
"for fear he would cut his throat with the iron": the Iliad's only
suicide watch (notes [2], [3]). Thetis rises with all the Nereids —
the catalogue of sea-names the ancients athetized and this
translation keeps (note [4]) — to mourn a son still alive; and in
their exchange the poem reaches its hinge: "quick-fated then you
shall be, child" — "Then let me die at once, since I could not
defend my companion." Plato put the exchange at the center of the
Apology; Socrates chose his death by quoting this one (note [8]).
With it comes the wrath's own retraction: "may strife perish from
among gods and men, and anger — sweeter far than dripping honey, it
swells like smoke in men's chests" (note [9]).

The middle movement belongs to voice and light: the unarmed Achilles
at the ditch, flame streaming from his head like the beacon of a
besieged island city — the Achaeans' own situation made into his
halo — killing twelve men with three shouts (note [11]); Hera
forcing the sun into Ocean to end the longest day of the poem (note
[13]); and, in the standing Trojan assembly, Polydamas's fourth and
fatal counsel rejected — "Enyalius is impartial: he kills the
would-be killer" — with the narrator's civic verdict on the applause
(notes [14]-[16]).

Then the Shield (478-608), which Zenodotus athetized entire and
which is the reason the book is immortal: the cosmos; the city at
peace with its weddings and its trial — where the blood-price for a
killed man is argued before elders, the very ποινή Achilles refused
in Book 9, shown working (note [26]); the city at war fighting this
poem's own battle in miniature, Fate dragging the dead by the feet
(note [27]); the plowland turning black behind the plow "though it
was made of gold" (note [28]); the harvest, the vineyard with the
boy singing the Linos-song, the lions on the bull, the dancing
floor of Daedalus with its potter's-wheel simile and — kept in our
printed text against Aristarchus's deletion — the divine singer
himself, the poet inside his own artifact (note [33]); and Ocean
round the rim. A world of peace is forged as the equipment of
revenge: the poem's whole argument, carried on one arm.

kept identical; "quick-fated" continues 1.417; the slave-women, the
fixed soliloquy frames, and the Book 1 "Most dread son of Cronos"
line all recur in their registered forms.

 115  Hector. My own death-spirit I will accept whenever
 116  Zeus wills to accomplish it, and the other immortal gods.
 117  For not even the might of Heracles escaped his doom —

 128  "Yes, child — all this is true. It is no bad thing

 137  bringing fine armor from the lord Hephaestus."

 140  "Sink now, all of you, into the broad fold of the sea,

 143  to Hephaestus famed for craft, to see if he is willing
 144  to give my son famed armor, shining bright."
 145  So she spoke; and they sank at once beneath the wave of the sea,

 147  to Olympus, to bring famed armor to her dear son.
 148  So her feet carried her toward Olympus. And the Achaeans,
 149  fleeing with unearthly clamor before man-slaughtering Hector,
 150  came to the ships and the Hellespont;
 151  nor could the strong-greaved Achaeans drag the corpse
 152  of Patroclus, Achilles' aide, out of the missiles:
 153  for the army overtook it again, and the horses,
 154  and Hector, Priam's son, like flame in his fury.

 156  straining to drag him off, calling loud on the Trojans;

 161  As shepherds in the field can in no way drive

 173  the one side fighting for the dead man's body,
 174  the other, the Trojans, storming to drag him off

 182  "Goddess Iris — which of the gods sent you to me with this message?"
 183  Then answered him wind-footed swift Iris:
 184  "Hera sent me, the honored wife of Zeus;
 185  and the high-throned son of Cronos does not know it, nor any other

 196  Then answered him wind-footed swift Iris:
 197  "We know it well — yes, they hold your famed armor.

 203  and Achilles, dear to Zeus, rose up. And around
 204  his powerful shoulders Athena threw the tasseled aegis,
 205  and around his head the brilliant goddess set a crown
 206  of golden cloud, and out of it lit a flame, blazing bright.[11]
 207  As when smoke rises from a city into the upper air,
 208  far off, from an island that enemies have surrounded,
 209  where all day long the men are put to the test of hateful war
 210  from their own city walls; and then, with the setting sun,
 211  beacon-fires blaze up one after another, and the glare
 212  shoots high for the neighboring islanders to see,
 213  in hope that they may come with ships as warders-off of ruin:
 214  so from the head of Achilles the light went up to the upper air.
 215  He went from the wall and stood at the ditch; nor did he mix
 216  with the Achaeans, for he heeded his mother's strict command.

 234  mourning; and among them followed swift-footed Achilles,
 235  shedding hot tears, when he saw his trusted companion
 236  lying on the litter, torn by the sharp bronze —[12]
 237  the man he had sent out with horses and chariot
 238  into the war, and never received again at his homecoming.

 240  much against his will, into the streams of Ocean;
 241  and the Sun set, and the brilliant Achaeans rested
 242  from the strong struggle and the leveling war.

 244  loosed their swift horses from under the chariots

 246  The assembly was held standing: no man dared[14]
 247  sit, for trembling held them all — because Achilles
 248  had appeared, who had long stayed out of the grievous fighting.
 249  Among them the level-headed Polydamas began to speak,
 250  Panthous's son: he alone looked before and behind.
 251  He was Hector's companion, born in the one night with him;
 252  but one was far the better in words, the other with the spear.
 253  With good will toward them he spoke and addressed them:
 254  "Weigh both sides well, my friends. For my part I command us:
 255  go to the city now — do not wait for the brilliant dawn
 256  on the plain beside the ships. We are far from our wall.
 257  So long as that man raged against brilliant Agamemnon,
 258  so long the Achaeans were easier men to fight —
 259  I too was glad to camp beside their swift ships,
 260  hoping we should take the double-ended vessels.
 261  But now I dread the swift-footed son of Peleus, terribly.
 262  So violent is that man's heart, he will not be willing
 263  to stay on the plain, where Trojans and Achaeans
 264  share between them the middle ground and the fury of the war-god:
 265  it is for our city he will fight, and for our women.
 266  Let us go to the town. Trust me: for so it will be.
 267  For now, the ambrosial night has stopped the swift-footed
 268  son of Peleus; but if he finds us here tomorrow
 269  when he comes out armed, then a man will know him
 270  well enough: gladly will he reach holy Ilion,
 271  whoever escapes; and dogs and vultures will eat many
 272  of the Trojans. May such a tale stay far from my hearing!
 273  But if we do as I say — though it grieves us —
 274  tonight we keep our force in the meeting-place, and the city
 275  is guarded by its towers and high gates and the tall
 276  polished doors, fitted close, bolted fast, that shut them;
 277  and at first light, armed in our gear,
 278  we take our stand along the towers. The worse for him then,
 279  if he wants to come from the ships and fight us for the wall:
 280  back to the ships he must go, when he has worn out his strong-necked
 281  horses with every kind of running, driving them under the town.
 282  Inside it his heart will not let him charge;
 283  never will he sack it: sooner the running dogs will eat him."

 285  "Polydamas, what you say is no longer to my liking —
 286  you who command us to run back and be penned in the city.
 287  Have you not all had your fill of being cooped inside those towers?
 288  In the days before, all mortal men would tell of
 289  Priam's city as rich in gold, rich in bronze;
 290  but now the fine heirlooms are gone out of our houses:
 291  sold off, our treasures have long since passed to Phrygia
 292  and lovely Maeonia, since great Zeus turned his wrath on us.
 293  And now — now, when the son of crooked-counseled Cronos has granted me
 294  to win glory at the ships, and pen the Achaeans against the sea —
 295  fool, do not lay such counsels open before the people!
 296  No Trojan will obey you: I will not allow it.
 297  Come then, let us all do as I say.
 298  Take your supper now, through the army, by companies;
 299  remember the watch; and let every man stay wakeful.
 300  And any Trojan whose goods torment him beyond measure —
 301  let him gather and give it to the people to consume in common:
 302  better any of ours enjoy them than the Achaeans.
 303  And at first light, armed in our gear,
 304  we wake the sharp war-god beside the hollow ships.
 305  If it is true that brilliant Achilles has risen by the ships,
 306  the worse for him, if that is what he wants. I at least
 307  will not run from him out of the harsh-sounding war: I will stand
 308  against him, face to face — whether he carries off the great triumph,

 311  the fools: for Pallas Athena had taken their wits from them.
 312  To Hector they gave their praise, whose counsels were ruinous;
 313  to Polydamas no man's, who had devised the good counsel.
 314  Then they took their supper through the army. But the Achaeans
 315  all night long mourned Patroclus with wails and groans;
 316  and Peleus' son led the thick lament among them,[17]
 317  laying his man-slaughtering hands on his companion's chest,
 318  groaning again and again — like a bearded lion

 326  I said I would bring back his far-famed son to Opoeis
 327  with his share of the spoils, when he had sacked Ilion.
 328  But Zeus does not accomplish all the designs of men:
 329  for the two of us are fated to redden the same earth

 332  nor by Thetis my mother: the earth will hold me here.

 336  and before your pyre I will cut the throats of twelve
 337  shining children of the Trojans, in my rage at your slaying.
 338  Until then, you shall lie just so by the beaked ships,
 339  and around you the deep-girdled women of Troy and Dardania

 341  the women we two labored to win, by strength and the long spear,
 342  sacking the rich cities of mortal men."

 344  to set a great tripod over the fire, so that with all speed

 392  "Hephaestus — come out here: Thetis has need of you."

 399  Eurynome, daughter of back-flowing Ocean.
 400  Nine years I forged intricate things beside them —
 401  brooches, and curved spiral-bracelets, and rosette-cups, and necklaces —
 402  there in the hollow cave; and around us the stream of Ocean
 403  ran on forever, murmuring with foam. And no one knew —
 404  not one of the gods, not one of mortal men:
 405  only Thetis knew, and Eurynome — the two who saved me.
 406  Now she has come to our house; and so I am bound
 407  to pay fair-braided Thetis the whole ransom of my life.
 408  Set out the fine guest-fare before her now,
 409  while I put away my bellows and all my tools."
 410  He spoke, and heaved his monstrous bulk up from the anvil-block,[21]
 411  limping; and beneath him his slender legs moved quickly.
 412  He set the bellows away from the fire, and gathered
 413  all the tools of his labor into a silver chest;
 414  and with a sponge he wiped his face and both his hands,
 415  his massive neck and his shaggy chest,
 416  and put on a tunic, and took up a thick staff, and came out
 417  limping; and there moved quickly, supporting their lord,
 418  attendants made of gold, in the likeness of living girls:
 419  in them is mind, in their breasts, and voice,
 420  and strength; and they know handiwork from the immortal gods.
 421  These moved bustling at their lord's side; and he, laboring on
 422  to where Thetis was, sat down on a shining chair,
 423  and clung to her hand, and spoke a word, and named her:
 424  "Thetis of the trailing robe — why have you come to our house,
 425  honored guest and dear? Before now you have not come often.
 426  Say what is in your mind. My heart bids me do it,
 427  if do it I can — if it is a thing that can be done."
 428  Then Thetis answered him, shedding tears:[22]
 429  "Hephaestus — is there any goddess, of all on Olympus,
 430  who has endured so many bitter griefs in her heart,
 431  as many as the sorrows Zeus, Cronos's son, has given me beyond all?
 432  Out of all the sisters of the sea he broke me to a man,
 433  to Peleus, Aeacus' son; and I endured a man's bed,
 434  much against my will. That man lies in his halls now
 435  worn out with wretched old age. But there is more for me:
 436  he gave me a son to bear and to rear,
 437  first among heroes; and he shot up like a sapling.
 438  I reared him like a young tree on the orchard's slope
 439  and sent him out with the beaked ships to Ilion
 440  to fight the Trojans; and I shall never receive him again,
 441  never coming home again to the house of Peleus.
 442  And while he still lives, and looks on the light of the sun,
 443  he grieves; and I can do nothing to help him, though I go to him.
 444  The girl the sons of the Achaeans picked out as his prize —
 445  her the lord Agamemnon took back out of his hands;
 446  and grieving for her he was eating his own heart out. Then the Trojans
 447  penned the Achaeans against the sterns, and would not let them
 448  come out; and the elders of the Argives begged him,
 449  naming over the many glorious gifts.
 450  He refused, then, to beat off the ruin himself;
 451  but he put his own armor on Patroclus
 452  and sent him into the war, and a great force with him.
 453  All day they fought around the Scaean Gates,
 454  and would have sacked the city that same day — had not Apollo
 455  killed the stout son of Menoetius in the front ranks,
 456  Patroclus who had wrought so much harm, and handed the glory to Hector.
 457  So now I come to your knees: will you give my son —
 458  quick-fated as he is — a shield and a helmet,
 459  and fine greaves fitted with ankle-pieces,
 460  and a corslet? What he had, his trusted companion lost
 461  when the Trojans broke him; and my son lies on the ground, eating his heart out."
 462  Then the famed crook-footed god answered her:[23]
 463  "Take heart. Do not let these things trouble your mind.
 464  Would that I could hide him away from harsh-sounding death
 465  as surely — when the terrible doom comes for him —
 466  as fine armor shall be his: armor such that any man
 467  of the many who see it hereafter will marvel."
 468  So speaking he left her there and went to his bellows,
 469  and turned them toward the fire and bade them work.
 470  And the bellows — twenty in all — blew on the crucibles,[24]
 471  sending out their blast at every strength:
 472  now to serve him pressing hard, and now the opposite,
 473  however Hephaestus willed it and the work required.
 474  And he threw unwearying bronze on the fire, and tin,
 475  and precious gold, and silver; and next he set
 476  the great anvil on the anvil-block, and took up in one hand
 477  the massive hammer, and in the other took the tongs.
 478  And he made first of all a shield, great and heavy,
 479  working the whole of it with cunning; and around it he threw a rim,
 480  bright, triple, glittering; and from it a silver strap.
 481  Five were the layers of the shield itself; and on its face
 482  he wrought many intricate things with his knowing craft.
 483  On it he made the earth, and the heaven, and the sea,[25]
 484  the unwearied sun and the moon at her full,
 485  and on it all the constellations with which heaven is crowned —
 486  the Pleiades and the Hyades, and the strength of Orion,
 487  and the Bear, which men also call by the name of the Wagon,
 488  who turns in her own place, keeping watch on Orion,
 489  and alone has no share in the baths of Ocean.
 490  And on it he made two cities of mortal men,[26]
 491  beautiful. In the one there were weddings and feasts:
 492  brides were being led from their chambers under blazing torches
 493  up through the town, and loud rose the wedding-song;
 494  and young men whirled in the dance, and among them
 495  flutes and lyres kept up their cry; and the women,
 496  standing each at her door, looked on and marveled.
 497  And the people were gathered in the meeting-place, where a quarrel
 498  had risen: two men quarreling over the blood-price
 499  of a man who was killed. The one claimed he had paid all,
 500  declaring it to the people; the other refused to take anything;
 501  and both were eager to take an end of it before an arbiter.
 502  And the people cheered for both, supporters of either side,
 503  and the heralds held the crowd in check; and the elders
 504  sat on polished stones in the sacred circle,
 505  the scepters of the clear-voiced heralds in their hands;
 506  with these they rose, and gave judgment each in turn.
 507  And in the middle lay two talents of gold,
 508  to be given to the one who spoke the straightest judgment.
 509  But around the other city two armies of fighting men were lying[27]
 510  blazing in their armor; and their counsel was divided —
 511  whether to sack it outright, or to divide in two
 512  all the wealth the lovely town held inside it.
 513  But the besieged were not yielding: they had armed in secret for an ambush.
 514  Their dear wives and little children stood on the wall
 515  and guarded it, with the men whom old age held;
 516  and the rest went out. And Ares led them, and Pallas Athena,
 517  both of gold, and golden the clothes they wore,
 518  beautiful and great in their armor — like the gods they were,
 519  conspicuous on every side; and the people under them were smaller.
 520  And when they came to the place that suited their ambush,
 521  by a river, where there was a watering-place for all the herds,
 522  there they settled themselves, wrapped in blazing bronze.
 523  And two scouts sat apart from the army,
 524  waiting to sight the sheep and the twist-horned cattle.
 525  And these came on soon, and two herdsmen followed with them,
 526  delighting in their pipes: the trap they never guessed.
 527  And the men in ambush saw them and ran out, and swiftly
 528  cut off the herds of cattle and the fine flocks
 529  of white-shining sheep, and killed the herdsmen over them.
 530  But the besiegers, when they heard the great noise among the cattle
 531  from where they sat before the assembly-ground, mounted at once
 532  behind their high-stepping horses and came on fast, and reached them.
 533  And they formed up and fought their battle on the river-banks,
 534  striking one another with the bronze-fitted spears.
 535  And Strife was joining the crowd, and Tumult, and deadly Fate,
 536  holding one man fresh-wounded and alive, another
 537  unwounded, another dead, dragging him by the feet through the fighting;
 538  and the cloak on her shoulders was red with the blood of men.
 539  They closed and fought like living mortals,
 540  and dragged away each other's dead.
 541  And on it he set a fallow field, soft, rich plowland,[28]
 542  wide, thrice-turned; and on it many plowmen
 543  wheeled their teams and drove them this way and that;
 544  and whenever they turned and came to the field's edge,
 545  a man would come and put in their hands a cup
 546  of honey-sweet wine; and they would turn back down the furrows,
 547  straining to reach the deep fallow's edge again.
 548  And the field grew black behind them, like earth the plow has turned,
 549  though it was made of gold: that was the very wonder of the work.
 550  And on it he set a king's estate, where laborers[29]
 551  were reaping, with sharp sickles in their hands.
 552  Some handfuls were falling to the ground in rows along the swath,
 553  and others the sheaf-binders were tying with twisted straw:
 554  three binders stood at their work, and behind them
 555  boys gathered the handfuls and carried them in their arms,
 556  keeping them supplied without pause. And the king among them,
 557  in silence, staff in hand, stood at the swath, glad at heart.
 558  And heralds apart, beneath an oak, were laying out the feast,
 559  dressing the great ox they had slaughtered; and the women
 560  sprinkled the meal with white barley in plenty, for the laborers' dinner.
 561  And on it he set a vineyard, heavy-laden with grape-clusters,[30]
 562  beautiful, golden: the grapes along it were black,
 563  and it stood staked from end to end on silver poles.
 564  And around it he drove a ditch of blue enamel, and about that a fence
 565  of tin; and one single path led in,
 566  by which the carriers passed whenever they gathered the vintage.
 567  And girls and young men, hearts light as children's,
 568  carried the honey-sweet fruit in woven baskets;
 569  and in the middle of them a boy with a clear-toned lyre
 570  played longingly, and sang the beautiful Linos-song
 571  in his delicate voice; and they, stamping in time,
 572  followed with song and cries, skipping on their feet.
 573  And on it he made a herd of straight-horned cattle:[31]
 574  the cows were wrought of gold and of tin,
 575  and streamed lowing from the farmyard toward their pasture
 576  beside a murmuring river, beside the waving reeds.
 577  And golden herdsmen walked along with the cattle,
 578  four of them, and nine quick-footed dogs came after.
 579  But two terrible lions, in among the leading cattle,
 580  had hold of a bellowing bull; and he, roaring loud,
 581  was dragged away; and the dogs and the young men went after him.
 582  The two lions, ripping open the great bull's hide,
 583  were gulping the entrails and the black blood; and the herdsmen
 584  urged their swift dogs in vain, setting them on:
 585  the dogs kept shying back from sinking teeth in the lions,
 586  but stood in close and barked, and leapt away.
 587  And on it the famed crook-footed god made a pasture[32]
 588  in a beautiful glen, a great pasture of white-shining sheep,
 589  with steadings and roofed huts and pens.
 590  And on it the famed crook-footed god wrought a dancing floor,[33]
 591  like the one that Daedalus once fashioned
 592  in wide Knossos for lovely-haired Ariadne.
 593  There young men and girls whose bride-price is many oxen
 594  were dancing, holding one another's hands at the wrist.
 595  The girls wore fine linen, and the young men tunics
 596  well-woven, glistening softly with oil;
 597  and the girls wore beautiful garlands, and the young men carried
 598  golden daggers hanging from silver belts.
 599  And now they would run on skillful feet,
 600  lightly, easily — as when a potter, seated, tries
 601  the wheel fitted to his hands, to see if it will spin —
 602  and now again they would run in lines toward one another.
 603  And a great crowd stood around the lovely dance,
 604  delighting; and among them a divine singer made music,
 605  playing the lyre; and two acrobats, leading the measure,
 606  whirled through the middle of them.
 607  And on it he set the great strength of the river Ocean,[34]
 608  along the outermost rim of the strong-built shield.
 609  And when he had made the shield, great and heavy,[35]
 610  he made him a corslet brighter than the blaze of fire,
 611  and he made him a heavy helmet, fitted to his temples,
 612  beautiful, intricate, and set upon it a crest of gold,
 613  and he made him greaves of pliant tin.
 614  And when the famed crook-footed god had labored out all the armor,
 615  he took it up and laid it before the mother of Achilles;
 616  and she like a hawk plunged down from snow-capped Olympus,
 617  carrying the glittering armor from Hephaestus.

NOTES

[1] (18.1-14) Achilles reads the rout before the message arrives,
    through a prophecy remembered: "the best of the Myrmidons,
    while I still lived, would leave the light of the sun." His
    mother's oracles (cf. 17.408-411) work in him as dread; the
    fixed soliloquy frame ("troubled, he spoke...") carries the
    poem's most fearful private arithmetic. His last clause before
    the news is a rebuke to the dead man — "I ordered him: beat
    off the fire, then back" — the conditions of 16.87-96,
    recited by the man who set them, to no one.

[2] (18.15-21) The delivery: four lines, weeping, with the fixed
    formula shared by Menelaus's instructions (17.685-693) —
    "Patroclus lies dead... naked: for Hector of the flashing
    helmet has the armor" (the line reused verbatim). Nothing
    softened, nothing arranged: the worst news in the poem is
    also its shortest speech.

[3] (18.22-35) The unmaking: the black cloud; both hands pouring
    soot over the head; the "nectar-fragrant tunic" fouled with
    ash; and the fallen line — "in the dust he lay stretched
    out, mighty in his mightiness" — the epitaph of Cebriones
    (16.776, register kept), now describing a living man
    performing his own burial. The slave-women's shrieking
    (δμῳαί, the register's word) makes the hut a house of
    mourning; and Antilochus holds Achilles' hands "for fear he
    might cut his own throat with the iron" — Aristarchus's
    reading, and the Iliad's only suicide watch: the poem
    considers, for one line, that its hero might end it here.
    Then the scream that a goddess hears through the sea.

[4] (18.35-49) The Nereid catalogue: thirty-three names of the
    sea's daughters, athetized by Zenodotus and Aristarchus and
    omitted in the Argolic edition — and kept here, because the
    list is the point: an entire society of the sea rises
    keening. The names are transparent sea-poetry — Glauce
    (Grey-green), Cymodoce and Cymothoë (Wave-gatherer,
    Wave-swift), Nemertes and Apseudes (Truthful and Unlying),
    Pherousa and Dynamene (Carrier and Capable), Galateia of the
    milk-white calm — Hesiod's Theogony shares many. Grief
    summons the whole element: when Achilles screams, the
    Mediterranean answers by name.

[5] (18.50-64) Thetis leads the lament (ἐξῆρχε γόοιο — the
    ritual term for the chief mourner) for a son not yet dead,
    and coins for herself the poem's most desolate word:
    δυσαριστοτόκεια — "unhappy in the best of sons": mother of
    the best, and cursed by the superlative itself. The sapling
    lines — "he shot up like a sapling; I reared him like a
    young tree on the orchard's slope" — recur verbatim in her
    petition to Hephaestus (437-443): a mother's lament already
    hardened into liturgy.

[6] (18.65-77) The Nereids beach at Troy; Thetis "took her son's
    head in her arms" — the position of the chief mourner at a
    corpse's head (cf. 24.724): she mourns him alive, enacting
    the funeral she will not attend. Her comfort is the poem's
    bitterest arithmetic: all this "has been accomplished by
    Zeus, exactly as you prayed" — the Book 1 petition returned
    with interest.

[7] (18.78-93) Achilles' answer: the prayer granted, "but what
    pleasure is it to me?" Patroclus "equal to my own head" —
    self and friend one body — and the armor's loss traced to
    its origin: the gods' wedding-gift "on the day they threw
    you into the bed of a mortal man." The logic runs backward
    from grief to his own conception: better his mother had
    stayed in the sea, better he had never been. Then the vow
    that closes the argument of the whole poem's first movement:
    no life "unless Hector first, beaten under my spear, loses
    his life."

[8] (18.94-116) The choice. Thetis: "Quick-fated then you shall
    be, child" (ὠκύμορος — her word for him since 1.417);
    "straight after Hector your own doom stands ready." Achilles:
    "Then let me die at once (αὐτίκα τεθναίην), since I was not
    to help my companion at his killing." Plato set this
    exchange at the center of the Apology (28c-d): Socrates,
    refusing to abandon his post for fear of death, cites
    Achilles despising danger "when the alternative was to live
    a coward who does not avenge his friends" — the pagan
    scripture of principled dying, quoted by philosophy at its
    own martyrdom. (Aeschines quoted the neighboring lines in
    court in 345 BC, reading the friendship as love — the
    ancient debate about what Patroclus was to Achilles begins
    in the orators.) The self-portrait that follows is pitiless:
    "a useless weight on the plowland" — the best of the
    Achaeans as unharvested ground.

[9] (18.107-126) The wish against strife: "may strife perish
    from among gods and men, and anger, that drives even the
    prudent man to harshness: sweeter by far than honey dripping
    down, it swells like smoke in the chests of men." The wrath
    of the poem's first word is renounced by its owner in its
    most quoted self-description — anger as honey and smoke,
    pleasure and suffocation in one body. Then the turn: "done
    is done"; death accepted "whenever Zeus wills"; the Heracles
    precedent (even Zeus's dearest died); and the terrible new
    economy of fame — to make "some deep-girdled woman of Troy
    or Dardania wipe with both her hands the tears from her soft
    cheeks": his glory henceforth denominated in enemy grief.
    Thetis does not argue; she schedules the armor.

[10] (18.148-202) Hera's covert operation: Iris sent "in secret
    from Zeus," finding Achilles weaponless — and Iris's answer
    to "how am I to go?" is the poem's discovery about presence:
    "go to the ditch, just as you are, and show yourself." The
    reuse of 16.42-43 (our English kept: "catch their breath...
    for the breathing-spaces of war are few") makes the point
    formally: what Patroclus's borrowed armor did in Book 16,
    Achilles' bare appearance will do now. The disguise-plot
    ends: the man himself is the weapon.

[11] (18.203-231) The epiphany at the ditch: aegis, golden
    cloud, and flame from the head — and the great simile turns
    the Achaean camp inside out: the light is like smoke and
    beacon-fires "from an island that enemies have surrounded...
    in hope that neighbors may come with ships as warders-off of
    ruin." The besieged fleet's distress-signal is Achilles
    himself: he is the rescue the beacons pray for. The trumpet
    simile (a city ringed by "life-breaking" enemies) keeps the
    siege inverted; Athena's voice doubles his; and the toll is
    the poem's most economical: twelve men dead "on their own
    chariots and their own spears" — killed by sound. He never
    crosses the ditch; his mother's command holds even here.

[12] (18.232-239) The body received: Achilles walking behind the
    bier "shedding hot tears, when he saw his trusted companion
    lying on the litter, torn by the sharp bronze — the man he
    had sent out with horses and chariot into the war, and never
    received again at his homecoming." The verb is Thetis's (59,
    89, 440): the failed reception, parent's and friend's, is
    now the poem's defining gesture of loss.

[13] (18.239-242) Hera sends the unwearied Sun into Ocean
    "much against his will": the goddess closes by force the day
    that began at 11.1 — the longest day in poetry, eight books
    long — and with it expires Hector's warrant, which ran
    "until the sun goes down" (11.193-194). The astronomical
    order itself is bent to end a battle: nothing in the poem
    says more economically how much the gods now need Achilles
    on the field tomorrow.

[14] (18.243-284) The standing assembly ("no man dared sit"):
    fear given a posture. Polydamas — introduced with his full
    credentials: born the same night as Hector, "one far the
    better in words, the other with the spear," the man who
    "alone looked before and behind" — gives the fourth and last
    of his counsels (12.61 accepted; 12.211 rejected; 13.726
    accepted; now this): into the city tonight, fight from the
    towers at dawn. Every clause is correct, and the poem lets
    him paint the alternative exactly as Book 22 will stage it:
    the horses worn out "with every kind of running, driving
    them under the town... sooner the running dogs will eat
    him."

[15] (18.285-310) Hector's answer — the register's darkly-
    glaring formula for the first time turned on his own
    counselor since 12.230. Its center is the war's balance-
    sheet: Priam's city, once "rich in gold, rich in bronze,"
    stripped — "sold off, our treasures have long since passed
    to Phrygia and lovely Maeonia" — ten years of attrition in
    four lines, the clearest economics in the poem. Then the
    fatal logic: Zeus has granted me glory at the ships —
    "I will not allow" retreat; and the creed: "I will stand
    against him, face to face — whether he carries off the
    great triumph, or I. Enyalius is impartial: he kills the
    would-be killer" (ξυνὸς Ἐνυάλιος — war the commons, shared
    ground where the hunter is hunted). Magnificent, and the
    exact miscalculation: the last time Hector trusted a grant
    of Zeus, it expired at sundown (note [13]). It has.

[16] (18.310-314) The narrator's civic verdict: the Trojans
    applaud — "the fools: for Pallas Athena had taken their
    wits from them. To Hector they gave their praise, whose
    counsels were ruinous; to Polydamas no man's, who had
    devised the good counsel." The poem's one explicit theory
    of collective political failure: the assembly's judgment
    itself god-damaged. Hector will remember this night with
    these very terms at 22.99-107 ("Polydamas will be first to
    heap reproach on me"); the applause the Trojans give here
    is the shame that keeps him outside the wall to die.

[17] (18.314-355) The wake. Achilles leads the lament with
    "man-slaughtering hands" laid on his companion's chest —
    the poem's fixed epithet for Hector's hands (ἀνδροφόνος)
    transferred once, here, to Achilles' (ancient copies read
    it either way; the transfer is the point). The lion robbed
    of his cubs "goes down glen after glen tracking the man's
    footprints... for bitter anger takes hold of him": grief as
    pursuit — the simile knows revenge is a species of
    mourning. The speech makes the broken promise to Menoetius
    explicit (Opoeis, the share of spoils), accepts the shared
    grave ("the two of us are fated to redden the same earth
    here in Troy"), and then makes the poem's darkest vow:
    Hector's armor and head; "and before your pyre I will cut
    the throats of twelve shining children of the Trojans" —
    human sacrifice, promised in cold blood and later paid
    (21.27-32; 23.175-176). The poem neither softens nor
    condemns it; it sets it beside the tenderness of the
    washing — the boiled water, oil, nine-year unguent, soft
    linen, white cloak — one night containing both.

[18] (18.356-368) Zeus and Hera, dry as a domestic ledger:
    "So you have managed it... Truly they must be your own
    children, these long-haired Achaeans — born from you!" Her
    answer scales godhead by analogy from below: even a mortal
    man does what he can for a friend — how should the best of
    goddesses "not stitch evils for the Trojans in my anger?"
    (κακὰ ῥάψαι — mischief as needlework: the craft-verb of the
    book, on the eve of its greatest crafting.) An ancient
    critic (Zenodorus) condemned the exchange; it is the
    Olympian marriage in equilibrium — the quarrel of Books 1
    and 15 subsided into married irony.

[19] (18.369-381) The house of Hephaestus, "imperishable,
    starry... he had made it himself" — and the first wonder:
    twenty tripods with golden wheels under their bases, "so
    that self-moved (αὐτόματοι) they might enter the assembly
    of the gods and come home to his house again." The first
    self-propelled machines in literature, unfinished on the
    workbench — their intricate handles still to be riveted —
    when the biggest commission of the poem walks in. The god
    of craft is discovered mid-project, sweating: labor is
    labor even on Olympus.

[20] (18.382-409) Charis — "Grace of the shining veil" — is
    Hephaestus's wife here (in the Odyssey it is Aphrodite:
    the poem marries the craftsman to the quality of his own
    work). Her hospitality frames the god's great autobiography:
    thrown from heaven by "my own bitch-faced mother," who
    wanted the lame child hidden; caught in the laps of Thetis
    and Eurynome; nine years in the sea-cave forging "brooches,
    and curved spiral-bracelets, and rosette-cups, and
    necklaces" while Ocean foamed around and no one knew. (At
    1.590-594 he tells of a different fall — hurled by Zeus,
    landing on Lemnos: two falls, unreconciled; a god may have
    more than one myth.) The debt is named in the language of
    ransom: ζῳάγρια, "the whole ransom of my life" — the shield
    of Achilles will be forged as the repayment of a rescue,
    the poem's economy of obligation reaching into heaven.

[21] (18.410-421) The god at rest: the "monstrous bulk" heaving
    up from the anvil-block, the sponge over face and hands and
    "shaggy chest" — divine labor with human sweat — and then
    the second wonder: "attendants made of gold, in the likeness
    of living girls: in them is mind, in their breasts, and
    voice, and strength; and they know handiwork from the
    immortal gods." Artificial intelligence, three millennia
    early: the lame god has built himself helpers with minds.
    The poem presents them without astonishment — in this house,
    the made and the living have equal standing.

[22] (18.428-461) Thetis's petition: her own biography first —
    married off "out of all the sisters of the sea... and I
    endured a man's bed, much against my will," Peleus now
    "worn out with wretched old age" (the wedding the gods
    attended, reduced to a widowhood-in-waiting); the sapling
    lines repeated verbatim from her lament (note [5]); and
    then the entire Iliad summarized in thirteen lines — the
    prize taken, the wrath, the embassy's gifts refused, the
    borrowed armor, Apollo's kill, Hector's glory — the poem
    retold inside the poem as one mother's grievance
    (Aristarchus athetized the summary as needless; it is the
    story's shape seen from the sea: this is what the epic
    looks like to a bereaved immortal). The request itself is
    a plain equipment-list: shield, helmet, greaves with
    ankle-pieces, corslet — for a son she names with her own
    epithet for him: "quick-fated."

[23] (18.462-467) Hephaestus's answer measures exactly what
    craft can and cannot do: "Would that I could hide him away
    from harsh-sounding death as surely... as fine armor shall
    be his." He can make anything except the one thing wanted.
    The armor is promised not as protection but as marvel —
    "any man of the many who see it hereafter will wonder" —
    art's honest offer: not to save, but to amaze.

[24] (18.468-482) The forge in motion: twenty bellows blowing
    "at every strength... however Hephaestus willed it and the
    work required" — automated, responsive tools; the four
    metals; anvil, hammer, tongs. The shield: five layers,
    triple shining rim, silver strap — and the maker's formula
    that will govern everything on it: "he wrought many
    intricate things with his knowing craft" (ἰδυίῃσι
    πραπίδεσσιν — with knowing wits: intelligence in the
    hands).

[25] (18.483-489) The cosmos first: earth, heaven, sea, sun,
    full moon, "all the constellations with which heaven is
    crowned" — Pleiades, Hyades, Orion's strength, and the
    Bear-also-called-Wagon "who turns in her own place, keeping
    watch on Orion, and alone has no share in the baths of
    Ocean": the circumpolar star that never sets, described
    with precise naked-eye astronomy (the same lines steer
    Odysseus at Od. 5.272-275). The shield begins where Genesis
    begins; everything human on it will happen under this
    permanent sky.

[26] (18.490-508) The city at peace: weddings first — torchlit
    processions, the hymenaios, whirling dancers, "the women,
    standing each at her door, looked on and marveled" (the
    audience inside the artwork, marveling as we do). Then the
    trial: two men quarreling "over the blood-price of a man
    who was killed" — one claiming full payment made, one
    refusing to accept — both eager for an arbiter; the elders
    in the sacred circle with the heralds' scepters, judging in
    turn; two talents of gold for "the straightest judgment."
    The most-argued lines in Greek legal history (already in
    antiquity) — and, in this poem, the shield's quiet answer
    to its bearer: in the city at peace, a killing becomes a
    procedure; ποινή can be paid, accepted, judged. Ajax said
    it to Achilles' face (9.632-636): a man accepts the price
    even for a slain brother. Achilles carries into battle a
    picture of the settlement he refused — and will, in Book
    24, at last enact.

[27] (18.509-540) The city at war fights this poem's own war in
    miniature: the divided counsel of the besiegers (sack, or
    take half the wealth — the very offer Hector floated at
    22.111-121's rehearsal); the wall held by wives, children,
    and old men (Troy's exact demography, 8.518-522); the
    ambush at the watering-place; the herdsmen killed
    "delighting in their pipes: the trap they never guessed"
    (the shield's one unbearable clause — music as the last
    sound of the unsuspecting); and the river-bank battle
    joined by Strife, Tumult, and deadly Fate dragging a dead
    man by the feet, "and the cloak on her shoulders was red
    with the blood of men. They closed and fought like living
    mortals" — the highest compliment art pays itself in the
    poem: the made men fight like real ones. What Hephaestus
    forges here is the Iliad.

[28] (18.541-550) The plowing: thrice-turned fallow, the teams
    wheeling, and at every turn "a man would come and put in
    their hands a cup of honey-sweet wine" — labor's rhythm
    marked in refreshment. Then the master-stroke: "the field
    grew black behind them, and looked like earth that the
    plow has turned, though it was made of gold: that was the
    very wonder of the work." Gold made to look like soil —
    the poem names realism itself as the miracle: not the
    precious material but its perfect surrender to truth.

[29] (18.551-561) The king's estate: reapers, binders, boys
    with armfuls, the slaughtered ox under the oak, women and
    barley — and at the center, the poem's image of right
    lordship: "the king among them, in silence, staff in hand,
    stood at the swath, glad at heart." A king whose joy is
    watching his people's work — the anti-Agamemnon: no prize
    taken, nothing seized; the staff (σκῆπτρον, the quarrel's
    own prop in Book 1) held in stillness over harvest instead
    of strife. (Some ancient copies added a line naming the
    grain "Eleusinian Demeter's" — the apparatus records the
    pious insertion.)

[30] (18.562-573) The vineyard: gold vines, black grapes,
    silver poles, enamel ditch, tin fence, "and one single path
    led in" — the picture composed like the object it is. At
    its heart the poem's most haunting music: the boy with the
    clear lyre singing "the beautiful Linos-song in his
    delicate voice" while the pickers stamp and skip in time.
    The Linos-song was the ancient harvest-lament — grief for
    the dying year sung inside abundance (Herodotus 2.79 found
    its double in Egypt) — so even the shield's happiest panel
    keeps one thread of mourning, in a boy's voice, at the
    vintage.

[31] (18.574-587) The cattle and the lions: the herd streaming
    out "beside a murmuring river, beside the waving reeds" —
    and then the shield's only irruption of the wild: two
    lions on the bellowing bull, "gulping the entrails and the
    black blood," the dogs barking close and shying back. The
    countryside's one violence is the oldest kind — predation,
    not war: on the shield as in the poem's similes, the lion
    is what battle keeps trying to look like.

[32] (18.588-590) The sheep pasture: glen, steadings, huts,
    pens — three lines with no event at all: the shield's one
    panel of pure peace, and the shortest.

[33] (18.591-607) The dancing floor "like the one that Daedalus
    once fashioned in wide Knossos for lovely-haired Ariadne" —
    Homer's only Daedalus, invoked as the craftsman's own
    benchmark: Hephaestus outdoing the human master. Young men
    and much-wooed girls ("whose bride-price is many oxen"),
    hand on wrist; linen and oiled tunics; garlands, gold
    daggers on silver belts; and the wheeling runs compared to
    a potter testing his wheel — a craft simile inside the
    craftwork, the second artisan invoked within the artifact.
    Then the line with the great textual history: "among them
    a divine singer made music, playing the lyre." Aristarchus
    deleted the singer (Athenaeus 180-181 preserves the
    surgery), claiming the verse was imported from Odyssey
    4.17-18 — but our printed text keeps him, and rightly: the
    shield's final human figure is the poet, singing inside
    the world he is part of, while two acrobats lead the
    measure. The Iliad's maker signs the work — inside it.

[34] (18.607-608) Ocean at the rim: the river that circles the
    world circles the shield — the cosmos of line 483 closed
    by its own boundary; the object is finished as the world
    is, edged with water. On this disk Achilles will carry the
    entire ordered world — seasons, courts, weddings, harvests,
    the dance — into a battle where he intends to die.

[35] (18.610-618) The rest, in one breath each: corslet
    "brighter than the blaze of fire," helmet with golden
    crest, greaves of pliant tin; and the delivery — Thetis
    "like a hawk plunged down from snow-capped Olympus,
    carrying the glittering armor." The book that began with a
    scream ends with a stoop of wings: the mother diving
    seaward-fast out of heaven, bringing her quick-fated son
    the most beautiful object in literature, so that he can go
    and be killed in it gloriously. Book 19 opens with the
    armor laid down clashing before him.
