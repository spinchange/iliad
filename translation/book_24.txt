BOOK 24

The gods vote against the rage; a mother tells her son to eat, to
sleep, to love; an old man kisses the hands that killed his sons —
and the two of them weep, eat, and look at each other. The poem
that began with the wrath ends with a burial done properly.

TRANSLATOR'S COMMENTARY

The Iliad does not end with a fall of a city or a death of a hero.
It ends with an act of mercy so difficult that heaven has to
engineer it and two broken men have to perform it — and with a
funeral. Book 24 begins where Book 23's community ended: everyone
else asleep, Achilles alone on the beach, cycling through his
postures of grief, harnessing at each dawn to drag Hector three
times round the tomb — and going nowhere, because Apollo keeps the
corpse whole with the golden aegis. The mutilation has become a
machine that grinds and does not feed. The gods' council states
the poem's verdict through Apollo's indictment — Achilles
"ravages dumb earth in his fury," having lost pity and that aidōs
"that harms men greatly, and profits them"; even a man who loses
a brother or a son "weeps, and laments, and lets go: for the
Fates gave mankind an enduring heart" (note [3]). Zeus's ruling
refuses both theft and equal honor: Hector was dearest of Troy's
mortals to the gods; Achilles shall have the glory — of giving
him back. Mercy is arranged as an honor, because that is the
only currency the recipient still accepts (note [4]).

The double embassy — Thetis down to Achilles, Iris down to Priam
— produces the book's two impossible journeys: a mother telling
her doomed son to eat, sleep, and lie with a woman, "for you will
not be with me long" (note [6]); and an old man driving a
treasure-wagon at night into the camp of the man who has killed
his sons, against his wife's plea (her wish to eat Achilles'
liver raw is the third and most terrible of the poem's raw-eating
wishes, because it is hers; note [8]). Hermes — the god whose
dearest thing is "to be man's companion" — walks Priam through
the sleeping army disguised as a princely squire with his first
beard, and delivers, along the way, the poem's kindest sentence:
Hector lies twelve days unburied and "dewy-fresh... the blessed
gods care for your son though he is nothing now but a dead man,
because they loved him at heart" (note [13]).

Then the scene the whole epic exists for. Priam enters unnoticed,
clasps Achilles' knees, and kisses "the terrible, man-slaughtering
hands that had killed so many of his sons" — while the poem
compares him, the suppliant king, to a murderer arriving in exile
at a rich man's hall: the roles of killer and victim deliberately
exchanged in the simile (note [15]). "Remember your father" opens
the plea that ends: "I have endured what no other mortal on earth
has yet endured: to lift to my lips the hand of the man who
killed my son." And the two of them weep together — Priam for
Hector, Achilles "now for his father, now again for Patroclus" —
two griefs, one sound, filling the house (note [17]). What
follows is the consolation of the two jars — no unmixed good
exists for mortals; Peleus's splendor and Priam's fifty sons end
the same way; "Bear it... you will not raise him up" — and then
supper, and the strangest, most humane moment in Homer: the two
enemies simply look at each other, each marveling at what the
other is, "and when they had taken their fill of looking" (note
[22]). The old rage flickers once — "Do not provoke me now, old
man" — and its live presence under the mercy is exactly what
makes the mercy real (note [19]).

The supplication-arc of the late books (Tros refused, Lycaon
refused, Hector refused) closes with supplication accepted; and
with it the poem's largest ring: an aged father comes with
ransom to redeem his child from a king's wrath — Chryses, Book 1,
line 12 — and this time the king says yes. Achilles washes and
lifts the body himself, apologizes to Patroclus, promises a share
of the ransom, and grants eleven days' truce with his hand on the
old man's wrist "so his heart should not be afraid." Briseis, in
one quiet clause, sleeps at his side again. Priam drives home
past the ford of Xanthus (the register's line, its third
appearance); Cassandra cries the city awake; and the three
laments — Andromache with the head in her hands, Hecuba, and
last Helen, mourning the one man in Troy who was kind to her —
carry the poem to the pyre, the golden box, the purple robes, the
mound raised under guard, the funeral feast. "So they busied
themselves with the burial of Hector, the tamer of horses":
the epic of wrath ends on rites performed — grief, at last,
allowed to do its work properly (note [30]).

 369  no strength to fight off a man who starts a quarrel.
 370  But I will do you no harm — no: I would defend you
 371  against another. You are the image of my own dear father."
 372  Then in turn the old man, godlike Priam, answered him:
 373  "It is all much as you say, dear child.
 374  But some god, even now, has held his hand over me,
 375  who sent a wayfarer like you to cross my path —
 376  a good omen: such is your body and your beauty, a wonder,
 377  and your mind is sound. Blessed are the parents you come of."
 378  Then in turn the courier, the slayer of Argus, addressed him:
 379  "Yes, old man — all this you have said aright.
 380  But come, tell me this, and recount it truly:
 381  are you sending away a great treasure, and a fine one,
 382  to foreign men somewhere, so that much at least survives you?
 383  Or are you all abandoning holy Ilion now
 384  in fear — such a man has perished, the best among you,
 385  your son: who never hung back from battle with the Achaeans."
 386  Then in turn the old man, godlike Priam, answered him:
 387  "Who are you, my brave one? Of what parents do you come,
 388  who speak so beautifully of my ill-fated son's death?"
 389  Then in turn the courier, the slayer of Argus, addressed him:
 390  "You test me, old man — asking about brilliant Hector.
 391  Him my own eyes have seen — and often — in the battle
 392  where men win glory; and when he drove the Argives
 393  onto their ships and kept killing, carving with the sharp bronze,
 394  we stood and marveled at it: for Achilles,
 395  in his anger at Atreus's son, would not let us fight.
 396  I am his squire; one strong-built ship brought us both.
 397  I am a Myrmidon by birth; my father is Polyctor —
 398  a wealthy man, and old, just as you are:
 399  six sons he has, and I am his seventh;
 400  I shook lots with the others, and the lot fell to me to sail here.
 401  Now I have come to the plain from the ships: for at daybreak
 402  the bright-eyed Achaeans will set their battle around the city.
 403  They chafe at sitting idle, and the kings of the Achaeans
 404  cannot hold them back, so hot they are for war."
 405  Then in turn the old man, godlike Priam, answered him:
 406  "If you are truly a squire of Achilles, Peleus's son,
 407  come then — tell me the whole truth:
 408  is my son still by the ships? Or has Achilles
 409  cut him limb from limb by now, and thrown him to his dogs?"
 410  Then in turn the courier, the slayer of Argus, addressed him:
 411  "Old man — the dogs have not eaten him, nor the birds:
 412  he lies there still, beside the ship of Achilles,
 413  just as he was, among the huts. Twelve dawns now
 414  he has lain there; and his flesh does not decay, and the worms
 415  do not eat him — the worms that devour men killed in war.
 416  It is true that around the tomb of his dear companion
 417  Achilles drags him, careless, whenever bright dawn shows —
 418  but he cannot disfigure him. You would marvel yourself, if you came,
 419  at how he lies there dewy-fresh, the blood all washed
 420  from him, no stain anywhere; and every wound has closed,
 421  every one that was struck — and many drove their bronze into him.
 422  Such is the care the blessed gods take for your son,
 423  a dead man though he is — because they loved him at heart."
 424  So he spoke; and the old man rejoiced, and answered:
 425  "My child — how good a thing it is to give the immortals
 426  their due gifts! For my son — if ever he truly was —
 427  never forgot, in his halls, the gods who hold Olympus;
 428  and so they have remembered him, even in the portion of death.
 429  But come — take from me this beautiful cup,
 430  and guard my body, and escort me, with the gods,
 431  until I come to the hut of Peleus's son."
 432  Then in turn the courier, the slayer of Argus, addressed him:
 433  "You test me again, old man — the young man — but you will not persuade me,
 434  asking me to take your gifts behind Achilles' back.
 435  I fear that man; my heart shrinks with too much awe
 436  to rob him — lest some evil find me afterward.
 437  But you — I would escort you with all care, even to famous Argos,
 438  by swift ship or on foot beside you;
 439  and no man would make light of your escort, or attack."
 440  He spoke, and the helper-god sprang up onto the chariot,[14]
 441  and swiftly caught the whip and reins into his hands,
 442  and breathed brave strength into the horses and the mules.
 443  And when they came to the towers and the ditch of the ships,
 444  the sentries were just busying themselves with supper;
 445  and the courier, the slayer of Argus, poured sleep
 446  over them all, and opened the gates at once, and thrust back the bars,
 447  and led in Priam and the shining gifts on the wagon.
 448  And when they came to the hut of Peleus's son —
 449  the high hut the Myrmidons had built their lord,
 450  cutting beams of fir, and roofed it over
 451  with a shaggy thatch of reeds mown from the meadow,
 452  and around it made their lord a great courtyard
 453  with close-set stakes; and one single bar of fir
 454  held the door — a bolt three Achaeans would drive home
 455  and three would draw, of the others: three men
 456  to that great bolt, though Achilles would ram it home alone —
 457  then Hermes the helper opened it for the old man,
 458  and brought in the famed gifts for the swift-footed son of Peleus,
 459  and stepped down from the chariot to the ground, and said:
 460  "Old man — I am an immortal god come down to you:
 461  Hermes. My father sent me with you as your escort.
 462  But now I will go back: I will not walk
 463  into Achilles' sight. It would stir indignation —
 464  an immortal god openly entertained by mortals.
 465  You go in; clasp the knees of Peleus's son,
 466  and beg him by his father, by his mother of the lovely hair,
 467  by his child — so you may stir the heart in him."
 468  So speaking, Hermes went his way to high Olympus;[15]
 469  and Priam leapt from the chariot to the ground
 470  and left Idaeus there to hold
 471  the horses and mules; and the old man walked straight in,
 472  to where Achilles, dear to Zeus, was sitting. Inside
 473  he found him — his companions sitting apart; only two,
 474  the hero Automedon and Alcimus, sprig of Ares,
 475  were busy at his side; he had newly finished eating,
 476  finished food and drink; the table still stood by him.
 477  Great Priam came in unseen by them all, and standing close,
 478  he clasped Achilles' knees with his hands, and kissed his hands —
 479  the terrible, man-slaughtering hands that had killed so many of his sons.
 480  As when thick blindness takes a man, who in his own country
 481  kills another, and comes among a different people,
 482  to a rich man's house, and wonder holds all who see him:
 483  so Achilles wondered, seeing godlike Priam;
 484  and the others wondered too, and looked at one another.
 485  And Priam, in supplication, spoke his word to him:[16]
 486  "Remember your father, Achilles like the gods —
 487  a man of my years, on the ruinous threshold of old age.
 488  It may be that the men who live around him
 489  are grinding him down, and there is no one to beat off ruin and destruction.
 490  Yet he at least, hearing that you are alive,
 491  rejoices in his heart, and hopes through all his days
 492  to see his dear son coming home from Troy.
 493  But I am doomed to the last: I fathered the best sons
 494  in wide Troy, and I tell you not one of them is left.
 495  Fifty I had, when the sons of the Achaeans came:
 496  nineteen were mine from a single womb,
 497  and the others women of the palace bore me.
 498  Rushing Ares has unstrung the knees of most of them;
 499  and the one who was left me, who guarded the city and the people —
 500  him you killed, days ago, fighting for his fatherland:
 501  Hector. For his sake I come now to the ships of the Achaeans
 502  to ransom him from you; and I bring ransom past counting.
 503  Respect the gods, Achilles, and pity me,
 504  remembering your own father. And I am still more pitiable:
 505  I have endured what no other mortal on earth has yet endured —
 506  to lift to my lips the hand of the man who killed my son."
 507  So he spoke; and he stirred in Achilles the desire to weep for his father.[17]
 508  He took the old man's hand, and gently pushed him back;
 509  and the two of them remembered: the one wept long and sore
 510  for man-slaughtering Hector, huddled at Achilles' feet,
 511  and Achilles wept for his own father — and now again
 512  for Patroclus; and the sound of their mourning filled the house.
 513  But when brilliant Achilles had taken his fill of lament,
 514  and the longing for it had gone out of his chest and limbs,
 515  he rose from his seat at once, and raised the old man up by the hand,
 516  pitying the grey head and the grey beard,
 517  and speaking winged words he addressed him:
 518  "Poor man. How much evil your heart has endured![18]
 519  How could you bear to come to the ships of the Achaeans — alone —
 520  into the eyes of the man who has stripped your many
 521  brave sons? Your heart must be iron.
 522  Come, sit on this chair; and our griefs — we will let them
 523  lie quiet in our hearts, for all our sorrow.
 524  There is nothing to be gained from chill lament.
 525  For this is the way the gods have spun it for wretched mortals:
 526  to live in grief. And they themselves are free of care.
 527  Two jars stand on the floor of Zeus's hall,
 528  full of the gifts he gives: one of evils, one of blessings.
 529  The man to whom Zeus who delights in thunder gives a mixture,
 530  meets now with evil, and now again with good;
 531  but the man to whom he gives only from the jar of sorrows —
 532  him he has made an outcast: ravening hunger drives him over the earth,
 533  and he wanders honored neither by gods nor by mortals.
 534  So it was with Peleus: the gods gave him shining gifts
 535  from his birth; he outshone all mankind
 536  in wealth and fortune, and was king of the Myrmidons,
 537  and mortal though he was, they made a goddess his wife.
 538  But even on him the god laid evil: no brood
 539  of ruling sons was born to him in his halls —
 540  one child he fathered, doomed out of season. And I give him
 541  no care in his old age: far from my fatherland
 542  I sit here in Troy, grieving you and your children.
 543  And you, old man — we hear you prospered once:
 544  all that Lesbos out to sea, the seat of Macar, bounds,
 545  and Phrygia inland, and the boundless Hellespont —
 546  over all these, old man, they say you were first in wealth and sons.
 547  But since the gods of heaven brought this affliction on you,
 548  there is nothing around your city now but battles and the killing of men.
 549  Bear it. Do not mourn without respite in your heart.
 550  You will accomplish nothing, grieving for your son;
 551  you will not raise him up — sooner you will suffer some new evil."
 552  Then in turn the old man, godlike Priam, answered him:[19]
 553  "Do not seat me on a chair, Zeus-nurtured — not while Hector
 554  lies in the huts uncared-for. Give him back
 555  quickly, so my eyes can see him; and accept the ransom —
 556  the great ransom we bring you. May you have joy of it, and come
 557  to your own fatherland — since you have let me,
 558  from the first, live on and look upon the light of the sun."
 559  Then, glaring darkly, swift-footed Achilles answered him:
 560  "Do not provoke me now, old man. I mean myself
 561  to give Hector back to you: a messenger came to me from Zeus —
 562  my mother, who bore me, the daughter of the old man of the sea.
 563  And I know you too, Priam — do not think to hide it:
 564  some god led you to the swift ships of the Achaeans.
 565  No mortal would have dared come into the camp — not even
 566  a man in his prime: he could not have slipped past the sentries,
 567  nor easily levered back the bar of our doors.
 568  So do not stir my heart further in its griefs,
 569  or I may not spare even you, old man, inside my huts —
 570  suppliant though you are — and may trespass against the commands of Zeus."
 571  So he spoke; and the old man was afraid, and obeyed.
 572  And the son of Peleus sprang out the door of the house like a lion —[20]
 573  not alone: two of his aides went with him,
 574  the hero Automedon and Alcimus, the two Achilles
 575  honored most of his companions after dead Patroclus.
 576  They loosed the horses and mules from the yokes,
 577  and led in the old man's clear-voiced herald
 578  and sat him on a bench; and from the polished wagon
 579  they lifted down the ransom past counting for Hector's head.
 580  But they left two mantles and a fine-spun tunic,
 581  so he might wrap the body and give it to be carried home.
 582  And Achilles called out slave-women, and ordered them to wash him
 583  and anoint him — but apart, moved away, so Priam would not see his son:
 584  lest the grief in his heart not master his anger
 585  at the sight of his child, and Achilles' own spirit be stirred,
 586  and he kill him, and trespass against the commands of Zeus.
 587  And when the slave-women had washed him and anointed him with oil,
 588  and wrapped the beautiful mantle and the tunic around him,
 589  Achilles himself lifted him and laid him on a bier,
 590  and his companions with him raised it onto the polished wagon.
 591  Then he groaned, and called his dear companion by name:
 592  "Do not be angry with me, Patroclus, if you learn —
 593  even in the house of Hades — that I released brilliant Hector
 594  to his dear father: the ransom he gave was not unworthy.
 595  And to you, in turn, I will give your share of it — all that is fitting."
 596  He spoke, and went back into the hut, brilliant Achilles,
 597  and sat down on the richly-worked couch he had risen from,
 598  against the far wall, and spoke his word to Priam:
 599  "Your son is given back, old man, as you commanded:
 600  he lies on a bier. At the showing of dawn
 601  you will see him yourself, and take him. Now — let us remember supper.
 602  For even lovely-haired Niobe remembered food,[21]
 603  though twelve children perished in her halls —
 604  six daughters, and six sons in the pride of their youth.
 605  The sons Apollo killed with his silver bow,
 606  in anger at Niobe; and Artemis who showers arrows killed the daughters —
 607  because Niobe had matched herself with fair-cheeked Leto:
 608  Leto, she said, bore two; she herself had borne many.
 609  So those two, though they were only two, destroyed them all.
 610  Nine days they lay in their blood, and there was no one
 611  to bury them: the son of Cronos had turned the people to stones.
 612  On the tenth day the gods of heaven buried them.
 613  And she remembered food, when she was worn out with weeping.
 614  And now, somewhere among the rocks, in the lonely mountains,
 615  on Sipylus, where they say the goddess nymphs
 616  have their beds — the nymphs who dance along the Achelous —
 617  there, stone though she is, she broods on the griefs the gods gave her.
 618  Come then, noble old man: let the two of us also remember
 619  food. Afterward you may weep for your dear son again,
 620  when you have brought him into Ilion. He will cost you many tears."
 621  He spoke, and swift Achilles sprang up and slaughtered[22]
 622  a white-fleeced sheep; and his companions flayed and dressed it well
 623  and duly, and cut it up with skill, and pierced it on the spits,
 624  and roasted it with care, and drew off every portion.
 625  And Automedon took bread and set it out along the table
 626  in beautiful baskets; and Achilles portioned out the meat.
 627  And they reached out their hands to the good things lying ready.
 628  And when they had put away the desire for drink and food,
 629  then Priam, Dardanus's descendant, marveled at Achilles —
 630  how big he was, how beautiful: like the gods to look upon;
 631  and Achilles marveled at Priam, Dardanus's descendant,
 632  looking at his fine face and listening to his words.
 633  And when they had taken their fill of looking at each other,
 634  the first to speak was the old man, godlike Priam:[23]
 635  "Give me a bed now quickly, Zeus-nurtured, so that we
 636  may lie down at last and take our fill of sweet sleep.
 637  For my eyes have never closed beneath their lids
 638  since my son lost his life under your hands:
 639  I have done nothing but groan, and brood on griefs past counting,
 640  rolling in the dung in the yard where my cattle are penned.
 641  Now at last I have tasted bread, and let the gleaming wine
 642  down my throat. Before this I had touched nothing."
 643  He spoke; and Achilles ordered his companions and the slave-women
 644  to set out beds under the porch, and to throw on them
 645  fine purple rugs, and spread blankets over these,
 646  and lay out fleecy cloaks on top for covering.
 647  And the women went from the hall with torches in their hands,
 648  and quickly and busily made up two beds.
 649  And swift-footed Achilles said to him, with an edge in his voice:
 650  "Sleep outside, dear old man — in case some Achaean
 651  councilor comes here: they are forever sitting
 652  at my side weaving plans, as is their custom.
 653  If one of them saw you through the running black night,
 654  he would tell it at once to Agamemnon, shepherd of the people,
 655  and there would be delay in the release of the body.
 656  But come, tell me this, and recount it truly:
 657  how many days do you need for the burial of brilliant Hector —
 658  so I may wait that long myself, and hold back the army."
 659  Then in turn the old man, godlike Priam, answered him:[24]
 660  "If you are truly willing that I give brilliant Hector burial,
 661  then this, Achilles, is how you would do me kindness.
 662  You know how we are penned in the city; and the wood
 663  is far to bring from the mountain, and the Trojans are afraid.
 664  Nine days we would mourn him in the halls;
 665  on the tenth we would bury him, and the people feast;
 666  on the eleventh we would raise the mound above him;
 667  and on the twelfth we will fight again — if we must."
 668  Then in turn swift-footed brilliant Achilles addressed him:
 669  "This too shall be as you command, old Priam:
 670  I will hold off the war for the time you ask."
 671  So speaking, he took the old man's right hand
 672  at the wrist, so his heart should not be afraid.
 673  So there in the fore-hall of the house they lay down,
 674  the herald and Priam, with close-packed counsel in their minds;
 675  but Achilles slept in the inner corner of his strong-built hut,
 676  and fair-cheeked Briseis lay beside him.
 677  Now all the other gods and the chariot-fighting men[25]
 678  slept the whole night through, subdued by soft sleep;
 679  but sleep would not take Hermes the helper,
 680  turning it over in his heart how he would bring king Priam
 681  away from the ships unseen by the strong gate-keepers.
 682  He stood above his head, and spoke a word to him:
 683  "Old man — no evil worries you, to judge by how you sleep
 684  among enemy men, because Achilles spared you.
 685  You have ransomed your dear son now; you paid a great price.
 686  But for you — alive — your sons left behind would pay
 687  three times as much again, if Agamemnon
 688  Atreus's son knew of you — if all the Achaeans knew."
 689  So he spoke; and the old man was afraid, and woke the herald.
 690  And Hermes yoked the horses and the mules for them,
 691  and drove them quickly through the camp himself; and no one knew.
 692  But when they came to the ford of the fair-flowing river,
 693  eddying Xanthus, whom immortal Zeus begot,
 694  then Hermes went his way to high Olympus,
 695  and Dawn in her saffron robe was spreading over all the earth,
 696  and they drove the horses toward the city with wailing
 697  and lamentation; and the mules carried the dead.
 698  Nor did any other know them, man or fair-girdled woman —[26]
 699  only Cassandra, like golden Aphrodite,
 700  who had climbed Pergamus, and saw her dear father
 701  standing in the chariot, and the herald, the city's crier,
 702  and saw him too — lying on the bier behind the mules —
 703  and shrieked, and cried out through the whole city:
 704  "Come, men of Troy, women of Troy: come and see Hector —
 705  if ever, while he lived, you rejoiced when he came home
 706  from battle: for he was a great joy to the city and all its people."
 707  So she spoke; and not one man was left in the city,
 708  not one woman: on all of them came grief past bearing;
 709  and close by the gates they met him bringing home the dead.
 710  First of all his dear wife and his lady mother
 711  tore their hair for him, rushing at the smooth-wheeled wagon,
 712  touching his head; and the crowd stood around them, weeping.
 713  And now the whole day long until the setting sun
 714  they would have mourned Hector before the gates, pouring their tears,
 715  had not the old man spoken to the people from his chariot:
 716  "Make way for the mules to pass. Afterward
 717  you may glut yourselves with weeping — when I have brought him home."
 718  So he spoke; and they parted, and made way for the wagon.
 719  And when they had brought him into the famous house, they laid him
 720  on a corded bed, and set beside him singers,
 721  leaders of the dirge, who sang their mournful song —
 722  they chanted the dirge, and the women wailed in answer.
 723  And among them white-armed Andromache led the lament,[27]
 724  holding in her hands the head of man-slaughtering Hector:
 725  "Husband — you were lost young out of life, and you leave me
 726  a widow in your halls; and the boy is just a baby still,
 727  whom we bore, you and I — the two ill-fated. And I think
 728  he will not reach manhood: before that, this city
 729  will be sacked from its height. For you have perished — you, its watcher,
 730  who guarded it, and kept safe its honored wives and little children.
 731  They will ride away soon in the hollow ships,
 732  and I with them. And you, my child — you will follow me
 733  where degrading labors wait for you,
 734  drudging under a harsh master's eye — or some Achaean
 735  will seize you by the arm and fling you from the wall — a wretched death —
 736  in anger, because Hector killed his brother,
 737  or his father, or his son: for very many Achaeans
 738  bit the boundless earth in their teeth at Hector's hands.
 739  Your father was not gentle in the sad fighting;
 740  and so the people mourn him through the city,
 741  and you have laid a cursed grief and mourning, Hector,
 742  on your parents. And for me beyond all others the bitter
 743  pain is left: you did not die in bed, reaching your arms to me,
 744  you spoke no close last word that I might have
 745  remembered always, nights and days, with my tears falling."
 746  So she spoke, weeping; and the women wailed in answer.
 747  And among them Hecuba in her turn led the thick lament:[28]
 748  "Hector — far dearest to my heart of all my children,
 749  dear to the gods you were while you lived with me,
 750  and they have cared for you even in the portion of death.
 751  My other sons — swift-footed Achilles would sell them,
 752  whichever ones he took, across the barren sea,
 753  into Samos, and Imbros, and Lemnos of the smoke.
 754  But you — when he had taken your life with the long-edged bronze,
 755  he dragged you again and again around the tomb of his companion
 756  Patroclus, whom you killed — though not even so did he raise him up.
 757  And now you lie in my halls dewy-fresh, as if newly slain,
 758  like one whom Apollo of the silver bow has visited
 759  and killed with his gentle arrows."
 760  So she spoke, weeping, and roused the endless lament.
 761  And third after them, Helen led the lament:[29]
 762  "Hector — far dearest to my heart of all my husband's brothers!
 763  My husband is godlike Alexandros,
 764  who brought me to Troy. I wish I had died before that day.
 765  This is now the twentieth year for me
 766  since I came away from there and left my own country;
 767  yet never once did I hear from you an evil word, or a slighting one.
 768  No — if any other rebuked me in the halls,
 769  a brother of my husband, or a sister, or a brother's fine-robed wife,
 770  or my husband's mother — though his father was always gentle as my own —
 771  you would turn them aside, you would restrain them,
 772  with your gentle-mindedness and your gentle words.
 773  And so I weep for you — and for myself, doomed as I am — grieving at heart:
 774  for there is no one else now in wide Troy
 775  gentle to me, or kind: they all shudder at me."
 776  So she spoke, weeping; and the vast crowd groaned in answer.
 777  And old Priam spoke his word among the people:[30]
 778  "Bring wood now, Trojans, into the city; and have no fear
 779  in your hearts of an Argive ambush: for Achilles,
 780  sending me from the black ships, gave me his pledge
 781  that he will do us no harm until the twelfth dawn comes."
 782  So he spoke; and they yoked oxen and mules to the wagons,
 783  and quickly then they gathered before the city.
 784  Nine days they hauled in wood past measuring;
 785  and when the tenth dawn rose with its light for mortals,
 786  then they carried out bold Hector, weeping,
 787  and set the body on the height of the pyre, and threw on the fire.
 788  And when young Dawn showed again, the rosy-fingered,
 789  then the people gathered around glorious Hector's pyre.
 790  And when they had assembled and were gathered together,
 791  first they quenched the burning-place with glistening wine,
 792  all of it, wherever the fire's strength had reached; and then
 793  his brothers and companions gathered the white bones,
 794  mourning, and the swollen tears ran down their cheeks.
 795  And they took the bones and laid them in a golden box,
 796  wrapping them in soft purple robes,
 797  and quickly set it down in a hollow grave,
 798  and roofed it over with great stones set close together;
 799  and swiftly they heaped the mound — with watchers posted round on every side,
 800  lest the strong-greaved Achaeans should storm out before the time.
 801  And when they had heaped the mound they went back; and then,
 802  gathered together in due order, they feasted a glorious feast
 803  in the house of Priam, the king nurtured of Zeus.
 804  So they busied themselves with the burial of Hector, the tamer of horses.

NOTES

[1] (24.1-21) The book opens on the community dissolving back
    into ordinary life — supper, sweet sleep — and one man
    exempt: Achilles cycling through the postures of grief
    (side, back, face-down, up and pacing), unreached by "sleep
    that subdues all things." The dawns are iterative — the
    Greek's tenses make the dragging a routine: harness, drag
    three times round the tomb, rest, leave him face-down. And
    the routine accomplishes nothing: Apollo with the golden
    aegis keeps the flesh whole, "pitying the man even in
    death." Rage has become machinery — running, grinding, and
    feeding no one. That futility is the book's premise.

[2] (24.22-30) The divine majority wants the corpse stolen;
    three gods refuse — Hera, Poseidon, Athena — because of the
    poem's oldest wound: "the blindness of Alexandros, who
    insulted the goddesses when they came to his sheep-fold,
    and praised the one who fed his fatal lust." This is the
    Iliad's only mention of the Judgment of Paris, and
    Aristarchus athetized the passage — partly because the poem
    nowhere else knows the story, partly for the coarse word
    μαχλοσύνη ("lust"). Kept here: at the very end, the poem
    finally names the beginning — a beauty contest on a
    hillside as the seed of everything we have watched.

[3] (24.31-54) Apollo's indictment is the poem's ethical
    summit-speech: Achilles has "destroyed pity," and lost
    aidōs — the shame-before-others "which harms men greatly,
    and profits them too": the double-edged social emotion
    that the whole epic runs on, defined in one line. The
    lion comparison strips the aristeia of its glamour
    (savagery "at the flocks of men, to take his feast"); and
    the norm is stated with terrible gentleness: a man may
    lose a brother or a son, "and yet, when he has wept and
    lamented, he lets go: for the Fates gave mankind an
    enduring heart" (τλητὸν γὰρ Μοῖραι θυμὸν θέσαν
    ἀνθρώποισιν) — endurance as the species-defining gift.
    The close is the book's moral in five words: "it is dumb
    earth he defiles in all his fury" — the dead man is
    beyond harm; the harm is all the living man's own.

[4] (24.55-76) Hera's counter is pure rank (a breast-fed
    mortal cannot be priced with a goddess's son — with her
    swipe at Apollo's lyre-playing at the very wedding:
    "companion of evils, faithless forever"); and Zeus's
    ruling splits the difference with statesman's care: no
    equal honor — but Hector was "dearest to the gods of all
    mortals in Ilion" for the oldest reason in the poem's
    theology, the unfailing altar. The theft is rejected on
    practical grounds (Thetis's watch), but the true design
    appears in one phrase: "I attach this glory (κῦδος) to
    Achilles" — the return of the body is arranged not as
    defeat but as honor, mercy denominated in the only
    currency the recipient still values.

[5] (24.77-119) Iris's dive "like the lead sinker that rides
    the horn of a field-ox down the line" — the most homely
    simile ever given a goddess: heaven's messenger as
    fishing-tackle. Thetis's veil is the poem's blackest
    object ("there is no garment blacker"): she dresses for
    her son's death to attend a council about his mercy. On
    Olympus the courtesies are exact — Athena yields her
    place, Hera hands the cup — the divine family arranging
    itself gently around a bereaved mother whose bereavement
    has not yet happened.

[6] (24.120-142) Thetis delivers Zeus's rebuke and adds what
    no god scripted: "it is good to lie with a woman in love.
    For you will not be with me long..." (Aristarchus
    athetized 130-132 as indecorous; they are the scene's
    heart). A mother's last advice to a doomed son is bread,
    bed, and love — the whole mortal portion, urged because
    the portion is nearly spent. And Achilles' answer, after
    three books of iron: "So be it" (τῇδ' εἴη) — the wrath
    that filled an epic ends in two words of assent, the
    moment the demand comes from the one person he cannot
    refuse, carrying an honor he can accept.

[7] (24.143-187) Iris's commission to Priam repeats Zeus's
    wording verbatim (146-158 = 175-187, kept identical here):
    heaven's mercy delivered twice, unedited — the poem's
    formulaic economy made providential. The house she enters
    is a tableau of collective ruin: sons weeping in the
    courtyard, daughters and sons' wives wailing within, and
    the king wrapped in his mantle with dung on his head and
    neck, "gathered on himself with his own hands." The
    message lands on a man already performing his own burial.

[8] (24.188-228) The marriage scene: Priam tells Hecuba, and
    her answer is the poem's final maternal ferocity — the
    wish to "fasten on that man's liver, and eat it raw."
    The raw-eating triplet closes: Hera (4.35), Achilles
    (22.346), and now Hecuba — goddess of the winning side,
    killer, and grieving mother speaking one idiom; grief and
    hate converge in the same appetite. Priam's answer sets
    the book's terms: he has seen the god face to face; and
    if the price is death, "I choose it. Let Achilles kill me
    on the spot — once I have taken my son in my arms, and put
    away my fill of weeping." The embassy is a father's
    calculated exchange: his life, if need be, for one
    embrace.

[9] (24.229-264) The ransom counted out (the poem loves the
    inventory: robes, cloaks, blankets, mantles, tunics,
    twelve of each; ten talents; tripods, cauldrons, the
    Thracian cup "not even this did the old man spare") — and
    then Priam's rage: first at the Trojans ("Have you no
    mourning of your own at home?"), then the terrible roll-
    call of the nine surviving sons — "liars, dancers, heroes
    of the beaten dance-floor, plunderers of their own
    people's lambs and kids" — against the dead: Mestor,
    Troilus, "and Hector, who was a god among men." Grief in
    old men, the poem knows, comes out as fury at the living
    for not being the dead. (Among the cursed: Helenus, Paris,
    Deiphobus — the city's remaining leadership, measured and
    found wanting by their father in public.)

[10] (24.265-282) The yoking, in loving procedural detail —
    the box-wood yoke, the knob, the guide-rings, the
    nine-cubit lashing wound three times each way. The poem
    slows to craftsman's tempo exactly as it did for
    Hera's chariot and Priam's own libation to come: ritual
    care in the doing is how the Iliad marks an act as
    sacred. This wagon is an altar being assembled.

[11] (24.283-322) Hecuba's libation-counsel — having failed
    to stop him, she arms him with piety: pour to Zeus, ask
    for the eagle on the right; "but if wide-seeing Zeus will
    not give you his messenger, I at least would not urge
    you." The omen comes: the dusky hunter-eagle, wings as
    wide "as the door of some rich man's high-roofed
    treasure-chamber" — the simile drawn from precisely the
    world of stored wealth Priam has just emptied for the
    ransom. And the exit: all Troy follows the wagon
    "mourning without end, as if he were going to his death"
    — which is, as far as anyone knows, the truth.

[12] (24.322-362) Zeus's commission to Hermes defines the
    god: "to you, beyond all gods, it is dearest to be man's
    companion" (ἀνδρὶ ἑταιρίσσαι) — the escort-god, the one
    Olympian whose office is company. He comes as a princely
    youth "with the first down on his lip — the age when
    youth has its highest grace," and the meeting at the ford
    by Ilus's tomb, at dusk, is built as pure terror first:
    the herald's whisper, the old man's hair standing on his
    bent limbs — before the god takes his hand. Every earlier
    night-crossing in this poem (Book 10) ended in blood;
    the poem uses that memory to make this one tremble.

[13] (24.362-440) The interrogation is Hermes at play —
    "You are the image of my own dear father"; the invented
    Myrmidon biography (seventh son of Polyctor, chosen by
    lot) — but its center is the report on the corpse: twelve
    days dead, dragged daily, and "his flesh does not decay...
    You would marvel yourself, if you came, at how he lies
    there dewy-fresh, the blood all washed from him, no stain
    anywhere; and every wound has closed... Such is the care
    the blessed gods take for your son, a dead man though he
    is — because they loved him at heart." The kindest
    sentence in the Iliad, spoken by a disguised god to a
    father in the dark. Priam's response makes it doctrine —
    "how good a thing it is to give the immortals their due
    gifts" — with the one heartbreaking parenthesis, "if ever
    he truly was" (εἴ ποτ' ἔην γε): grief's suspicion that
    the beloved might have been a dream. Hermes' refusal of
    the cup — no gifts "behind Achilles' back" — teaches with
    a light hand the order of things: even gods respect what
    is owed to Achilles; mercy must come from the man
    himself.

[14] (24.440-468) Into the camp: sleep poured on the sentries,
    the gates opened — and the poem pauses to build Achilles'
    hut in full (fir beams, meadow-thatch, the palisade
    court) for the sake of one detail: the fir-wood bar that
    "three Achaeans would drive home and three would draw...
    though Achilles would ram it home alone." The door is a
    strength-gauge; Hermes opens it for an old man. His
    parting instructions are the supplication-manual: knees,
    and the names — father, mother, child.

[15] (24.469-485) The entrance. Priam walks in unseen (a
    god's last gift), and the recognition is staged in
    gestures before any word: knees clasped, and the kiss —
    "the terrible, man-slaughtering hands that had killed so
    many of his sons" (χεῖρας δεινὰς ἀνδροφόνους): the fixed
    epithet of Hector's hands, then Achilles', pressed now to
    a father's lips. Then the simile that turns the world
    over: as when a man who has killed someone in his own
    country arrives in exile at a rich man's hall, "and
    wonder holds all who see him" — the innocent suppliant
    king compared to a murderer, the killer cast as the
    startled host. The reversal is the scene's meaning: guilt
    and grief have changed places so many times in this poem
    that only an exchanged simile can measure the moment.
    "And the others wondered too, and looked at one another"
    — the poem's people, like its audience, checking that
    everyone is seeing this.

[16] (24.486-507) The plea. "Remember your father" — the
    argument Hermes assigned and Priam perfects: Peleus,
    same age, same encircling enemies — but with hope,
    because his son lives; "But I am doomed to the last":
    fifty sons, nineteen from one womb, and the guardian
    of all of them killed "days ago, fighting for his
    fatherland: Hector" — the name held back to the end of
    its clause. Then the two lines on which the Iliad
    closes its account of what humans can do: "I have
    endured what no other mortal on earth has yet endured —
    to lift to my lips the hand of the man who killed my
    son." Endurance — Apollo's τλητὸν θυμόν of line 49 —
    performed past its imaginable limit.

[17] (24.507-518) The weeping. Priam's words stir in
    Achilles "the desire to weep for his father" — grief
    answering grief by way of substitution — and the poem's
    quietest, deepest sentence: "the two of them remembered:
    the one wept long and sore for man-slaughtering Hector,
    huddled at Achilles' feet, and Achilles wept for his own
    father — and now again for Patroclus; and the sound of
    their mourning filled the house." Two men weeping for
    different dead in one sound: shared mourning without
    shared objects — the only communion the Iliad believes
    in, and enough. When it is spent, Achilles lifts the
    old man up by the hand, "pitying the grey head and the
    grey beard" — the exact grey head and beard Priam
    foresaw defiled by his own dogs (22.74): the image
    returns, and is pitied instead.

[18] (24.518-553) The consolation of the jars. Achilles'
    speech is the poem's theology spoken by its protagonist:
    "this is the way the gods have spun it for wretched
    mortals: to live in grief. And they themselves are free
    of care." Two jars on Zeus's floor — evils and blessings
    — and only two possible mixtures: mixed, or purely evil.
    No mortal draws unmixed good; the best life alternates.
    The proofs are the two fathers in the room: Peleus —
    wealth, kingship, a goddess-wife — and one doomed son
    who gives him "no care in his old age"; Priam — wealth
    and sons from Lesbos to the Hellespont — and this. The
    counsel is Apollo's from line 49, now spoken by the man
    it indicted: "Bear it... you will not raise him up."
    Achilles has become the teacher of the endurance he
    could not practice; saying it to Priam is how he begins
    to practice it.

[19] (24.552-573) The flash of the old fire: Priam pushes —
    no chair while Hector lies uncared-for — and Achilles
    darkens: "Do not provoke me now, old man... or I may
    not spare even you... suppliant though you are, and may
    trespass against the commands of Zeus." The rage is not
    cured; it is contained, and knows itself. That self-
    knowledge — Achilles managing his own danger like a man
    banking a fire — is what makes the mercy trustworthy:
    the poem does not sentimentalize its hero into safety;
    it shows him holding himself, barely and deliberately,
    on the right side of the line. "And the old man was
    afraid, and obeyed."

[20] (24.574-597) The ransoming, managed with the same self-
    knowledge: the body washed and anointed away from
    Priam's sight, "lest the grief in his heart not master
    his anger... and Achilles' own spirit be stirred, and he
    kill him" — mercy engineering its own conditions. Then
    Achilles himself lifts Hector onto the bier: the hands
    that dragged him now carry him — the body given back by
    the same strength that destroyed it. And the apology to
    Patroclus, with its scrupulous accounting ("the ransom
    he gave was not unworthy — and I will give you your
    share"): even now, the dead friend is a partner whose
    consent matters, and the transaction must be squared
    with the grave.

[21] (24.598-622) Niobe. The paradigm is chosen with
    terrifying precision: the mother of twelve dead children
    "remembered food" — therefore eat. Inside it, the poem's
    darkest wonder-tale: nine days the children lay in blood
    "and there was no one to bury them: the son of Cronos
    had turned the people to stones. On the tenth day the
    gods of heaven buried them" — a world petrified by grief
    until heaven itself performs the funeral; and Niobe on
    Sipylus, "stone though she is, she broods on the griefs
    the gods gave her" (614-617 were athetized by
    Aristophanes and Aristarchus — the mourner turned
    mountain, weeping forever, kept here). Eating, in this
    logic, is not the end of grief but the body's assent to
    go on being mortal: Niobe ate; Achilles ate (24.475-476;
    19's fast long broken); now Priam must. The shared meal
    is the sacrament of the enduring heart.

[22] (24.623-635) The meal and the gaze. Achilles slaughters,
    his companions dress, Automedon serves, Achilles
    portions — the host's offices performed in full for the
    father of his enemy. And then the poem's most humane
    astonishment: "Priam marveled at Achilles — how big he
    was, how beautiful: like the gods to look upon; and
    Achilles marveled at Priam... And when they had taken
    their fill of looking at each other" (the verb of
    satiety the book has used for grief and food now given
    to sight). Two men who have destroyed each other's lives
    sit and behold each other as marvels. It is the closest
    the Iliad comes to peace: not reconciliation, not
    forgiveness — just the full, mutual, unhurried sight of
    another human being.

[23] (24.636-660) Priam's first sleep-request since his son
    died ("my eyes have never closed... I have done nothing
    but groan"), and his first food ("Before this I had
    touched nothing" — his fast ending in the same hut where
    Achilles' ended). Achilles' bed-arrangement comes "with
    an edge in his voice" (ἐπικερτομέων): sleep outside,
    lest a wandering Achaean councilor see you and
    Agamemnon "delay the release of the body" — the grim
    comedy of a mercy that must be hidden from its own
    army, and a last flick at the king he quarreled with.
    Then the question no one could have predicted in Book
    22: "how many days do you need for the burial of
    brilliant Hector?"

[24] (24.661-678) The truce: nine days of mourning, the
    tenth for burial and the feast, the eleventh for the
    mound — "and on the twelfth we will fight again — if we
    must" (εἴ περ ἀνάγκη: the war resumed as necessity, not
    appetite; no one in this room wants it anymore).
    Achilles grants it with the book's definitive gesture:
    he takes the old man's right hand "at the wrist, so his
    heart should not be afraid" — the killer's hand as
    guarantee of safety. And then, in one clause with no
    comment: "and fair-cheeked Briseis lay beside him." The
    woman the wrath began over is home; Thetis's counsel
    (130-132) is kept; the poem's first quarrel closes in
    a domestic sentence.

[25] (24.679-695) Hermes' midnight warning turns the ransom
    grammar around — "for you — alive — your sons left
    behind would pay three times as much": Priam himself is
    the last treasure in the camp — and the departure
    crosses "the ford of the fair-flowing river, eddying
    Xanthus, whom immortal Zeus begot": the register's line
    (14.433-434 = 21.1-2), at its third and last station.
    Hector was revived at this ford; the rout was split
    here; now his body crosses it homeward. Hermes leaves
    at the boundary; Dawn in her saffron robe spreads over
    the whole earth; the poem's last day begins.

[26] (24.696-724) Cassandra — her only appearance in the
    poem's action, "like golden Aphrodite" — is Troy's
    watcher now (climbing Pergamus as Priam climbed the
    tower), and her cry is the city's whole relation to
    Hector in one sentence: "come and see Hector — if ever,
    while he lived, you rejoiced when he came home from
    battle." The gate-scene reprises Book 22's discovery in
    the key of ritual — wife and mother first, hands on his
    head — and Priam's crowd-order ("you may glut yourselves
    with weeping — when I have brought him home") is grief
    marshaled into procession. The bed, the singers, the
    antiphony: at last, the apparatus of proper mourning
    around the body that was promised to dogs.

[27] (24.725-749) Andromache's lament — holding "the head of
    man-slaughtering Hector in her hands": the epithet of
    the killing hands (479, 509) given now to the head a
    widow cradles; the poem's fiercest word gentled by its
    final position. Her lament is all future tense: the
    child who "will not reach manhood," the city sacked,
    the ships, the slavery — and the alternative she
    imagines for Astyanax, flung from the wall by an
    Achaean avenging what "Hector killed — his brother, or
    his father, or his son": the fate the tradition indeed
    assigns him, spoken here as a mother's reasoned dread.
    Her last grievance is the tenderest in the poem: "you
    did not die in bed, reaching your arms to me, you spoke
    no close last word that I might have remembered always"
    — not the death, but the missing goodbye.

[28] (24.750-763) Hecuba's lament is a theodicy: her sold
    sons (Samos, Imbros, "Lemnos of the smoke") against
    this one, killed, dragged — "though not even so did he
    raise him up" (the futility of the outrage stated by
    the victim's mother) — and yet lying "dewy-fresh, as if
    newly slain, like one whom Apollo of the silver bow has
    visited and killed with his gentle arrows." The gods'
    care, which Hermes reported to Priam in the dark, is
    now visible in Troy by torchlight: the body itself has
    become the proof that heaven loved him.

[29] (24.764-779) Helen's lament, third and last — the
    poem's final speech given to the woman the war was
    fought over. Twenty years from home, and her praise of
    Hector is the smallest and most exact in the three
    laments: "never once did I hear from you an evil word,
    or a slighting one" — and when others reviled her, "you
    would turn them aside... with your gentle-mindedness
    and your gentle words." She mourns him as the last
    kindness in Troy — "there is no one else now in wide
    Troy gentle to me, or kind: they all shudder at me" —
    so that the poem's cause weeps for the poem's best
    man as her only friend. The crowd's groan answers her:
    the city, for one moment, grieving with the woman it
    shudders at.

[30] (24.780-804) The ending: the truce honored (wood
    gathered nine days under Achilles' pledge), the pyre,
    rosy-fingered Dawn, the wine-quench and white bones —
    the rites following, step for step, the liturgy of
    Patroclus's funeral in Book 23: the two enemies buried
    by the same book of rites. The bones in the golden box,
    the soft purple robes, the hollow grave roofed with
    great stones, the mound raised quickly with watchers
    posted (grief efficient under threat — the war visible
    at the edges of the funeral), and the feast "in the
    house of Priam, the king nurtured of Zeus." Then the
    last line: "So they busied themselves with the burial
    of Hector, the tamer of horses" — the poem of wrath
    ends with no death, no fall, no victory: with rites
    performed, a feast eaten, and a single dead man given
    his due — the γέρας θανόντων Achilles named at 23.9.
    Everything else — Achilles' death, the horse, the sack,
    the fates Andromache numbered — stands just outside
    the final line, known to every hearer and refused
    entry. (Antiquity's rhapsodes felt the door ajar: a
    variant continuation ran "So they busied themselves
    with the burial of Hector; and there came the Amazon,
    the daughter of great-hearted Ares..." — splicing the
    epic onward into the Aethiopis. Our text ends where
    the poet ended: on the funeral, the feast, and the
    name of the man the city buried.) The Iliad begins
    with a father begging a king for his child and being
    refused; it ends with a father begging a king for his
    child and being answered. That is the distance the
    poem travels; the wrath of Achilles is the space
    between.
