BOOK 6

Hector in Troy: Glaucus and Diomedes trade armor on the field; Hector and
Andromache at the Scaean Gates.

TRANSLATOR'S COMMENTARY

Book 6 is the poem's great turn inward: the battle continues offstage while
Hector walks through his city — mother, brother, sister-in-law, wife, son —
and every meeting is a farewell taken in advance. The book's engineering is
visible and unbearable at once: Athena refuses Troy's prayer in a single
line ("But Pallas Athena turned her head away," 311 — athetized by
Aristarchus, kept here as the bleakest line in the poem), Hector answers
Andromache's plea with the same verbatim prophecy Agamemnon spoke as a
threat in Book 4 ("the day will come when holy Ilion shall perish," 448-449
— an echo the translation reproduces word for word), and the household
mourns Hector alive before he has died (500-502). Against all this the
poem sets its one scene of family comedy — the baby screaming at his
father's helmet crest, and both parents laughing (466-474).

The Glaucus-Diomedes encounter (119-236) — which some ancient critics
wanted to transpose elsewhere in the poem (note [6]) — carries the book's
other cargo: the generation-of-leaves simile, the Bellerophon story with
the poem's only reference to writing (the "baneful signs... in a folded
tablet," note [10]), and the guest-friendship that stops a duel and ends
in the proverbial exchange of gold armor for bronze.

Two register events. First, the δμώς policy adopted from the Odyssey
project goes live: δμῳαί is rendered "slave-women" at its first
occurrences (6.323, 375-376, note [21]), with ἀμφίπολος "handmaid" and
ταμίη "housekeeper" kept distinct — the vocabulary of the household the
book itself is about, in the book where Hector imagines Andromache's "day
of slavery" (463). Second, Agamemnon's atrocity-counsel at 55-60 (kill
even the unborn) is followed by the narrator's much-disputed half-line
"for his urging was of things due" (αἴσιμα παρειπών) — translated at face
value and flagged in note [3], where the interpretive dispute belongs.

  11  inside the bone; and darkness covered his eyes.

  23  Bucolion was proud Laomedon's son,[2]
  24  eldest born, though his mother bore him in darkness:
  25  shepherding his flocks he lay with the nymph in love,
  26  and she conceived, and bore him twin sons —
  27  and Mecisteus' son unstrung the strength and the shining limbs
  28  of both, and stripped the armor from their shoulders.
  29  And Polypoetes steadfast in battle killed Astyalus;
  30  and Odysseus cut down Pidytes of Percote

  32  Antilochus, Nestor's son, took Ablerus with his shining spear,
  33  and the lord of men Agamemnon took Elatus,
  34  who lived by the banks of fair-flowing Satnioeis
  35  in steep Pedasus. And the hero Leïtus caught Phylacus
  36  as he ran; and Eurypylus cut down Melanthius.

  37  Then Menelaus, good at the war-cry, took Adrestus
  38  alive: for his two horses, bolting across the plain,
  39  fouled in a tamarisk branch, snapped the curved chariot
  40  at the pole's tip, and themselves ran on
  41  toward the city, where the rest were bolting in panic,
  42  while he rolled from the car beside the wheel,
  43  face-down in the dust on his mouth. And beside him stood
  44  Atreus' son Menelaus, holding his long-shadowed spear.
  45  Then Adrestus clasped his knees and begged him:
  46  "Take me alive, son of Atreus — and take a worthy ransom.
  47  Many treasures lie stored in my rich father's house,
  48  bronze, and gold, and iron worked with much labor:
  49  out of these my father would gladden you with ransom past counting
  50  if he learned I was alive by the ships of the Achaeans."
  51  So he spoke, and was moving the heart in his chest;
  52  and soon he would have given him to his aide to lead down
  53  to the Achaeans' swift ships. But Agamemnon
  54  came running to meet him, and cried out his word:
  55  "Soft-hearted Menelaus! Why do you care so tenderly
  56  for men? Were such fine things done in your house
  57  by Trojans? Let not one of them escape sheer destruction
  58  and our hands — not even the boy his mother carries
  59  in her womb, not even he: let all of them together
  60  perish out of Ilion, unmourned, and leave no trace."
  61  So speaking the hero turned his brother's mind,
  62  for his urging was of things due; and Menelaus pushed[3]
  63  the hero Adrestus back with his hand, and the lord Agamemnon
  64  stabbed him in the flank; he doubled over, and Atreus' son
  65  set a heel on his chest and pulled out the ash spear.
  66  Then Nestor called to the Argives with a far-carrying shout:
  67  "Friends, Danaan heroes, squires of Ares —
  68  let no man now hang back for spoils,
  69  to reach the ships carrying the biggest load:
  70  let us kill men. Afterward, at your ease,
  71  you will strip the corpses dead across the plain."
  72  So speaking he stirred the fury and heart of every man.

  73  Then once more the Trojans, under the Achaeans dear to Ares,
  74  would have climbed back into Ilion, broken by their own cowardice,
  75  had not Helenus, Priam's son, far the best of bird-readers,[4]
  76  come and stood by Aeneas and Hector:
  77  "Aeneas and Hector — since on you two above all rests
  78  the labor of the Trojans and Lycians, because for every venture
  79  you are the best men, for fighting and for counsel:
  80  make your stand here; go everywhere and hold the army
  81  before the gates, before they run and fall
  82  into their women's arms, and become a joy to our enemies.
  83  Then, when you have set all the companies moving,
  84  we will hold here and fight the Danaans,
  85  hard-pressed though we are: necessity drives.
  86  But you, Hector — go to the city, and speak there
  87  to your mother and mine: let her gather the older women
  88  at grey-eyed Athena's temple on the city's height,
  89  and open with her key the doors of the holy house,
  90  and the robe that seems to her the loveliest and amplest
  91  in the hall, and far the dearest to herself,
  92  let her lay it on the knees of lovely-haired Athena,[5]
  93  and vow to sacrifice to her in the temple twelve heifers,
  94  yearlings, never goaded, if she will pity
  95  the town, and the Trojans' wives, and their little children —
  96  if she will hold back Tydeus' son from holy Ilion,
  97  that wild spearman, that strong machine of rout,
  98  who I say has proved the strongest of the Achaeans.
  99  Not even Achilles, that leader of men, ever made us fear so,
 100  who they say was born of a goddess; but this man
 101  rages beyond measure, and no one can match his fury."
 102  So he spoke, and Hector did not disobey his brother.
 103  At once he leapt in his armor from the chariot to the ground,
 104  and shaking his two sharp spears went everywhere through the army,
 105  driving men to fight, and woke the dread din of battle.
 106  And they wheeled about and stood to face the Achaeans;
 107  and the Argives gave ground and left off their killing:
 108  they thought some immortal had come down from the starry sky
 109  to stand by the Trojans, so suddenly they wheeled.
 110  And Hector called to the Trojans with a far-carrying shout:
 111  "High-hearted Trojans, and you far-famed allies —
 112  be men, my friends; remember your rushing courage,
 113  while I go up to Ilion and tell
 114  the elders of the council and our wives
 115  to pray to the divine ones, and vow them hecatombs."
 116  So speaking Hector of the flashing helmet went;
 117  and against his ankles and his neck the black oxhide beat,
 118  the rim that ran outermost on his bossed shield.

 119  Now Glaucus, Hippolochus' son, and Tydeus' son[6]
 120  came together in the space between, raging to fight.
 121  And when they were close, advancing on one another,
 122  Diomedes, good at the war-cry, spoke to him first:
 123  "Who are you, brave friend, of mortal men?
 124  I have never seen you in the battle where men win glory
 125  before this; yet now you have come out far in front of all
 126  in your daring, to stand my long-shadowed spear.
 127  Unhappy are the fathers whose sons face my fury!
 128  But if you are some immortal come down from the sky —
 129  I will not fight with heavenly gods.
 130  No — even Dryas' son, strong Lycurgus,[7]
 131  did not live long, who strove with heavenly gods:
 132  he once chased the nurses of raving Dionysus
 133  down the holy mountain of Nysa; and all together
 134  they flung their sacred wands to the ground, as man-slaughtering
 135  Lycurgus beat them with an ox-goad; and Dionysus in terror
 136  dived under the salt sea's wave, and Thetis took him,
 137  frightened, to her bosom: for a strong trembling held him
 138  at the man's shouting. But the gods who live at ease were angered
 139  at Lycurgus, and Cronos' son struck him blind; nor did he
 140  live long after, hated by all the immortal gods.
 141  I would not choose to fight the blessed gods.
 142  But if you are a mortal, one of those who eat the fruit of the field,
 143  come closer — and come the sooner to the borders of destruction."
 144  Then Hippolochus' shining son addressed him:
 145  "Great-hearted son of Tydeus — why ask my lineage?
 146  As the generation of leaves, so is the generation of men.[8]
 147  The wind pours some leaves to the ground, and others the forest
 148  grows in their fullness when the season of spring comes on:
 149  so with the generations of men — one grows, one dies away.
 150  But if you wish to hear even this, and to know
 151  my lineage — many men know it already:
 152  there is a city, Ephyra, in a fold of horse-pasturing Argos,
 153  and there lived Sisyphus, craftiest of men —[9]
 154  Sisyphus, Aeolus' son; and he fathered a son, Glaucus,
 155  and Glaucus fathered blameless Bellerophon.
 156  To him the gods gave beauty and lovely manhood;
 157  but Proetus plotted evil against him in his heart,
 158  and drove him out of the land of the Argives — being far
 159  the stronger: for Zeus had bowed them under his scepter.
 160  For Proetus' wife, brilliant Anteia, was mad
 161  to lie with him in secret love; but she could not
 162  move right-minded, war-minded Bellerophon.
 163  So she lied to King Proetus:
 164  'Die yourself, Proetus — or kill Bellerophon,
 165  who wanted to lie with me in love, and I unwilling.'
 166  So she spoke, and anger took hold of the king at what he heard.
 167  He shrank from killing him — his heart stood in awe of that —
 168  but he sent him to Lycia, and gave him baneful signs,
 169  scratching in a folded tablet many life-destroying marks,[10]
 170  and told him to show it to his wife's father: that he might perish.
 171  So he went to Lycia, under the gods' blameless escort.
 172  And when he came to Lycia and the flowing Xanthus,
 173  the lord of wide Lycia honored him with a ready heart:
 174  nine days he feasted him, and slaughtered nine oxen.
 175  But when the tenth rosy-fingered Dawn appeared,
 176  then he questioned him, and asked to see the token,
 177  whatever it was he brought from his son-in-law Proetus.
 178  And when he had received his son-in-law's evil token,
 179  first he ordered him to kill the raging Chimaera —
 180  a thing of divine stock, not of men:
 181  in front a lion, behind a serpent, in the middle a she-goat,
 182  breathing out the terrible fury of blazing fire.[11]
 183  And he killed her, trusting the portents of the gods.
 184  Second, he fought the far-famed Solymi:
 185  the hardest battle of men, he said, he ever entered.
 186  Third, he cut down the Amazons, the match of men.
 187  And as he came back the king wove him another close-set trap:
 188  choosing the best men out of wide Lycia
 189  he set an ambush; and they never came home again —
 190  blameless Bellerophon killed them all.
 191  But when the king came to know he was the brave son of a god,
 192  he kept him there, and gave him his daughter,
 193  and gave him half of all his kingly honor;
 194  and the Lycians cut him a precinct beyond all others,
 195  fine land for orchard and plowland, to enjoy.
 196  And she bore three children to war-minded Bellerophon:
 197  Isander, and Hippolochus, and Laodameia.
 198  By Laodameia Zeus the counselor lay,
 199  and she bore godlike Sarpedon of the bronze helmet.
 200  But when even Bellerophon came to be hated by all the gods,
 201  he wandered alone across the Aleian plain,
 202  eating out his heart, shunning the trodden paths of men;[12]
 203  and his son Isander was killed by Ares insatiate of war
 204  as he fought the far-famed Solymi;
 205  and his daughter — Artemis of the golden reins killed her in anger.
 206  But Hippolochus fathered me: from him I claim my birth.
 207  He sent me to Troy, and he charged me strictly
 208  always to be the best, and to hold my head above all others,
 209  and not to shame the stock of my fathers, who were far the best
 210  in Ephyra, and in wide Lycia.
 211  Of this lineage and this blood I claim to be."
 212  So he spoke; and Diomedes, good at the war-cry, rejoiced.
 213  He planted his spear in the earth that feeds so many
 214  and spoke with honeyed words to the shepherd of the people:
 215  "Why — then you are my guest-friend from our fathers' time![13]
 216  For brilliant Oeneus once entertained blameless
 217  Bellerophon in his halls, and kept him twenty days;
 218  and they gave one another fine gifts of friendship:
 219  Oeneus gave a war-belt bright with crimson,
 220  and Bellerophon a golden two-handled cup —
 221  I left it in my house when I came away.
 222  Tydeus I do not remember: I was still small
 223  when he left me, that time the Achaean host perished at Thebes.
 224  So now I am your dear guest-friend in the heart of Argos,
 225  and you are mine in Lycia, whenever I come to that country.
 226  Let us keep clear of each other's spears, even in the crush:
 227  there are Trojans enough for me, and famed allies,
 228  to kill whomever a god delivers and my feet overtake,
 229  and Achaeans enough for you, to cut down whom you can.
 230  And let us exchange armor with each other, so that these men too
 231  may know we claim to be guest-friends from our fathers' time."
 232  So the two spoke, and leapt down from their chariots
 233  and took each other's hands and pledged their faith.
 234  And then Zeus, Cronos' son, took Glaucus's wits away:
 235  he exchanged with Tydeus' son Diomedes armor
 236  of gold for bronze — a hundred oxen's worth for nine.[14]

 237  Now when Hector came to the Scaean Gates and the oak,[15]
 238  the Trojans' wives and daughters came running around him,
 239  asking after sons and brothers and kinsmen
 240  and husbands. And he told them to pray to the gods,
 241  all of them, one after another: griefs hung over many.
 242  But when he came to Priam's beautiful palace,
 243  built with polished colonnades — and in it
 244  were fifty chambers of polished stone,[16]
 245  built close by one another, where the sons
 246  of Priam slept beside their wedded wives,
 247  and for the daughters, opposite, within the court,
 248  were twelve roofed chambers of polished stone,
 249  built close by one another, where the sons-in-law
 250  of Priam slept beside their honored wives —
 251  there his gentle-giving mother came to meet him,
 252  leading in Laodice, loveliest of her daughters;
 253  and she clung to his hand, and spoke a word, and named him:
 254  "Child, why have you left the bold fighting and come here?
 255  Surely they are wearing you down — those cursed sons
 256  of the Achaeans, fighting around the town — and your heart sent you
 257  here, to lift your hands to Zeus from the city's height.
 258  But wait while I bring you honey-sweet wine,
 259  to pour first to father Zeus and the other immortals,
 260  and then to do yourself good, if you will drink.
 261  Wine greatly swells the strength of a wearied man —
 262  and you are wearied with defending your kinsmen."
 263  Then great Hector of the flashing helmet answered her:
 264  "Bring me no wine, honey-hearted lady mother,
 265  or you may unstring my limbs and I forget my courage.
 266  And I shrink from pouring gleaming wine to Zeus[17]
 267  with unwashed hands: a man spattered with blood and filth
 268  cannot pray to the son of Cronos, lord of the dark cloud.
 269  No — you go to the temple of Athena, driver of the spoil,
 270  with burnt offerings, when you have gathered the older women,
 271  and the robe that is loveliest and amplest
 272  in the hall, and far the dearest to yourself,
 273  lay it on the knees of lovely-haired Athena,
 274  and vow to sacrifice to her in the temple twelve heifers,
 275  yearlings, never goaded, if she will pity
 276  the town, and the Trojans' wives, and their little children —
 277  if she will hold back Tydeus' son from holy Ilion,
 278  that wild spearman, that strong machine of rout.
 279  So go to the temple of Athena, driver of the spoil;
 280  and I will go after Paris, to summon him,
 281  if he is willing to hear me speak. May the earth gape for him
 282  where he stands! The Olympian reared him as a great affliction
 283  to the Trojans, and to great-hearted Priam, and Priam's sons.
 284  If I could see that man gone down into Hades' house,
 285  I could say my heart had forgotten its joyless misery."
 286  So he spoke; and she went to the hall and called
 287  her handmaids, and they gathered the older women through the city.
 288  She herself went down to the fragrant storeroom
 289  where her robes were kept, the richly patterned work of women
 290  of Sidon, whom godlike Alexandros himself[18]
 291  brought from Sidon, sailing the wide sea,
 292  on that voyage on which he carried home high-born Helen.
 293  Of these Hecuba took one to carry as gift to Athena,
 294  the one that was loveliest in its patterning, and amplest,
 295  and it shone like a star; it lay beneath all the others.
 296  Then she set out, and the many older women hurried after.
 297  And when they came to Athena's temple on the city's height,
 298  fair-cheeked Theano opened the doors for them,
 299  Cisseus' daughter, wife of Antenor, breaker of horses,
 300  whom the Trojans had made priestess of Athena.[19]
 301  With a wailing cry they all lifted their hands to Athena;
 302  and fair-cheeked Theano took the robe
 303  and laid it on the knees of lovely-haired Athena,
 304  and vowing she prayed to the daughter of great Zeus:
 305  "Lady Athena, guardian of the city, brilliant among goddesses:
 306  break now the spear of Diomedes, and grant
 307  that the man himself fall on his face before the Scaean Gates,
 308  and we will sacrifice to you at once, here in the temple,
 309  twelve heifers, yearlings, never goaded, if you will pity
 310  the town, and the Trojans' wives, and their little children."
 311  So she spoke, vowing. But Pallas Athena turned her head away.[20]

 312  So they were praying to the daughter of great Zeus;
 313  and Hector had gone to the house of Alexandros,
 314  the beautiful house he had built himself with the men
 315  who were then the best builders in deep-soiled Troy:
 316  they had made him a chamber and a hall and a courtyard
 317  close by Priam and Hector, on the city's height.
 318  There Hector entered, dear to Zeus, and in his hand
 319  he held a spear eleven cubits long; and before him the bronze
 320  point of the shaft shone, and a ring of gold ran round it.
 321  He found Paris in the chamber busy with his beautiful armor,
 322  the shield and the corslet, and handling his curved bow;
 323  and Argive Helen sat among her slave-women,[21]
 324  directing her handmaids in their famous work.
 325  And seeing him Hector raked him with shaming words:
 326  "Strange man — it is not decent to nurse this anger in your heart.
 327  The people are dying around the city and the steep wall
 328  as they fight; and it is for your sake that the war-cry and the war
 329  burn around this town. You would quarrel yourself
 330  with any man you saw hanging back from hateful war.
 331  Up — before the town is warmed by the enemy's fire!"
 332  And godlike Alexandros answered him:
 333  "Hector, you rake me justly, not more than justly;
 334  so I will tell you — and you mark me, and hear me out.
 335  It was not so much anger at the Trojans, or resentment,
 336  that kept me sitting in my chamber: I wanted to give in to grief.
 337  But just now my wife, coaxing me with soft words,
 338  urged me back to the war; and it seems even to me
 339  that it will be better so. Victory shifts from man to man.
 340  Come then, wait while I put on the gear of Ares —
 341  or go, and I will follow: I think I shall overtake you."
 342  So he spoke; and Hector of the flashing helmet answered him nothing.
 343  But Helen spoke to Hector with honeyed words:
 344  "Brother — brother of me, the dog, the evil-scheming horror:[22]
 345  how I wish that on the day my mother first bore me
 346  some foul storm of wind had carried me off
 347  to a mountain, or into the wave of the loud-roaring sea,
 348  where the wave would have swept me away before all this was done.
 349  But since the gods ordained these evils so,
 350  then I wish I had been wife to a better man,
 351  one who felt the world's anger and the long reproaches of men.
 352  But this one — his wits are not steady now, and never
 353  will be; and I think he will reap the harvest of it.
 354  But come in now, brother, and sit on this chair,
 355  since the labor rings your heart above all others' —
 356  for the sake of me, the dog, and for Alexandros's blindness:
 357  on whom Zeus has set an evil doom, so that hereafter
 358  we may be subjects of song for men still to come."[23]
 359  Then great Hector of the flashing helmet answered her:
 360  "Do not seat me, Helen, though you love me. You will not persuade me.
 361  Already my heart is driving me to stand by
 362  the Trojans, who feel their great longing for me gone.
 363  No — you rouse this man, and let him hurry himself,
 364  so he may catch me still inside the city.
 365  For I am going home first, to see
 366  my household, and my dear wife, and my little son:
 367  I do not know if I shall come back to them again,
 368  or whether the gods will break me now under Achaean hands."
 369  So speaking Hector of the flashing helmet went.
 370  And quickly he came to his well-settled house —
 371  but he did not find white-armed Andromache in the hall:
 372  she had gone with the child and one fair-robed handmaid
 373  and stood on the tower, weeping and mourning.
 374  When Hector did not find his blameless wife within,
 375  he stopped on the threshold and spoke among the slave-women:
 376  "Come, slave-women, tell me the truth of it:
 377  where has white-armed Andromache gone from the hall?
 378  To one of my sisters, or my brothers' fair-robed wives?
 379  Or out to Athena's temple, where the other
 380  lovely-braided women of Troy are appeasing the dread goddess?"
 381  And the busy housekeeper answered him:
 382  "Hector — since you order me strictly to tell the truth:
 383  not to one of your sisters, nor your brothers' fair-robed wives,
 384  nor out to Athena's temple, where the other
 385  lovely-braided women of Troy are appeasing the dread goddess —
 386  she went up on the great tower of Ilion, because she heard
 387  the Trojans were worn down, and the Achaeans' power was great.
 388  She has gone hurrying to the wall
 389  like a woman gone mad; and the nurse carries the child beside her."
 390  So the housekeeper spoke; and Hector sped from the house
 391  back the same way, down the well-built streets.
 392  When, crossing the great city, he reached the gates —
 393  the Scaean Gates, by which he meant to go out on the plain —
 394  there his richly dowered wife came running to meet him,
 395  Andromache, daughter of great-hearted Eëtion —
 396  Eëtion, who lived under wooded Placus,
 397  in Thebe-under-Placus, lord over the Cilician men:
 398  his daughter was wife to Hector of the bronze helmet.
 399  She met him there, and with her came the handmaid
 400  holding the child at her breast, all baby-thoughts still, a little one,
 401  the beloved son of Hector, like a beautiful star:
 402  Hector called him Scamandrius, but the others
 403  Astyanax — for Hector alone was the saving of Ilion.[24]
 404  He looked at his boy and smiled in silence;
 405  but Andromache came and stood close beside him, weeping,
 406  and she clung to his hand, and spoke a word, and named him:
 407  "Strange man — your own fury will destroy you. And you have no pity
 408  for your son, a little child, or for doomed me, who soon
 409  shall be your widow: for soon the Achaeans will kill you,
 410  falling on you all together. And for me it would be better,
 411  losing you, to go under the earth: there will be no other
 412  comfort ever again, once you have met your doom —
 413  only griefs. And I have no father, no lady mother.
 414  My father — brilliant Achilles killed him,
 415  the day he sacked the well-settled city of the Cilicians,
 416  Thebe of the high gates. He killed Eëtion —
 417  but he did not strip him: his heart stood in awe of that.[25]
 418  He burned his body in the wrought armor
 419  and heaped a grave-mound over him; and around it the mountain nymphs,
 420  daughters of aegis-bearing Zeus, planted elm trees.
 421  And my seven brothers in our halls —
 422  all of them went to Hades' house in a single day:
 423  swift-footed brilliant Achilles killed them all
 424  among their rolling-gaited cattle and their white sheep.
 425  And my mother, who was queen under wooded Placus —
 426  he brought her here with the rest of the plunder,
 427  but set her free again, taking ransom past counting,
 428  and in her father's halls Artemis who showers arrows struck her down.
 429  Hector — so you are my father, and my lady mother,
 430  and my brother; and you are my strong young husband.[26]
 431  Come, take pity now, and stay here on the tower:
 432  do not make your child an orphan and your wife a widow.
 433  And station the army by the wild fig tree, where the city
 434  is easiest to scale, and the wall lies open to assault.[27]
 435  Three times their best men have come there and tried it,
 436  the two Ajaxes and famous Idomeneus together,
 437  and the sons of Atreus, and Tydeus' powerful son —
 438  whether someone skilled in god-signs told them of it,
 439  or their own hearts drive and command them to it."
 440  Then great Hector of the flashing helmet answered her:
 441  "All this is my care too, wife. But I would stand
 442  in terrible shame before the Trojans, and the Trojan women
 443  with their trailing gowns, if like a coward I skulked apart from the war;
 444  and my own heart will not let me. I have learned to be brave
 445  always, and to fight among the foremost Trojans,
 446  winning great fame for my father, and for myself.
 447  For this I know well in my mind and heart:
 448  the day will come when holy Ilion shall perish,[28]
 449  and Priam, and the people of Priam of the good ash spear.
 450  But it is not the Trojans' coming grief that so much moves me —
 451  not Hecuba's own, not lord Priam's,
 452  not my brothers', who many and brave
 453  may fall in the dust under enemy hands —
 454  as yours: when some bronze-shirted Achaean
 455  leads you away in tears, and takes your day of freedom;
 456  and in Argos you would weave at another woman's loom,
 457  and carry water from the spring Messeïs or Hypereia,
 458  hating it, every step — and hard necessity on you;
 459  and someone will say, seeing you letting fall your tears:
 460  'This is the wife of Hector, who was best in battle
 461  of all the horse-taming Trojans, when they fought around Ilion.'
 462  So someone will say; and for you it will be fresh grief,
 463  for want of such a man to beat off the day of slavery.[29]
 464  But may the heaped earth cover me, dead,
 465  before I hear your cry and see you dragged away."
 466  So speaking, glorious Hector reached out for his boy —
 467  and the child shrank back, crying, against the breast
 468  of his fair-belted nurse, terrified at the sight of his own father,
 469  frightened by the bronze and the horsehair crest,
 470  seeing it nod terribly from the helmet's top.
 471  And his dear father laughed aloud, and his lady mother;[30]
 472  at once glorious Hector took the helmet from his head
 473  and laid it, all shining, on the ground.
 474  Then he kissed his dear son, and swung him in his arms,
 475  and spoke in prayer to Zeus and the other gods:
 476  "Zeus, and you other gods: grant that this boy,
 477  my son, may become, as I am, preeminent among the Trojans,
 478  as strong and brave as I; and rule Ilion in strength;
 479  and may men someday say he is far better than his father
 480  as he comes home from war. May he carry back the bloody spoils
 481  of the enemy he has killed, and his mother's heart be glad."[31]
 482  So speaking he laid his son in the arms
 483  of his dear wife; and she took him to her fragrant breast,
 484  laughing through her tears. And her husband saw, and pitied her,
 485  and stroked her with his hand, and spoke a word, and named her:
 486  "Strange woman — do not grieve too much for me in your heart.
 487  No man will hurl me to Hades beyond my portion;
 488  but his fate, I say, no man has ever escaped,
 489  coward or brave man, once he has been born.[32]
 490  Go home now, and set your own work in order —
 491  the loom and the distaff — and tell your handmaids
 492  to go about their work. War shall be men's concern —
 493  all men's, and mine above all, of all who were born in Ilion."[33]
 494  So speaking, glorious Hector took up the helmet
 495  with its horsehair crest; and his dear wife went home,
 496  turning back again and again, letting the full tears fall.
 497  And quickly she came to the well-settled house
 498  of man-slaughtering Hector, and found inside her many
 499  handmaids; and in all of them she stirred lament.
 500  So they mourned Hector, still alive, in his own house:
 501  for they said he would never again come back
 502  out of the war, escaping the fury and the hands of the Achaeans.[34]

 503  Nor did Paris linger long in his high house:
 504  when he had put on his famed armor, patterned with bronze,
 505  he ran through the city, sure of his quick feet.
 506  As when a stalled horse, corn-fed at the manger,
 507  breaks his tether and gallops drumming across the plain,
 508  used to bathing in the fair-flowing river,
 509  glorying: he holds his head high, and the mane
 510  streams along his shoulders; sure of his own splendor,
 511  his knees carry him lightly to the haunts and pastures of horses:[35]
 512  so Paris, Priam's son, came striding down from the height
 513  of Pergamos, blazing in his armor like the beaming sun,
 514  laughing aloud, and his quick feet carried him. And at once
 515  he came on brilliant Hector, his brother, just turning away
 516  from the place where he had talked with his wife.
 517  Godlike Alexandros spoke to him first:
 518  "Elder brother — surely I have held you back in your hurry,
 519  lingering so long; I did not come promptly, as you asked."
 520  Answering him spoke Hector of the flashing helmet:
 521  "Strange man — no fair-minded man could disparage
 522  your work in battle: you have your courage.
 523  But you hang back by choice; you will not try. And my heart
 524  aches in me when I hear your shame on Trojan lips —
 525  the men who bear this long labor for your sake.
 526  But let us go. We will make all this good later, if ever Zeus
 527  grants us to set out for the heavenly gods who are forever
 528  the mixing-bowl of freedom in our halls,
 529  when we have driven the strong-greaved Achaeans out of Troy."

Notes

[1] (6.12-19) Axylus of Arisbe, "a friend to all mankind," who kept open
    house by the road — and not one guest stood by him at his death. The
    hospitality-vignette inverted: xenia builds a network of obligation,
    and the poem records the one moment it cannot pay out.

[2] (6.21-28) Bucolion, Laomedon's eldest son "borne in darkness"
    (σκότιον — illegitimate), and the water-nymph Abarbarea: a shepherd's
    love-story compressed into five lines so that its twin sons can die
    in two. Nymph-brides on Mount Ida are a recurring Trojan genealogy
    (cf. Aeneas's own conception, 2.820-821).

[3] (6.45-65) The Adrestus scene: a ransom-supplication (the standard
    battlefield transaction, cf. 1.12-13) refused in the war's most
    chilling terms — "not even the boy his mother carries in her womb."
    The narrator's comment, αἴσιμα παρειπών, is rendered at face value
    ("for his urging was of things due") and has been disputed since
    antiquity: does the poet endorse the atrocity as just payment for
    Paris's crime, or does αἴσιμα mean only "words of destiny"? The
    translation does not soften it; the dispute lives here.

[4] (6.76) Helenus, Priam's son, "far the best of bird-readers" — the
    Trojan counterpart of Calchas (1.69, same formula); distinct from
    the Helenus killed at 5.707.

[5] (6.90-97) The peplos-offering: a woven robe laid on the knees of the
    seated cult-statue, with a vow of twelve unworked heifers — the
    fullest description of civic ritual supplication in the poem. The
    instruction is relayed verbatim three times (Helenus 90-97, Hector
    269-278, performed 293-310), per messenger convention; the English
    repeats identically.

[6] (6.119-236) The Glaucus-Diomedes encounter. Aristonicus reports that
    some ancient critics transposed the whole episode elsewhere in the
    poem (apparatus, Line 119-236) — it sits oddly in mid-battle, two
    enemies talking genealogy at leisure. Kept, of course, where the
    tradition kept it: its meditation on lineage, mortality, and
    obligation is the hinge between the battlefield and Troy's interiors.

[7] (6.130-140) Lycurgus who chased Dionysus's nurses with an ox-goad —
    the Iliad's only Dionysus narrative (the god is strikingly marginal
    in Homer), told as a cautionary paradigm against god-fighting.
    Thetis's sea-rescue of the terrified child is her third such rescue
    in the poem's memory (Zeus at 1.396-406, Hephaestus at 18.398).

[8] (6.146-149) "As the generation of leaves, so is the generation of
    men" — the most famous simile in the poem, and the classic statement
    of its view of mortality; quoted, imitated, and answered from
    Mimnermus to the present. Glaucus's point is scornful — why ask
    lineage? — and he then gives sixty lines of it: the tension is the
    speech.

[9] (6.152-155) Sisyphus "craftiest of men" — here simply a clever
    Corinthian ancestor; the Iliad knows nothing (or says nothing) of
    the underworld stone, which appears first in Odyssey 11.

[10] (6.160-170) Proetus's wife Anteia and the lethal letter: the
     oldest Greek instance of the "Potiphar's wife" motif — and the
     "baneful signs... scratched in a folded tablet" are the only
     reference to writing in all of Homer. Whether the poet imagined
     true script or ominous symbols was already debated in antiquity;
     the translation keeps the vagueness ("marks").

[11] (6.179-186) Bellerophon's labors: the Chimaera (lion-serpent-goat,
     fire-breathing — the poem's canonical description), the Solymi,
     the Amazons "the match of men" (cf. 3.189). Pegasus is
     conspicuously absent: Homer's Bellerophon does his killing without
     the winged horse of later art.

[12] (6.200-205) Bellerophon's end: "hated by all the gods," wandering
     the Aleian plain ("the Plain of Wandering" — the name puns on his
     roaming), "eating out his heart" — the poem's image of a man
     dropped by fortune, cause unstated. His son falls to Ares, his
     daughter to Artemis: in Homeric idiom, sudden death of a man is
     Ares' or Apollo's arrow, of a woman Artemis's (cf. 6.428).

[13] (6.215-231) The discovery: Diomedes' grandfather hosted Glaucus's
     grandfather for twenty days, therefore the grandsons may not fight
     — guest-friendship binds across generations and across the war
     itself. They will kill other men's guest-friends all afternoon;
     the institution is particular, not humanitarian. The gift-list
     (war-belt for cup) is a miniature of the economy.

[14] (6.234-236) "Zeus took Glaucus's wits away": gold armor for bronze,
     "a hundred oxen's worth for nine" — proverbial by Plato's time for
     a bad bargain. The narrator's only intrusion into the scene, and a
     famously debated one: divine folly, or the reckless magnificence
     that is exactly what Glaucus's speech about glory demanded?

[15] (6.237) "the Scaean Gates and the oak" — the oak of Zeus (cf.
     5.693), a fixed landmark of the battlefield's edge; some
     manuscripts read "tower" for "oak" (apparatus, Line 237).

[16] (6.242-250) Priam's palace: fifty chambers for sons, twelve for
     daughters and sons-in-law — dynastic architecture, the physical
     form of Priam's abundance that the war is grinding away (cf. his
     own count of lost sons at 24.493-498).

[17] (6.264-268) Hector's double scruple — wine would slacken him;
     blood-spattered hands cannot pour to Zeus: battlefield piety in
     its exact ritual grammar (cf. the hand-washing at 1.449, 3.270).

[18] (6.289-295) The Sidonian robes: Phoenician luxury goods brought
     home by Paris "on that voyage on which he carried home high-born
     Helen" — the abduction had a shopping itinerary. The offering to
     Athena is thus chosen from the very cargo of the crime.

[19] (6.298-300) Theano (cf. 5.70) as priestess of Athena "whom the
     Trojans had made" so — a civic appointment, the only such notice
     in Homer, and evidence that Athena has an established cult inside
     the city she is destroying.

[20] (6.311) "But Pallas Athena turned her head away." Aristarchus
     athetized the line (apparatus); it is the bleakest single verse in
     the poem — the exact ritual performed exactly, and refused in
     four words. Kept, emphatically: without it the whole peplos
     sequence loses its point.

[21] (6.323-324, 375-376) First occurrences of δμῳαί, rendered
     "slave-women" per the project's adopted policy (never "servants"
     or "maids"); ἀμφίπολος remains "handmaid," ταμίη "housekeeper,"
     τιθήνη "nurse." The household vocabulary matters in this book
     above all: its climax turns on Andromache's foreseen "day of
     slavery" (463).

[22] (6.344-356) Helen to Hector: "me, the dog, the evil-scheming
     horror" — her third self-directed dog-insult (3.180, 3.404's
     "hateful me"); no one in the poem abuses Helen except Helen. At
     356 the printed text has "Alexandros's blindness" (ἄτης) —
     Zenodotus read ἀρχῆς, "Alexandros who began it" (apparatus; cf.
     3.100). The ἄτη-reading ties Paris to the poem's key word for
     ruinous delusion.

[23] (6.357-358) "so that hereafter we may be subjects of song for men
     still to come" — the poem explaining its own existence through
     Helen's mouth: their suffering is Zeus's device for making poetry.
     Cf. Alcinous's version of the same thought at Odyssey 8.579.

[24] (6.402-403) The child's two names: Hector calls him Scamandrius
     (after the river), the city calls him Astyanax — "lord of the
     city" — "for Hector alone was the saving of Ilion." The people's
     name for the son is their thanks to the father; its bitter payoff
     comes at 22.506-507. Third instance of the double-naming motif
     (cf. 1.403-404, 2.813-814), here mortal/civic rather than
     divine/human.

[25] (6.414-428) Andromache's family history is Achilles' work in its
     entirety: father killed (but honored — burned in his armor, mound
     heaped, elms planted by nymphs: even his enemies' awe, σεβάσσατο),
     seven brothers in one day, mother ransomed then dead. The raid on
     Thebe is the poem's economic backstory (Chryseis was taken there,
     1.366-369; Briseis's city Lyrnessus fell in the same campaign,
     2.690-691). When Achilles and Andromache's griefs finally meet, in
     Book 24, they will already share every dead person in them.

[26] (6.429-430) "you are my father, and my lady mother, and my
     brother; and you are my strong young husband" — arithmetic of
     the preceding catalogue: everything the war has taken, refigured
     in the one person it is about to take.

[27] (6.433-439) Andromache's tactical advice — mass the army at the
     wild fig tree where the wall is scalable and thrice-probed.
     Aristarchus athetized the lines (apparatus, Line 433-439), on the
     ancient view that military counsel was unfitting in her mouth.
     Kept: the poem's women know exactly how Troy falls; the fig tree
     recurs as a tactical landmark at 11.167 and 22.145.

[28] (6.447-449) "the day will come when holy Ilion shall perish, and
     Priam, and the people of Priam of the good ash spear" — verbatim
     the lines Agamemnon spoke at 4.163-165. There they were a threat
     sworn in anger; here Hector speaks them as settled knowledge, and
     fights on anyway. The reuse is the measure of the man. (Echo
     note; English repeated word for word.)

[29] (6.454-465) The enslaved future, imagined by the husband: the
     loom of another woman, the water of Messeïs or Hypereia, the
     stranger's comment on her tears. "Day of freedom" / "day of
     slavery" (ἐλεύθερον ἦμαρ / δούλιον ἦμαρ) — the Homeric idiom that
     makes a condition a date. Hector's wish to be dead first will be
     granted exactly.

[30] (6.466-474) The helmet scene: the baby screaming at the nodding
     crest, and both parents laughing — the poem's only moment of
     family comedy, set precisely between prophecy and farewell. The
     flashing-helmet epithet the poem has attached to Hector some
     forty times becomes, for four lines, a thing his son cannot
     recognize him through.

[31] (6.476-481) Hector's prayer: "may men someday say he is far
     better than his father... and his mother's heart be glad." The
     tradition beyond the poem answers with the child thrown from the
     walls; the Iliad says nothing of it, and the silence is part of
     the effect. Even within the prayer, joy is measured in "bloody
     spoils."

[32] (6.486-489) "his fate, I say, no man has ever escaped, coward or
     brave man, once he has been born" — the consolation of shared
     mortality, Hector's plainest theology; the same thought Achilles
     will offer Priam in Book 24 at far greater length.

[33] (6.490-493) "the loom and the distaff... War shall be men's
     concern" — the fixed formula dividing the spheres (Hector will be
     echoed nearly verbatim by Telemachus, with "speech" for "war," at
     Odyssey 1.356-359). Coming directly after note [27]'s athetized
     tactics, the line enacts the boundary the critics wanted enforced.

[34] (6.500-502) "So they mourned Hector, still alive, in his own
     house" — the lament raised before the death; the poem lets the
     funeral of Book 24 begin here, eighteen books early.

[35] (6.506-511) The stalled horse: Paris released, glorying, mane
     streaming — repeated verbatim at 15.263-268, where it describes
     Hector returning to battle; the same simile serving vanity there
     and heroism here is a masterclass in how formulas mean by
     placement. English fixed for the recurrence.
