BOOK 8

Zeus bans the gods from the battle; the golden scales tip against the
Achaeans; the Trojans camp on the plain under a thousand watchfires.

TRANSLATOR'S COMMENTARY

Book 8 exists to reverse the war's flow and set up everything that
follows: Zeus takes the field-glass seat on Ida, forbids divine
interference on pain of Tartarus, tips his golden scales, and by
nightfall the Trojans — for the first time in ten years — are camped in
the open plain. Ancient critics disliked the book intensely (its
nickname was "the docked battle"), and the apparatus shows the highest
athetesis-density of any book so far: the golden-cord boast, Zeus's
smiling "I am not in earnest," the four named horses, Andromache's
fodder, the whole Iris relay, the Heracles digression, and even the
shepherd's gladness in the watchfire simile all drew critical daggers.
This translation keeps the printed text entire and records each mark;
what the critics read as padding largely reads now as the book's
system: everything in it — the cord, the scales, the thunder repeated
three times, the prophecy — performs Zeus's singular, uncontested weight.

Two disclosures anchor the book. At 475-478 Zeus names the plot of the
rest of the poem: Hector will not stop "until the swift-footed son of
Peleus rises beside the ships... over Patroclus fallen" — the first
explicit statement of Patroclus's death, delivered as decree eight books
before the event (note [26]). And at 161-166 stands the one passage
flagged for this book in disputed_lines.md: Hector's taunt to Diomedes
("you were a woman after all... painted doll"), athetized by
Aristophanes and Aristarchus, with Zenodotus's variant close (note
[10]). The book ends on the poem's most famous nocturne — the
watchfires like stars around the moon, the shepherd glad at heart (a
gladness Zenodotus deleted), a thousand fires, fifty men at each, and
the horses waiting for Dawn (notes [30], [31]).

Repetition discipline mattered here: the Athena arming scene reuses
Book 5's lines and this translation's Book 5 English verbatim; Iris
relays Zeus's threat word for word and adds one line of her own venom;
and the Hera-Athena murmuring scene repeats 4.20-24 exactly. New
register entries: saffron-robed Dawn, "much-enduring brilliant
Odysseus" (first Iliad occurrence, in the awkward scene where he fails
to hear Diomedes — note [7]), the golden scales, and "fair-throned
Dawn" closing the book.

   2  when Zeus who delights in thunder made an assembly of the gods
   3  on the topmost peak of many-ridged Olympus;
   4  and he spoke to them himself, and all the gods gave ear:

   6  so I may say what the heart in my chest commands me.
   7  Let no female god attempt — and no male either —
   8  to cut across this word of mine: all of you together

  12  struck out of all order he shall come back to Olympus —
  13  or I will take and hurl him into misty Tartarus,
  14  far down, where the deepest pit yawns under the earth,
  15  where the gates are iron and the threshold bronze,
  16  as far beneath Hades as heaven is above the earth:[2]
  17  then he will know how far I am strongest of all gods.

  24  I would haul you up, and the earth and the sea with you;
  25  then I would bind the cord around a horn of Olympus,
  26  and the whole of it would hang in the air.
  27  By so much am I beyond gods, and beyond men."[3]
  28  So he spoke, and they all went still in silence,

  51  and he sat on the peaks, exulting in his glory,

  64  There rose together the groaning and the boasting of men
  65  killing and killed, and the earth ran with blood.

  71  one for the horse-taming Trojans, one for the bronze-shirted Achaeans,

  73  The Achaeans' fates settled down on the earth
  74  that feeds so many, and the Trojans' rose toward the wide sky.
  75  And he thundered aloud from Ida, and sent a blazing
  76  flash among the Achaean host; and they saw it

  83  on the very crown of the head, where a horse's first hairs

  85  It reared in agony as the shaft went into the brain,

  89  through the rout, carrying their bold driver —
  90  Hector. And there the old man would have lost his life,

  95  Take care no one plants a spear in that back as you flee!

 101  and speaking winged words he addressed him:
 102  "Old man, the young fighters are wearing you hard;
 103  your force is unstrung, and hard old age attends you;
 104  your aide is feeble now, and your horses slow.
 105  Come, get up into my car, and see
 106  what the horses of Tros are like, how they know their plain —[8]
 107  how to wheel fast this way and that, in pursuit or flight.
 108  I took the pair from Aeneas — those two machines of rout.
 109  Let our aides look after yours; these two
 110  we will drive straight at the horse-taming Trojans, so that even Hector
 111  may know whether my spear too rages in my hands."
 112  So he spoke, and the Gerenian horseman Nestor did not disobey.
 113  Then their two aides looked after Nestor's mares,
 114  strong Sthenelus, and Eurymedon the kindly;
 115  and the two of them mounted Diomedes' chariot.
 116  Nestor took the glossy reins into his hands
 117  and whipped the horses; and quickly they closed with Hector.
 118  Straight at him, as he charged, the son of Tydeus cast:
 119  him he missed — but his driver and aide,
 120  Eniopeus, son of high-hearted Thebaeus,
 121  he struck in the chest by the nipple as he held the reins.
 122  He crashed from the car, and the swift-footed horses
 123  shied back; and there his life and strength were unstrung.
 124  And terrible grief closed over Hector's heart for his driver;
 125  but he left him lying there, though grieving for his companion,
 126  and went after a bold driver in his place; nor did his horses
 127  lack a master long: for soon he found
 128  bold Archeptolemus, Iphitus' son, and set him up
 129  behind the swift horses, and gave the reins into his hands.
 130  And now there would have been ruin, deeds beyond remedy,
 131  and they would have been penned in Ilion like lambs,
 132  had not the father of men and gods been quick to see.
 133  Thundering terribly he let fly a white-hot bolt
 134  and dashed it to the ground before the horses of Diomedes;
 135  and a terrible flame rose up of burning sulphur,
 136  and the two horses, terrified, cowered under the car.
 137  The glossy reins slipped out of Nestor's hands,
 138  and he was afraid at heart, and said to Diomedes:
 139  "Tydeus' son — come, turn the single-hoofed horses to flight.
 140  Do you not see? Victory out of Zeus does not go with you.
 141  Today it is that man Zeus, Cronos' son, hands the glory —
 142  today; later, if he chooses, he will give it
 143  to us in turn. No man can fend off the purpose of Zeus,
 144  not even the strongest: for he is stronger by far."[9]
 145  Then answered him Diomedes, good at the war-cry:
 146  "Yes, old man — all this you have said rightly and in due measure.
 147  But this bitter grief comes over my heart and spirit:
 148  Hector one day will say, speaking among the Trojans:
 149  'Tydeus' son ran before me to his ships.'
 150  So he will boast one day. Then let the wide earth gape for me."
 151  Then answered him the Gerenian horseman Nestor:
 152  "Ah me, war-minded Tydeus' son — what a thing to say.
 153  Even if Hector calls you coward and weakling,
 154  the Trojans will never believe him, nor the Dardanians,
 155  nor the wives of the great-hearted Trojan shieldmen
 156  whose husbands in their strength you tumbled in the dust."
 157  So speaking he turned the single-hoofed horses to flight
 158  back through the rout; and the Trojans and Hector after them
 159  with unearthly clamor poured their groaning missiles.
 160  And over him great Hector of the flashing helmet shouted:
 161  "Tydeus' son — the Danaans of swift colts honored you
 162  beyond all others, with the seat, the meats, the full cups.
 163  Now they will dishonor you: you were a woman after all.
 164  Away with you, painted doll! Never, with me not yielding,
 165  shall you scale our towers, never carry off our women
 166  in your ships: before that, I will deal you your doom."[10]
 167  So he spoke; and Tydeus' son was torn two ways,
 168  whether to wheel the horses and fight him face to face.
 169  Three times he pondered it in mind and heart,
 170  and three times from the mountains of Ida Zeus the counselor thundered,
 171  giving his sign to the Trojans: victory that turns the battle's tide.[11]
 172  And Hector called to the Trojans with a far-carrying shout:
 173  "Trojans and Lycians and Dardanians who fight at close quarters —
 174  be men, my friends; remember your rushing courage.
 175  I know that the son of Cronos has bowed his head and promised me
 176  victory and great glory — and grief for the Danaans.
 177  The fools! — contriving those walls of theirs,
 178  feeble things, not worth a thought: they will not hold my fury,
 179  and our horses will lightly leap the ditch they dug.
 180  But when I have come among their hollow ships,
 181  then let there be some memory of burning fire,
 182  so I may fire the ships and kill the men themselves —
 183  the Argives by their ships, dazed in the smoke."
 184  So speaking he called to his horses, and named them:
 185  "Xanthus, and you, Podargus, and Aethon, and brilliant Lampus:[12]
 186  now pay me back your keep — all the care and plenty
 187  of Andromache, great-hearted Eëtion's daughter,
 188  who set the honey-hearted wheat before you first,
 189  and mixed wine for you to drink when your hearts commanded —
 190  before me, and I claim to be her strong young husband.[13]
 191  After them now, at full stretch, so we may take
 192  the shield of Nestor, whose fame now reaches heaven —
 193  solid gold, they say, the cross-rods and the shield itself —
 194  and off the shoulders of Diomedes, breaker of horses,
 195  the wrought corslet Hephaestus labored to make.[14]
 196  Could we take those two things, I should hope to put the Achaeans
 197  aboard their swift ships this very night."
 198  So he spoke in his vaunting; and the lady Hera was outraged:
 199  she shook on her throne and made high Olympus tremble,
 200  and spoke straight out to the great god Poseidon:
 201  "Shame, wide-ruling earth-shaker — not even you,
 202  in your own chest, grieve for the Danaans as they die.
 203  Yet to you, to Helice and Aegae, they bring gifts up,[15]
 204  many and pleasing; and you once wished them victory.
 205  If only we chose — all of us who stand by the Danaans —
 206  to thrust the Trojans back and hold off wide-seeing Zeus,
 207  he could sit there and sulk, alone on Ida."
 208  Deeply troubled, the lord earth-shaker answered her:
 209  "Hera, reckless of tongue — what a word you have spoken!
 210  I would not choose to have the rest of us fight
 211  Zeus, Cronos' son: for he is stronger by far."
 212  So they spoke such things to one another;
 213  and all the space the ditch walled off between wall and ships
 214  filled with a crush of horses and shield-bearing men
 215  penned in: and the penner was Hector, Priam's son,
 216  the equal of quick Ares, now that Zeus gave him glory.
 217  And he would have burned the balanced ships with blazing fire,
 218  had not the lady Hera put it in Agamemnon's mind

 221  holding a great purple cloak in his heavy hand,
 222  and stood on Odysseus's black ship, huge of hull,
 223  which lay midmost, so a shout could carry to both ends,
 224  both to the huts of Ajax, Telamon's son,
 225  and to Achilles' — for they had drawn their balanced ships up
 226  at the ends, trusting their courage and the strength of their hands.[16]

 228  "Shame, Argives! Vile disgraces — admired only for your looks!
 229  Where have the boasts gone, when we said we were the best —

 231  eating your fill of the flesh of straight-horned oxen,

 235  Hector, who soon will fire the ships with blazing flame.

 246  and nodded: the host should be saved, and not perish.

 265  after them Eurypylus, Euaemon's shining son;

 386  the many-colored robe she herself had made and worked with her hands,

 390  heavy, huge, compact — with which she breaks the ranks of men,

 393  and self-moved the gates of heaven groaned open, kept by the Seasons,

 399  "Up, go, swift Iris: turn them back; do not let them
 400  come against me. It will be no fair meeting if we join in war.
 401  For I say this outright, and it will be accomplished:
 402  I will lame their swift horses under the chariot,
 403  hurl the two of them from the car, and smash the chariot;
 404  and not in ten circling years will they be healed
 405  of the wounds the thunderbolt tears — so grey-eyes may learn
 406  what it is to fight against her own father.
 407  With Hera I am not so indignant, not so angry:
 408  it has always been her way to cross whatever I say."
 409  So he spoke; and storm-footed Iris rose to bear the message,
 410  and went from the mountains of Ida to high Olympus.
 411  At the very gates of many-folded Olympus
 412  she met them and held them, and told them the word of Zeus:
 413  "Where are you two rushing? What is this madness raging in your hearts?
 414  The son of Cronos forbids you to stand by the Argives.
 415  For thus the son of Cronos threatens — and he will do it:
 416  to lame your swift horses under the chariot,
 417  hurl the two of you from the car, and smash the chariot;
 418  and not in ten circling years will you be healed
 419  of the wounds the thunderbolt tears — so you may learn,
 420  grey-eyes, what it is to fight against your own father.
 421  With Hera he is not so indignant, not so angry:
 422  it has always been her way to cross whatever he says.
 423  But you — appalling; you shameless dog — if you truly
 424  dare to lift your gigantic spear against Zeus."[24]
 425  So speaking, swift-footed Iris went her way;
 426  and Hera spoke her word to Athena:
 427  "Shame — child of aegis-bearing Zeus: I say no longer
 428  can the two of us make war on Zeus for the sake of mortals.
 429  Of them, let one man perish, and another live,
 430  as it may fall; and let him, pondering his own purposes,
 431  judge between Trojans and Danaans, as is fitting."[25]
 432  So speaking she turned the single-hoofed horses back.
 433  The Seasons unyoked their lovely-maned horses for them,
 434  and tethered them at the ambrosial mangers,
 435  and leaned the chariot against the shining inner wall;
 436  and the goddesses sat down on golden chairs
 437  among the other gods, their dear hearts sorrowing.
 438  And father Zeus drove his strong-wheeled chariot and horses
 439  from Ida to Olympus, and came to the sessions of the gods.
 440  For him the famed earth-shaker unyoked the horses
 441  and set the car on its stand, and spread a cloth across it;
 442  and wide-seeing Zeus himself sat down on his golden throne,
 443  and great Olympus shook beneath his feet.
 444  Apart from Zeus, and alone, sat Athena and Hera,
 445  and did not speak to him or question him;
 446  but he understood in his heart, and spoke:
 447  "Why so stricken, Athena and Hera?
 448  Surely you are not worn out with battle where men win glory —
 449  with killing Trojans, against whom you have set your dread hatred!
 450  Come what may — such is my fury, so untouchable my hands —
 451  all the gods there are on Olympus could not turn me.
 452  As for you two: trembling took your shining limbs
 453  before you ever saw the war, and war's grim works.
 454  For I will say this outright — and it would have been accomplished:
 455  struck by my thunderbolt, you two would never have come back
 456  on your own chariot to Olympus, where the immortals have their seat."
 457  So he spoke, and they murmured at it, Athena and Hera,
 458  who sat side by side plotting evils for the Trojans.
 459  Athena, to be sure, stayed silent and said nothing,
 460  sulking at father Zeus, wild anger gripping her;
 461  but Hera's breast could not contain her anger, and she spoke:
 462  "Most dread son of Cronos, what a word you have spoken!
 463  Well do we know your strength is nothing feeble;
 464  but even so we grieve for the Danaan spearmen
 465  who will fill up an evil doom and perish.
 466  Yes: we will hold back from the war, if you command;
 467  but we will put counsel into the Argives — such as will help them —
 468  so they do not all perish under your anger."
 469  Answering her spoke Zeus the cloud-gatherer:
 470  "At dawn you will see him — if you have the heart for it,
 471  ox-eyed lady Hera — the overmastering son of Cronos
 472  wrecking still more of the great host of Argive spearmen.
 473  For massive Hector will not stop from the fighting
 474  until the swift-footed son of Peleus rises beside the ships,
 475  on that day when they fight at the ships' sterns,
 476  in the narrowest, most terrible press, over Patroclus fallen.[26]
 477  So it is decreed by heaven. And for your anger
 478  I care nothing — not if you go to the nethermost limits
 479  of earth and sea, where Iapetus and Cronos
 480  sit, and take no joy in the rays of Hyperion the Sun,
 481  nor in any wind, and deep Tartarus is all around them:
 482  not if you reach that place in your wandering do I care
 483  for your sulking — since there is nothing more dog-like than you."[27]
 484  So he spoke; and white-armed Hera answered him nothing.
 485  And the bright light of the sun fell into Ocean,
 486  drawing black night across the grain-giving earth.
 487  To the Trojans the light sank unwelcome; but to the Achaeans
 488  dark night came welcome — thrice prayed for — very welcome.

 489  Now glorious Hector made an assembly of the Trojans,
 490  leading them apart from the ships, by the eddying river,
 491  in a clear space, where ground showed empty of the dead.[28]
 492  Stepping from their chariots to the earth they listened to the word
 493  that Hector spoke, dear to Zeus. In his hand
 494  he held a spear eleven cubits long; and before him the bronze
 495  point of the shaft shone, and a ring of gold ran round it.
 496  Leaning on this, he spoke his words to the Trojans:
 497  "Hear from me, Trojans and Dardanians and allies.
 498  Just now I said that I should destroy the ships and all
 499  the Achaeans, and go back again to windy Ilion;
 500  but darkness came too soon. That, above all, has saved
 501  the Argives now, and the ships at the surf-line of the sea.
 502  So then: for now, let us yield to black night
 503  and make our supper ready. The lovely-maned horses —
 504  loose them from under the cars, and throw fodder beside them;
 505  and bring oxen from the city, and fat sheep,
 506  quickly; get honey-hearted wine, and bread
 507  from your halls; and gather plenty of wood besides,
 508  so that all night long, till early-born Dawn,
 509  we may burn fires in plenty, and the glare go up to heaven —
 510  lest even by night the long-haired Achaeans
 511  make a run for home over the broad back of the sea.
 512  Not at their ease, at least! — not unbloodied shall they board:
 513  let many a one of them have a shaft to digest at home,
 514  struck by an arrow or a sharp spear
 515  as he leaps for his ship — so that others too may shudder
 516  to bring tearful war on the horse-taming Trojans.
 517  And let heralds dear to Zeus announce through the city
 518  that boys in first youth and grey-templed old men
 519  are to post themselves around the town on the god-built towers;
 520  and the women, every one of them, each in her hall,
 521  to burn a great fire; and let the watch be kept unbroken,
 522  lest a raiding party enter the town while the army is away.
 523  So let it be, great-hearted Trojans, as I say it:
 524  let the word that serves this night stand as spoken,
 525  and the word for dawn I will speak among the horse-taming Trojans tomorrow.
 526  I hope — with prayer to Zeus and the other gods —
 527  to drive out of here those dogs whom the death-fates carry,[29]
 528  whom the fates carried to us on their black ships.
 529  So: through the night we will guard ourselves,
 530  and early, toward dawn, armed in our gear,
 531  we will waken sharp Ares beside the hollow ships.
 532  I will know whether Tydeus' son, strong Diomedes,
 533  will thrust me from the ships back on their wall — or whether I
 534  will break him with the bronze and carry off his bloody spoils.
 535  Tomorrow he will find out his own worth — whether he can stand
 536  my spear as it comes on. No: among the first, I think,
 537  he will lie speared, and many companions around him,
 538  as the sun climbs tomorrow. Would that I were as surely
 539  deathless and ageless for all my days,
 540  and honored as Athena and Apollo are honored,
 541  as surely as this day now brings evil to the Argives!"[30]
 542  So Hector spoke; and the Trojans roared applause.
 543  They loosed their sweating horses from under the yoke
 544  and tethered them with straps, each by his own chariot;
 545  and they brought oxen from the city, and fat sheep,
 546  quickly; got honey-hearted wine, and bread
 547  from their halls; and gathered plenty of wood besides;
 548  and they offered to the immortals perfect hecatombs.
 549  The winds carried the savor from the plain into the sky —
 550  sweet savor; but the blessed gods took no share of it;
 551  they would not: for holy Ilion was hated in their hearts,[31]
 552  and Priam, and the people of Priam of the good ash spear.
 553  But they, high in their hopes, on the causeways of battle
 554  sat all night long, and their fires burned in multitudes.
 555  As when in the sky the stars around the shining moon
 556  appear in all their glory, when the air falls windless,
 557  and all the lookout-peaks show clear, and the jutting headlands,
 558  and the glens; and from heaven the measureless air breaks open,
 559  and every star is seen; and the shepherd's heart is glad:[32]
 560  so many, between the ships and the streams of Xanthus,
 561  shone the fires the Trojans burned before Ilion.
 562  A thousand fires burned in the plain, and beside each one
 563  sat fifty men in the glare of the blazing fire.
 564  And the horses, champing white barley and spelt,
 565  stood beside the chariots, and waited for fair-throned Dawn.

Notes

[1] (8.1) "Dawn in her saffron robe" (κροκόπεπλος) — a new dawn-formula
    for the register, distinct from "early-born rosy-fingered Dawn"
    (1.477); the book will close with a third, "fair-throned Dawn" (565).

[2] (8.13-16) Tartarus mapped: iron gates, bronze threshold, "as far
    beneath Hades as heaven is above the earth" — the cosmos as three
    equal stories, with the threat calibrated to the architecture.

[3] (8.18-27) The golden cord: all gods and goddesses on one end, Zeus
    on the other — and he would reel in "the earth and the sea with
    you," and hang the whole from a horn of Olympus. Zenodotus athetized
    25-26 and Aristarchus condemned the sequel (apparatus, Lines 25-26,
    28-34). Later antiquity allegorized the cord endlessly (the sun,
    the chain of being); in context it is a weight-boast, and the whole
    book is its demonstration.

[4] (8.38-40) Zeus smiles: "I do not speak with a set purpose; toward
    you I mean to be gentle." Aristarchus athetized (= 22.183-184, where
    the lines recur). The wink complicates the ban — how seriously Zeus
    means his own decree is exactly what Books 8-15 keep testing.

[5] (8.47-48) Gargaron, Ida's peak, "where his precinct is, and his
    smoking altar" — Zeus takes his seat above the battle at one of his
    own cult-sites: spectator's box and shrine in one.

[6] (8.69-74) The golden scales: two "fates of death" weighed, and the
    Achaeans' day of doom sinks. Aristarchus athetized 73-74
    (apparatus). Zeus weighs but does not choose — the gesture that
    poses, without solving, the poem's deepest question about Zeus and
    fate; it returns, for two men instead of two armies, at 22.209-213.

[7] (8.90-98) Odysseus, called by name to stand for old Nestor, "did
    not hear" (οὐδʼ ἐσάκουσε) — or did not heed? The Greek permits
    both, and ancient readers already argued; the poem moves on
    without comment, and Diomedes goes back alone. First Iliad
    occurrence of the Odyssey's great epithet "much-enduring brilliant
    Odysseus" — in the poem's least flattering moment for him.

[8] (8.105-108) Diomedes' offer repeats his own words about the horses
    of Tros (5.221-223, verbatim in Greek and in this translation) —
    the captured team already a talking point. (Echo note.)

[9] (8.139-144) Nestor's counsel of retreat: "No man can fend off the
    purpose of Zeus." The old man articulates the book's rule — divine
    favor is temporal ("today... later") — and Diomedes' agonized
    compliance (146-150 reuse the formulas of 1.286 and 4.182) marks
    the limit of even the best mortal's aristeia.

[10] (8.161-166) Hector's taunt — "you were a woman after all. Away
     with you, painted doll!" (κακὴ γλήνη, a glass-eyed puppet or
     doll). Aristophanes and Aristarchus athetized 164-166, and
     Zenodotus's text read "I will deal you your death" (πότμον
     ἐφήσω) for "deal you your doom" (δαίμονα δώσω) at 166 —
     the entry for this book in disputed_lines.md (#4). Kept: the
     gendered insult answers 163's "you were a woman," and Diomedes'
     three-fold hesitation at 167-169 shows it landed.

[11] (8.169-171) "Three times he pondered... and three times Zeus
     thundered" — the three-then-fourth pattern (5.436-438) with the
     fourth beat removed: against Zeus there is no fourth try, only
     the sign obeyed.

[12] (8.185) Hector names four horses — Xanthus, Podargus, Aethon,
     Lampus — but a Homeric chariot is drawn by two, and Aristarchus
     athetized the line as un-Homeric (apparatus). This Xanthus is not
     Achilles' immortal horse (19.400) nor the river; the poem's names
     circulate.

[13] (8.186-190) Andromache fed the horses "before me" — wheat, and
     wine mixed in — the domestic tenderness of Book 6 recruited into
     Hector's war-speech (Aristarchus athetized 189). The detail is
     the marriage in one image; that it surfaces as an argument to
     horses is Hector all over.

[14] (8.191-195) The fabulous targets: Nestor's shield of solid gold
     "whose fame now reaches heaven," Diomedes' corslet forged by
     Hephaestus — neither is mentioned anywhere else in the poem.
     Rumor-armor, conjured by desire at the moment of imagined
     victory.

[15] (8.203) Helice and Aegae — Poseidon's great cult-seats on the
     Achaean gulf; Hera's argument is a priest's ledger: they pay you;
     act accordingly.

[16] (8.220-226) The camp's geography, stated plainly: Odysseus's ship
     at the midpoint, where a shout reaches both ends; Ajax and
     Achilles holding the exposed extremities, "trusting their courage
     and the strength of their hands." The arrangement (repeated at
     11.5-9) is the war's org-chart in ship-positions — and explains
     why Hector's assault will fall where it falls.

[17] (8.228-235) "the boasts you made on Lemnos" — the wine-stop of
     the outbound voyage (the same Lemnos whose wine-fleet supplied
     the camp at 7.467-475): each man good for a hundred Trojans, two
     hundred. Agamemnon's rebuke opens with the same "Shame, Argives!"
     line as Hera's at 5.787 — the army's leaders now sound like its
     goddess.

[18] (8.247-252) The eagle drops the fawn beside the altar of "Zeus of
     All Voices" (Πανομφαῖος — source of every omen): a sign delivered
     to the address of signs. Zeus grants the prayer's minimum — the
     host will not perish — while the day's verdict stands.

[19] (8.266-334) Teucer's aristeia: shooting from under Ajax's shield
     and slipping back "like a child to his mother" — the bastard
     brother (Agamemnon says the word, 284, in praise) whose skill is
     framed by dependency; his reward-list runs tripod, horses, "or a
     woman"; and his day ends with Hector's stone to the collarbone —
     "the deadliest spot" (326-327, the same anatomical formula as the
     horse's crown at 83-84).

[20] (8.302-308) Gorgythion: the son of a mother "beautiful as the
     goddesses," and the poppy heavy with seed and spring rain — the
     poem's most famous image of a death, a garden conscripted to
     carry a battlefield. Virgil took it for Euryalus (Aeneid
     9.435-437). The vignette-technique of 4.473-489 perfected in
     three lines.

[21] (8.348-349) Hector wheeling "with the eyes of the Gorgon, or of
     Ares bane of mortals" — the Gorgon-face from Athena's own aegis
     (5.741) lent to the poem's most human warrior at his most
     daemonic. The book's Hector is glory running ahead of its limit.

[22] (8.362-369) Athena's grievance: she was Zeus's instrument for
     saving Heracles through Eurystheus's labors — including the
     descent "to bring out of Erebus the hound of hateful Hades":
     Cerberus, present but unnamed, as at Odyssey 11.623. Her wish
     that he had drowned in Styx is savage even by Olympian standards
     (Zenodotus omitted, Aristarchus athetized 371-372, the
     Thetis-supplication reprise).

[23] (8.384-391) Athena's arming repeats 5.733-747 in abridged form —
     the robe she made herself falling on her father's floor — and
     Zenodotus/Aristophanes/Aristarchus objected here too (apparatus,
     Line 385-387). The English repeats the Book 5 rendering verbatim,
     per the repetition policy.

[24] (8.397-424) The Iris relay: Zeus's threat — lamed horses, smashed
     chariot, thunderbolt wounds that "not in ten circling years" will
     heal — delivered word for word, with the messenger's licensed
     shift of person... and then one line of Iris's own: "you
     shameless dog" (423, κύον ἀδεές). Aristarchus athetized 420-424;
     but the herald who adds venom the principal never spoke is a
     precise, humanized touch. Dogs are this book's insult-currency
     (299, 423, 483, 527).

[25] (8.427-431) Hera's retreat: "let one man perish, and another
     live, as it may fall." The coldest couplet in the poem's divine
     politics — the goddess of the Achaean cause, rebuffed once,
     reverting to actuarial indifference. It is the exact opposite of
     the poem's own attention, which never lets a death be "as it may
     fall."

[26] (8.470-477) The Patroclus prophecy: Hector will not stop "until
     the swift-footed son of Peleus rises beside the ships, on that
     day when they fight at the ships' sterns... over Patroclus
     fallen. So it is decreed" (θέσφατον). The poem's plot through
     Book 18, announced by Zeus as settled fact — the first time
     Patroclus's death is spoken. Suspense in Homer is never whether,
     only how and at what cost.

[27] (8.478-483) "where Iapetus and Cronos sit" — the imprisoned
     Titans, sunless and windless in deep Tartarus (cf. 5.898, Zeus's
     threat to Ares; and 14.279). Zeus's rhetoric to his wife reaches
     for the family's oldest violence — and ends on "nothing more
     dog-like than you."

[28] (8.489-491) The Trojan assembly "in a clear space, where ground
     showed empty of the dead" — the plain is now so strewn that open
     ground needs remarking. Hector's eleven-cubit spear repeats
     6.318-320 verbatim (English likewise): the domestic Hector of
     Book 6 and the field-marshal of Book 8 carry the same spear.

[29] (8.526-528) "those dogs whom the death-fates carry, whom the
     fates carried here on their black ships" — κῆρες as both escort
     and doom: the Achaeans came shipped by their own deaths. Hector's
     grimmest wordplay.

[30] (8.538-541) "Would that I were as surely deathless and ageless
     for all my days... as surely as this day brings evil to the
     Argives": the adynaton-wish at the peak of Hector's confidence.
     Zenodotus omitted 535-537 and Aristarchus flagged 538-541 with
     critical points (apparatus) — ancient discomfort at Hector
     wishing himself Athena's and Apollo's equal. Kept: the wish
     measures exactly how far Zeus's one day of favor has carried him;
     its cadence returns, desperate, at 22.304-305.

[31] (8.548-552) The Trojans sacrifice perfect hecatombs — "but the
     blessed gods took no share of it; they would not: for holy Ilion
     was hated in their hearts." Ritual perfectly performed and
     refused (cf. 6.311): between Athena's turned head and this
     rejected savor, Troy's fate is already liturgically sealed.

[32] (8.555-565) The watchfires: stars around the moon in windless
     air, every peak and headland clear, "and the shepherd's heart is
     glad" — the observer's joy that Zenodotus deleted (apparatus,
     Line 557-558) and that makes the simile: dread from the Achaean
     wall is wonder from the hills. A thousand fires, fifty men to
     each; and the last word of this most martial book belongs to
     horses champing barley, "waiting for fair-throned Dawn."
