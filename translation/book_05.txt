BOOK 5

The aristeia of Diomedes: gods bleed — Aphrodite, then Ares himself,
wounded by a mortal's spear.

TRANSLATOR'S COMMENTARY

Book 5 is the poem's first aristeia — the sustained blaze of a single
hero — and its most theologically audacious book: a mortal wounds two gods,
and the poem takes the wounds seriously enough to invent divine physiology
for them (ichor, "for they eat no bread," note [16]) and divine medicine
(Paiëon and the rennet simile, notes [18], [43]). The structure is a
standing order and its two-stage release: Athena's instruction at 127-132
— fight no god except Aphrodite — governs the first half; her revocation at
825-834 licenses the second. Diomedes is exactly as obedient as the order
requires, which is what separates his god-fighting from hybris: when Apollo
warns him off with the poem's theology compressed into one couplet ("never
of one kind / are the race of immortal gods and of men who walk the
earth," 441-442, note [21]), he yields at the fourth rush. That
three-times-then-the-fourth pattern is planted here for its terrible
recurrences later in the poem.

The book's gods are physical to the point of comedy, and the translation
does not tone it down: the chariot axle groans under Athena's weight
(athetized by Aristarchus, note [39]), Ares bellows like ten thousand men
and then whimpers to his father, and Zeus's rebuke of him (889-891) repeats
verbatim the words Agamemnon flung at Achilles in Book 1 — "most hateful to
me are you... strife is always dear to you, and wars, and battles" — the
poem's own judgment on the war-god delivered in a mortal quarrel's language
(note [42]). Dione's consolation of the wounded Aphrodite (382-404)
reaches back to older, stranger myth — Ares thirteen months in a bronze
jar, Hera and Hades shot by Heracles — and Dione herself, Aphrodite's
mother here, contradicts the Hesiodic foam-birth (note [17]).

Two continuity matters are recorded rather than smoothed: Pylaemenes,
killed at 576, will reappear alive in Book 13 — the most famous
authorial nod in the poem (note [26]) — and the Ortilochus/Orsilochus
spellings at 542-549 follow the printed text's own variation. New fixed
epithets are registered for Ares ("bane of mortals," "blood-stained,"
"rushing," "brazen") and for the book's one-off divine names (Cypris,
Enyo, Paiëon, the Seasons). The formula checker has been extended to
cover the Ares set.

   1  Then it was that Pallas Athena gave Tydeus' son[1]
   2  Diomedes fury and daring, so that he might stand out
   3  among all the Argives and win himself high fame.
   4  She kindled unwearying fire from his helmet and shield,
   5  like the star of late summer that beyond all others
   6  blazes bright when it rises bathed in Ocean:
   7  such was the fire she kindled from his head and shoulders,
   8  and she drove him into the middle, where the press was thickest.

   9  There was among the Trojans one Dares, wealthy, blameless,[2]
  10  a priest of Hephaestus; and he had two sons,
  11  Phegeus and Idaeus, skilled in all fighting.
  12  These two broke from the line and charged him head on,
  13  the two of them from their car, he on foot from the ground.
  14  And when they were close, advancing on one another,
  15  Phegeus first hurled his long-shadowed spear:
  16  over Tydeus' son's left shoulder the spear-point
  17  passed, and did not hit him. Second, Tydeus' son
  18  rose with his bronze; and not in vain did the shaft escape his hand:
  19  he struck his chest between the nipples, and thrust him from the car.
  20  Idaeus sprang away, leaving the beautiful chariot;
  21  he did not dare stand over his slain brother —
  22  nor would he himself have escaped black doom either,
  23  but Hephaestus snatched him out and saved him, wrapped in night,
  24  so that his old priest should not be wholly broken with grief.
  25  And the son of great-hearted Tydeus drove off the horses
  26  and gave them to his companions to lead down to the hollow ships.
  27  Now when the great-hearted Trojans saw the two sons of Dares,
  28  one fleeing, one killed beside his chariot,
  29  the hearts of all were shaken. And grey-eyed Athena
  30  took rushing Ares by the hand and spoke to him:
  31  "Ares, Ares, bane of mortals, blood-stained stormer of walls —[3]
  32  shall we not leave the Trojans and Achaeans
  33  to fight it out, and let father Zeus give glory where he will,
  34  while we two withdraw, and keep clear of the wrath of Zeus?"
  35  So speaking she led rushing Ares out of the battle,
  36  and sat him down on the high bank of Scamander.

  37  Then the Danaans bent the Trojans back; and each of the captains
  38  took his man. First the lord of men Agamemnon
  39  threw great Odius, captain of the Halizones, from his chariot:
  40  as he turned, first of all, the spear fixed in his back
  41  between the shoulders and drove through his chest;
  42  he fell with a thud, and his armor clattered upon him.
  43  And Idomeneus cut down Phaestus, son of Maeonian Borus,
  44  who had come from deep-soiled Tarne:
  45  him Idomeneus famed with the spear, just as he mounted
  46  behind his horses, stabbed with the long spear through the right shoulder;
  47  he crashed from the car, and hateful darkness seized him.
  48  And Idomeneus' aides stripped him of his armor.
  49  And Scamandrius, son of Strophius, the keen hunter,
  50  fell to Atreus' son Menelaus and his sharp spear —
  51  a fine huntsman: Artemis herself had taught him
  52  to bring down all the wild things the mountain forest breeds.
  53  But Artemis who showers arrows was no help to him then,[4]
  54  nor the long shots in which he once excelled;
  55  Atreus' son Menelaus, famed with the spear,
  56  stabbed him with the spear in the back as he fled before him,
  57  between the shoulders, and drove it through his chest;
  58  he crashed face-down, and his armor clattered upon him.
  59  And Meriones cut down Phereclus, son of Tecton,
  60  Harmon's son, whose hands knew how to fashion
  61  all cunning work, for Pallas Athena loved him beyond all men;
  62  he was the one who had built for Alexandros the balanced ships
  63  that began the evil — a curse to all the Trojans[5]
  64  and to himself, since he knew nothing of the gods' decrees.
  65  Him Meriones, when he caught him in the chase,
  66  struck in the right buttock, and the point passed under the bone
  67  straight through into the bladder;
  68  he dropped to his knees with a scream, and death enfolded him.
  69  And Meges killed Pedaeus, Antenor's son —
  70  bastard-born, but brilliant Theano had reared him[6]
  71  with care, like her own dear children, to please her husband.
  72  Phyleus' son, famed with the spear, came in close
  73  and struck him with the sharp spear in the tendon of the head;
  74  straight on through the teeth the bronze cut under his tongue,
  75  and he crashed in the dust, his teeth clenched on cold bronze.
  76  And Eurypylus, Euaemon's son, killed brilliant Hypsenor,
  77  son of high-hearted Dolopion, who was made
  78  Scamander's priest, and was honored by his people like a god:
  79  him Eurypylus, Euaemon's shining son,
  80  running him down as he fled before him, struck on the shoulder
  81  with a sweep of his sword, and sheared away his heavy arm;
  82  the bloody arm fell in the field; and over his eyes
  83  closed purple death, and fate the overpowering.[7]

  84  So they labored in the strong encounter.
  85  But Tydeus' son — you could not have told which side he fought on,
  86  whether he ran with the Trojans or the Achaeans:
  87  for he stormed across the plain like a river in flood,
  88  a winter torrent that scatters the dikes in its rushing;
  89  the close-built dikes cannot contain it,
  90  nor the walls of the fruitful orchards hold it,
  91  when it comes suddenly, swollen with the rain of Zeus,
  92  and everywhere the fine works of strong men crash before it:
  93  so before Tydeus' son the packed companies of the Trojans
  94  were driven, and for all their numbers they could not stand him.
  95  But when Lycaon's shining son caught sight of him
  96  storming across the plain, driving the companies before him,
  97  quickly he bent his curved bow at Tydeus' son
  98  and shot him as he charged, striking the right shoulder
  99  at the plate of the corslet; the bitter arrow flew through
 100  and held straight on, and the corslet was spattered with blood.
 101  Over him Lycaon's shining son shouted aloud:
 102  "Rouse yourselves, great-hearted Trojans, goaders of horses!
 103  The best of the Achaeans is hit; and I say he will not
 104  long stand up under the strong shaft — if truly it was
 105  the lord Apollo, Zeus's son, who sped me here from Lycia."
 106  So he spoke in his boasting. But the swift shaft did not break him:
 107  he drew back and stood before his horses and chariot
 108  and spoke to Sthenelus, Capaneus' son:
 109  "Up, good son of Capaneus — get down from the chariot,
 110  and pull this bitter arrow out of my shoulder."
 111  So he spoke, and Sthenelus leapt from the car to the ground
 112  and stood beside him and drew the swift shaft clean through the shoulder,
 113  and the blood came spurting up through the supple tunic.
 114  Then Diomedes, good at the war-cry, prayed aloud:
 115  "Hear me, child of aegis-bearing Zeus, Unwearied one!
 116  If ever with kind intent you stood by my father
 117  in the heat of war, now befriend me in turn, Athena:
 118  grant me to kill this man — let him come in range of my spear —
 119  who shot me before I saw him and now boasts of it, and says
 120  I shall not long look on the bright light of the sun."
 121  So he spoke in prayer, and Pallas Athena heard him.
 122  She made his limbs light, the feet and the hands above them,
 123  and standing close she spoke winged words:
 124  "Take heart now, Diomedes, and fight the Trojans:
 125  for into your chest I have put your father's fury,
 126  the fearless fury the shield-shaking horseman Tydeus had;
 127  and I have taken from your eyes the mist that was on them before,[8]
 128  so that you may know clearly god and man alike.
 129  Therefore, if some god comes here to test you,
 130  do not fight face to face with any immortal —
 131  any of the others; but if Aphrodite, Zeus's daughter,
 132  comes into the fighting, wound her with the sharp bronze."
 133  So speaking, grey-eyed Athena went her way,
 134  and Tydeus' son went back and mixed with the front-fighters;
 135  and eager as his heart had been before to fight the Trojans,
 136  now fury three times as great took hold of him — like a lion
 137  that a shepherd in the field, guarding his woolly sheep,
 138  has grazed as it leapt the fold-wall, and not brought down:
 139  he has only roused its strength; and after that, no help —
 140  he slips in among the folds, and the flock panics, abandoned;
 141  the sheep are heaped in piles on one another,
 142  while the lion in its fury leaps the deep fold's wall again:
 143  so in his fury strong Diomedes mixed with the Trojans.

 144  There he took Astynous, and Hypeiron, shepherd of the people:
 145  one he struck above the nipple with the bronze-pointed spear;
 146  the other he hit with the great sword on the collarbone
 147  by the shoulder, and sheared the shoulder from the neck and back.
 148  These he left, and went after Abas and Polyidus,
 149  sons of Eurydamas, the old reader of dreams;
 150  but the old man read no dreams for them at their going —[9]
 151  strong Diomedes killed them both.
 152  Then he went after Xanthus and Thoon, sons of Phaenops,
 153  both late-born and beloved; and their father was worn with hard age,
 154  and had fathered no other son to leave over his possessions.
 155  There Diomedes killed them, and took the dear life
 156  of both, and left the father lamentation and bitter cares:
 157  he never welcomed them home alive from the fighting,
 158  and kinsmen of the widowed house divided the estate.
 159  Then he took two sons of Priam, Dardanus' descendant,
 160  Echemmon and Chromius, both in one chariot.
 161  As a lion leaps in among cattle and snaps the neck
 162  of a heifer or a cow as they graze the woodland,
 163  so the son of Tydeus forced them both from their horses,
 164  hard against their will, and stripped their armor;
 165  and the horses he gave his companions to drive to the ships.

 166  Now Aeneas saw him tearing the ranks of men apart,
 167  and went off through the fighting and the clatter of spears
 168  searching for godlike Pandarus, if he might find him.
 169  He found Lycaon's son, blameless and strong,
 170  and stood before him and spoke a word to his face:
 171  "Pandarus, where are your bow, and your winged arrows,
 172  and your fame? No man here rivals you in it,
 173  and no man in Lycia claims to be better than you.
 174  Come — lift your hands to Zeus, and loose a shaft at this man,
 175  whoever he is, who is master here, who has done the Trojans
 176  so much evil already, and unstrung the knees of many good men —
 177  unless he is some god enraged at the Trojans,
 178  angry over sacrifices; and a god's wrath is a heavy thing."
 179  Then answered him Lycaon's shining son:
 180  "Aeneas, counselor of the bronze-shirted Trojans,
 181  in everything I liken him to the war-minded son of Tydeus:
 182  I know him by his shield and his crested helmet's eye-slits,
 183  and by the look of his horses. Yet I cannot say for sure he is not a god.
 184  But if he is the man I think, the war-minded son of Tydeus,
 185  then not without a god does he rage like this: one of the immortals
 186  stands close by him, shoulders wrapped in cloud,
 187  who turned aside my swift shaft even as it reached him.
 188  For I have already loosed a shaft at him, and I hit him
 189  in the right shoulder, straight through the plate of the corslet;
 190  I said to myself I had hurled him down to Aïdoneus —
 191  and yet I did not break him. Some god is angry.
 192  And horses I have none, no chariot I might mount —
 193  though somewhere in Lycaon's halls stand eleven chariots,[10]
 194  beautiful, new-built, fresh from the maker, with cloths
 195  spread over them; and beside each a yoked pair of horses
 196  stands, champing white barley and spelt.
 197  Endlessly, as I was leaving, the old spearman Lycaon
 198  charged me in our well-built house:
 199  he told me to mount horse and chariot
 200  and lead the Trojans in the strong encounters.
 201  But I would not listen — it would have been far better —
 202  sparing the horses, for fear that in the crush of men
 203  they would go short of fodder, they who were used to eating their fill.
 204  So I left them, and came on foot to Ilion,
 205  trusting my bow — and it was to do me no good.
 206  Already I have shot at two of their best,
 207  Tydeus' son and Atreus' son, and out of both
 208  I drew real blood with my hits — and only roused them the more.
 209  It was a bad fate that made me take my curved bow from its peg
 210  that day when I led my Trojans to lovely Ilion,
 211  doing a favor for brilliant Hector.
 212  If ever I come home, and set eyes on
 213  my fatherland and my wife and my great high-roofed house,
 214  let some stranger strike my head off then and there
 215  if I do not break this bow with my hands and throw it
 216  into the blazing fire. It follows me around like wind."
 217  Then Aeneas, captain of the Trojans, answered him:
 218  "No more of that kind of talk. Nothing will change
 219  until we two go against this man with horses
 220  and chariot and try him in his armor, face to face.
 221  Come, get up into my car, and see
 222  what the horses of Tros are like, how they know their plain —
 223  how to wheel fast this way and that, in pursuit or flight.
 224  They will carry the two of us safe to the city, if once again
 225  Zeus hands the glory to Diomedes, Tydeus' son.
 226  Come then, take the whip and the glossy reins,
 227  and I will dismount to do the fighting; or you
 228  face the man yourself, and I will look after the horses."
 229  Then answered him Lycaon's shining son:
 230  "Aeneas, keep the reins yourself, and your own two horses.
 231  Better they pull the curved car under the driver they know,
 232  if we should have to run from the son of Tydeus.
 233  I would not have them take fright and stand useless, balking,
 234  unwilling to carry us out of the fighting, missing your voice,
 235  while the son of great-hearted Tydeus springs on us
 236  and kills the two of us and drives off the single-hoofed horses.
 237  You drive your own chariot and your own horses,
 238  and I will take him, when he comes on, with the sharp spear."
 239  So the two spoke, and mounting the patterned chariot
 240  they drove the swift horses hard at Tydeus' son.
 241  And Sthenelus saw them, Capaneus' shining son,
 242  and quickly he spoke winged words to Tydeus' son:
 243  "Diomedes, Tydeus' son, delight of my heart,
 244  I see two strong men bent on fighting you,
 245  men of measureless power: one the master bowman
 246  Pandarus — and he claims to be Lycaon's son;
 247  the other Aeneas, who claims blameless Anchises
 248  for father — and his mother is Aphrodite.
 249  Come, let us give ground on the chariot. Stop raging[11]
 250  through the front-fighters like this, or you may lose your dear life."
 251  Glaring at him darkly, strong Diomedes answered:
 252  "No talk of flight: I do not think you will persuade me.
 253  It is not in my blood to dodge a fight
 254  or cower down. My fury is still steady in me.
 255  I balk at mounting the chariot: no — just as I am
 256  I will go to meet them. Pallas Athena forbids me to tremble.
 257  As for those two, their swift horses will not carry both
 258  back out of range of us — even if one gets away.
 259  And I will tell you another thing — put it away in your heart:
 260  if Athena of the many counsels grants me the glory
 261  to kill them both, then you check our own swift horses
 262  here, and stretch the reins tight to the chariot rail,
 263  and remember to make a dash for Aeneas' horses,
 264  and drive them out of the Trojan lines to the strong-greaved Achaeans.
 265  For they are of that stock which wide-seeing Zeus gave Tros[12]
 266  in payment for his son Ganymede — the best
 267  of all horses under the dawn and the sun;
 268  and from that stock the lord of men Anchises stole a breed,
 269  putting his mares to them without Laomedon's knowledge.
 270  From these a line of six was born him in his halls:
 271  four he kept himself and reared at the manger,
 272  and these two he gave to Aeneas, machines of rout.
 273  If we could take those two, we would win high fame."
 274  So the two of them spoke such things to one another,
 275  and the other two came quickly on, driving the swift horses.
 276  First Lycaon's shining son called out to him:
 277  "Hard-hearted, war-minded son of proud Tydeus —
 278  so my swift shaft did not break you, my bitter arrow.
 279  Now I will try with the spear, and see if I hit."
 280  He spoke, and balancing his long-shadowed spear he hurled it
 281  and struck the shield of Tydeus' son: straight through it
 282  the bronze point flew and pressed on to the corslet.
 283  Over him Lycaon's shining son shouted aloud:
 284  "You are hit clean through the flank! I do not think
 285  you will hold up long now. You have given me great glory!"
 286  Unshaken, strong Diomedes answered him:
 287  "A miss, not a hit! But you two, I think, will not
 288  be done before one or the other of you falls
 289  and gluts with his blood Ares, the fighter under the ox-hide shield."
 290  So speaking he hurled; and Athena steered the shaft
 291  to the nose beside the eye, and it passed the white teeth.
 292  The unwearying bronze cut off his tongue at the root[13]
 293  and the point came out under the base of the chin;
 294  he crashed from the car, and his armor clattered upon him,
 295  glancing-bright, shimmering; and the swift-footed horses
 296  shied aside; and there his life and strength were unstrung.
 297  Then Aeneas sprang down with shield and long spear,
 298  fearing the Achaeans might somehow drag the corpse away.
 299  He strode around it like a lion sure of its strength,
 300  holding before him his spear and the shield balanced every way,
 301  set on killing whoever came against him,
 302  crying terribly. But Tydeus' son caught up a boulder
 303  in his hand — a great feat: two men could not carry it,[14]
 304  such as mortals are now — yet he heaved it easily, alone,
 305  and struck Aeneas with it on the hip, where the thigh
 306  turns in the hip-socket — the cup, as men call it:
 307  he crushed the cup, and tore both tendons through,
 308  and the jagged stone stripped the skin away. The hero
 309  dropped to his knees and stayed there, propping himself
 310  with one thick hand on the earth; and black night covered his eyes.
 311  And now the lord of men Aeneas would have perished,
 312  if Zeus's daughter Aphrodite had not been quick to see —
 313  his mother, who conceived him to Anchises herding cattle.
 314  She poured her white arms around her dear son,
 315  and spread before him a fold of her shining robe
 316  to be a screen against missiles, for fear some Danaan of the swift colts
 317  might drive bronze into his chest and take his life.
 318  So she began to carry her dear son out of the fighting.

 319  But the son of Capaneus did not forget the orders
 320  that Diomedes, good at the war-cry, had laid on him:
 321  he checked their own single-hoofed horses
 322  clear of the tumult, stretching the reins tight to the rail,
 323  made his dash for the lovely-maned horses of Aeneas,
 324  and drove them out of the Trojan lines to the strong-greaved Achaeans,
 325  and gave them to Deipylus, his dear companion, whom he honored
 326  beyond all his agemates because their minds ran together,
 327  to drive to the hollow ships. Then the hero
 328  mounted his own car and took the glossy reins
 329  and drove his strong-hoofed horses hard after Tydeus' son,
 330  eagerly. But Diomedes had gone after Cypris with the pitiless bronze,[15]
 331  knowing her for a god without war-strength — none
 332  of those goddesses who lord it in the wars of men,
 333  no Athena, no Enyo, sacker of cities.
 334  And when he caught her, chasing through the thick of the crowd,
 335  the son of great-hearted Tydeus lunged
 336  and leaping in stabbed with the sharp spear the tip
 337  of her soft hand; and the spear pierced the skin straight through
 338  the ambrosial robe the Graces themselves had made her,
 339  above the base of the palm. And the immortal blood of the goddess ran —
 340  ichor, such as runs in the veins of the blessed gods:[16]
 341  for they eat no bread, they drink no gleaming wine,
 342  and so they are bloodless, and are called deathless ones.
 343  She gave a great shriek, and flung her son from her —
 344  and Phoebus Apollo caught him up in his arms
 345  and hid him in a blue-dark cloud, for fear some Danaan of the swift colts
 346  might drive bronze into his chest and take his life.
 347  And over her Diomedes, good at the war-cry, shouted aloud:
 348  "Give way, daughter of Zeus, from war and fighting!
 349  Is it not enough that you cozen strengthless women?
 350  If you must haunt the war, then I think
 351  war will make you shudder after this, even at the rumor of it."
 352  So he spoke; and she went off dazed, in terrible pain;
 353  and wind-footed Iris took her and led her out of the crowd
 354  crushed with agony, her lovely skin darkening.
 355  She found rushing Ares seated on the battle's left,
 356  his spear leaning on a cloud-bank, and his swift horses.
 357  Dropping to her knees before her dear brother
 358  she begged and begged him for the golden-fronted horses:
 359  "Dear brother, take me in — give me your horses,
 360  so I may reach Olympus, where the immortals have their seat.
 361  The wound is too much for me — a mortal man struck me:
 362  Tydeus' son, who would fight father Zeus himself now."
 363  So she spoke, and Ares gave her the golden-fronted horses,
 364  and she climbed into the chariot, her dear heart aching,
 365  and Iris climbed in beside her and took the reins in her hands
 366  and whipped the horses on; and the two flew, nothing loath.
 367  Quickly they came to the gods' seat, steep Olympus,
 368  and there swift wind-footed Iris halted the horses,
 369  loosed them from the car, and threw them ambrosial fodder;
 370  and brilliant Aphrodite fell on the knees of Dione,[17]
 371  her mother; and Dione gathered her daughter in her arms
 372  and stroked her with her hand, and spoke a word, and named her:
 373  "Who has done this to you, dear child — which of the sons of Heaven —
 374  so wantonly, as though you had been caught doing wrong in the open?"
 375  Then answered her laughter-loving Aphrodite:
 376  "Tydeus' son wounded me, high-hearted Diomedes,
 377  because I was carrying my dear son out of the fighting —
 378  Aeneas, who is far the dearest of all things to me.
 379  This is no longer the dread din of Trojans and Achaeans:
 380  now the Danaans are fighting even with the immortals."
 381  Then answered her Dione, brilliant among goddesses:
 382  "Bear up, my child, and endure it, grieved though you are.[18]
 383  Many of us who keep our homes on Olympus have endured
 384  things from men, in the hard griefs we lay on one another.
 385  Ares endured it, when Otus and strong Ephialtes,
 386  the sons of Aloeus, bound him in strong bonds:
 387  thirteen months he lay bound in a bronze jar,
 388  and Ares the insatiate of war might have perished there,
 389  if their stepmother, beautiful Eëriboea,
 390  had not brought word to Hermes; and Hermes stole Ares out,
 391  worn down already, for the hard bonds were breaking him.
 392  Hera endured it, when the strong son of Amphitryon
 393  struck her in the right breast with a three-barbed arrow;
 394  then pain took hold of her too, past all comfort.
 395  Hades the gigantic endured a swift arrow with the rest,
 396  when the same man, the son of aegis-bearing Zeus,
 397  shot him at Pylos among the dead, and gave him over to pain:
 398  he went to the house of Zeus and high Olympus
 399  aching at heart, skewered with agony; the arrow
 400  was driven deep in his massive shoulder, and it wore his spirit.
 401  Paiëon sprinkled pain-killing drugs upon the wound
 402  and healed him: for he was not of mortal make.
 403  The hard man, the violent-worker! — he cared nothing what outrage he did,
 404  who vexed with his arrows the gods who hold Olympus.
 405  And this one — grey-eyed Athena set him on you.
 406  The fool: the son of Tydeus does not know in his heart
 407  that the man who fights the immortals does not live long;
 408  his children do not climb to his knees and call him papa[19]
 409  when he comes home from war and the dread fighting.
 410  So now let Tydeus' son, for all his strength,
 411  look to it, or a better fighter than you may face him,
 412  and Aegialeia, Adrastus' watchful-minded daughter,
 413  wake her whole house from sleep with her wailing,
 414  mourning the wedded husband of her youth, the best of the Achaeans —
 415  the strong wife of Diomedes, breaker of horses."
 416  She spoke, and with both hands wiped the ichor from the arm;
 417  the arm was made whole, and the heavy pains were eased.
 418  But Athena and Hera, looking on,
 419  began to provoke Zeus, son of Cronos, with needling words;
 420  and the goddess grey-eyed Athena led the talk:
 421  "Father Zeus, will you be angry at all with me for what I say?
 422  It must be that Cypris was coaxing some Achaean woman
 423  to run off with the Trojans she now so wonderfully loves,
 424  and stroking that soft-robed Achaean woman's gown,
 425  she scratched her slender hand on a golden brooch-pin."
 426  So she spoke, and the father of men and gods smiled,
 427  and he called golden Aphrodite to him and said:
 428  "Not to you, my child, are given the works of war.[20]
 429  No — you attend to the lovely works of marriage;
 430  all this shall be the business of quick Ares and Athena."
 431  So they spoke such things to one another.

 432  But Diomedes, good at the war-cry, sprang at Aeneas,
 433  though he well knew Apollo himself held his arms above him;
 434  he did not stand in awe even of the great god, but pressed on,
 435  always straining to kill Aeneas and strip his famed armor.
 436  Three times he sprang at him, raging to kill,
 437  and three times Apollo beat back his shining shield;
 438  but when the fourth time he came on like more than man,[21]
 439  Apollo the worker-from-afar called out with a terrible cry:
 440  "Think, son of Tydeus — and fall back. Do not try to think
 441  thoughts equal to the gods: never of one kind
 442  are the race of immortal gods and of men who walk the earth."
 443  So he spoke, and Tydeus' son gave back a little way,
 444  shunning the wrath of Apollo who strikes from afar.
 445  And Apollo set Aeneas down apart from the crowd
 446  in holy Pergamos, where his own temple stood;
 447  and Leto and Artemis who showers arrows
 448  healed him in the great inner shrine, and made him glorious.
 449  But Apollo of the silver bow made a phantom,[22]
 450  the very likeness of Aeneas, armor and all,
 451  and around the phantom the Trojans and brilliant Achaeans
 452  hacked at one another's chests, at the round
 453  ox-hide shields and the light fringed bucklers.
 454  Then Phoebus Apollo spoke to rushing Ares:
 455  "Ares, Ares, bane of mortals, blood-stained stormer of walls —
 456  will you not go in and drag this man out of the fighting,
 457  this son of Tydeus, who would fight father Zeus himself now?
 458  First he wounded Cypris, close in, on the hand at the wrist;
 459  then he charged at me myself, like more than man."
 460  So speaking, he sat down on the height of Pergamos,
 461  and baneful Ares went in among the Trojan ranks and roused them,
 462  in the likeness of Acamas, swift captain of the Thracians,
 463  and called to the Zeus-nurtured sons of Priam:
 464  "You sons of Priam, the king whom Zeus sustains,
 465  how long will you let your people be killed by the Achaeans?
 466  Until they are fighting around the well-built gates?
 467  A man is down whom we honored like brilliant Hector —
 468  Aeneas, son of great-hearted Anchises.
 469  Come, let us save our brave companion from the tumult!"
 470  So speaking he stirred the fury and heart of every man.
 471  And then Sarpedon raked brilliant Hector hard:[23]
 472  "Hector, where has that fury gone that you had before?
 473  You used to say, I think, that you would hold the city without armies,
 474  without allies — alone, with your sisters' husbands and your brothers.
 475  I cannot see one of them now, cannot pick one out:
 476  they cower like dogs around a lion.
 477  It is we who do the fighting — we, your mere allies.
 478  I am an ally myself, and I came from very far:
 479  far away is Lycia, on the eddying Xanthus,
 480  where I left my dear wife and my little son,
 481  and great wealth besides, which every have-not covets.
 482  Even so I drive my Lycians on, and I am eager myself
 483  to fight my man — though there is nothing of mine here
 484  for the Achaeans to carry off or drive away.
 485  And you just stand there — you do not even order
 486  the rest of your people to hold and defend their wives.
 487  Take care, or like men caught in the meshes of an all-catching net
 488  you become the prey and spoil of your enemies —
 489  and they will sack your well-peopled city soon.
 490  All this should be your care, night and day:
 491  to beg the captains of your far-famed allies
 492  to hold on without flinching — and to put away hard reproach."
 493  So spoke Sarpedon, and the word bit Hector to the heart.
 494  At once he leapt in his armor from the chariot to the ground,
 495  and shaking his two sharp spears went everywhere through the army,
 496  driving men to fight, and woke the dread din of battle.
 497  And the Trojans wheeled about and stood to face the Achaeans;
 498  and the Argives, massed close, awaited them and did not run.
 499  As the wind carries chaff across the sacred threshing-floors
 500  when men are winnowing, and tawny-haired Demeter
 501  in the driving wind divides the grain from the husks,
 502  and the chaff-heaps whiten: so the Achaeans then
 503  turned white from above with dust, which through their ranks
 504  the horses' hooves beat up to the all-bronze sky
 505  as the fighting rejoined; and the drivers wheeled them round.
 506  Straight on they carried the fury of their hands; and rushing Ares
 507  drew night over the battle to help the Trojans,
 508  ranging everywhere — carrying out the charge
 509  of Phoebus Apollo of the golden sword, who had bidden him
 510  wake the Trojans' spirit when he saw Pallas Athena
 511  leaving the field: for she was the Danaans' helper.
 512  And Apollo himself sent Aeneas out from the rich inner shrine
 513  and put fury into the chest of the shepherd of the people.
 514  Aeneas took his place among his companions; and they rejoiced
 515  to see him come to them alive and unhurt
 516  and full of brave fury. But they asked him nothing:
 517  the other toil would not let them — the toil the god of the silver bow
 518  was waking, and Ares bane of mortals, and Strife insatiably raging.

 519  And on the other side the two Ajaxes and Odysseus and Diomedes
 520  drove the Danaans on to fight; and they of themselves
 521  feared neither the Trojans' force nor their charges,
 522  but stood fast, like the clouds the son of Cronos
 523  halts in windless weather on the mountain-tops,
 524  motionless, while the fury of the North Wind sleeps, and the other
 525  hard-blowing winds, whose shrill blasts
 526  scatter the shadowing clouds whenever they rise:
 527  so the Danaans stood the Trojans, steady, unfleeing.
 528  And Atreus' son ranged through the crowd, commanding everywhere:
 529  "Friends — be men! Take hold of a fighting heart;
 530  feel shame before each other in the strong encounters.
 531  Where men feel shame, more are saved than slain;[24]
 532  where they run, no fame rises, and no strength."
 533  He spoke, and hurled his spear quick, and struck a front-rank man,
 534  Deicoon, Pergasus' son, companion of great-hearted
 535  Aeneas — a man the Trojans honored like Priam's own children,
 536  for he was quick to fight among the foremost.
 537  The lord Agamemnon struck his shield with the spear,
 538  and the shield did not stop it: the bronze passed clean through
 539  and drove through the war-belt into the lower belly;
 540  he fell with a thud, and his armor clattered upon him.
 541  Then Aeneas took two of the Danaans' best men,
 542  the sons of Diocles, Crethon and Orsilochus,
 543  whose father lived in well-built Pherae,
 544  rich in substance; his line was from the river
 545  Alpheius, which flows wide through the Pylians' land,
 546  and the river begot Ortilochus, lord over many men,[25]
 547  and Ortilochus fathered great-hearted Diocles,
 548  and of Diocles were born twin sons,
 549  Crethon and Orsilochus, skilled in all fighting.
 550  These two in their first manhood, on the black ships,
 551  followed with the Argives to Ilion of the fine colts,
 552  winning recompense for the sons of Atreus, Agamemnon
 553  and Menelaus; and there the end of death enfolded them both.
 554  Like two lions reared by their mother
 555  on the mountain peaks, in the thickets of deep forest,
 556  that harry the cattle and the fat sheep
 557  and lay waste the steadings of men, until they too
 558  are killed at men's hands by the sharp bronze:
 559  such were these two, broken under the hands of Aeneas,
 560  and they fell like tall fir trees.
 561  And as they fell, Menelaus dear to Ares pitied them,
 562  and strode through the front-fighters helmed in flashing bronze,
 563  shaking his spear; and Ares whetted his fury,
 564  with this thought: that he should go down under Aeneas' hands.
 565  But Antilochus saw him, great-hearted Nestor's son,
 566  and strode through the front-fighters; for he feared for the shepherd of the people,
 567  lest he suffer harm and cheat them of all their labor.
 568  The two were holding out their hands and sharp spears
 569  face to face, raging to fight,
 570  when Antilochus came and stood close by the shepherd of the people;
 571  and Aeneas did not stand his ground, quick fighter though he was,
 572  when he saw two men holding the line side by side.
 573  And when they had dragged the corpses back to the Achaean host,
 574  they laid the poor pair in their companions' arms,
 575  and turned themselves, and fought on among the foremost.

 576  There the two of them took Pylaemenes, the equal of Ares,[26]
 577  captain of the great-hearted Paphlagonian shieldmen:
 578  him Atreus' son Menelaus, famed with the spear,
 579  stabbed with the spear as he stood, striking through the collarbone;
 580  and Antilochus struck Mydon, his driver and aide,
 581  Atymnius' brave son, just as he wheeled the single-hoofed horses —
 582  caught his elbow square with a boulder; and out of his hands
 583  the reins, white with ivory, dropped in the dust.
 584  Antilochus sprang in and drove his sword through his temple,
 585  and gasping he pitched from the well-made chariot
 586  headfirst in the dust, on his crown and shoulders.
 587  A long moment he stood there planted — he had struck deep sand —
 588  till his own horses knocked him flat in the dust
 589  as Antilochus whipped them, and drove them to the Achaean camp.
 590  But Hector marked them across the ranks, and charged them
 591  shouting; and with him followed the strong companies
 592  of the Trojans; and Ares led them, and the lady Enyo —[27]
 593  she bringing Tumult with her, the shameless spirit of carnage,
 594  while Ares handled a gigantic spear in his fists
 595  and ranged now in front of Hector, now behind him.
 596  And seeing him Diomedes, good at the war-cry, shuddered.
 597  As a man crossing a great plain stops helpless
 598  at a quick-running river flowing down to the sea,
 599  sees it seething with foam, and starts back and away:
 600  so Tydeus' son now gave ground, and said to his people:
 601  "Friends — no wonder we marvel at brilliant Hector
 602  as spearman and daring fighter:
 603  beside him always stands some god who beats ruin away.
 604  There — Ares stands by him now, in the likeness of a mortal man.
 605  Keep your faces to the Trojans, and give ground
 606  backward; do not rage to fight force against force with gods."
 607  So he spoke; and the Trojans came on very close.
 608  There Hector killed two men skilled in fighting,
 609  Menesthes and Anchialus, both in one chariot.
 610  And as they fell, great Telamonian Ajax pitied them,
 611  and came up close and made his stand, and cast his shining spear
 612  and struck Amphius, Selagus' son, who in Paesus
 613  lived rich in substance, rich in grainland; but fate
 614  led him to fight as ally for Priam and his sons.
 615  Telamonian Ajax struck him in the war-belt:
 616  the long-shadowed spear fixed in the lower belly,
 617  and he fell with a thud. And glorious Ajax ran in
 618  to strip his armor; but the Trojans rained down spears on him,
 619  sharp, glittering; and his shield took many.
 620  He set his heel on the corpse and pulled out
 621  his bronze spear; but the rest of the fine armor he could not
 622  lift from the shoulders: the missiles pressed him too hard.
 623  And he feared the strong ring of the proud Trojans,
 624  who stood over him many and brave with spears set,
 625  who, big and strong and proud as he was,
 626  thrust him back from them; and he gave ground, staggering.

 627  So they labored in the strong encounter.
 628  And Tlepolemus, Heracles' son, tall and brave,
 629  was driven against godlike Sarpedon by fate the overpowering.
 630  And when they were close, advancing on one another —
 631  the son and the grandson of Zeus the cloud-gatherer —
 632  Tlepolemus spoke to him first:
 633  "Sarpedon, counselor of the Lycians — what forces you
 634  to cower here, a man who knows nothing of battle?
 635  They lie when they call you the son of aegis-bearing
 636  Zeus: you fall far short of those men
 637  who were born of Zeus in the generations before us —
 638  what they say the might of Heracles was,
 639  my own father, bold-hearted, lion-spirited!
 640  He came here once for the horses of Laomedon,[28]
 641  with only six ships and fewer men,
 642  and sacked the city of Ilion, and widowed her streets.
 643  But your heart is a coward's, and your people are dying away.
 644  I do not think you will be any bulwark to the Trojans
 645  for having come from Lycia — no, not if you are very strong —
 646  but broken by me you will pass the gates of Hades."
 647  Then Sarpedon, captain of the Lycians, answered him:
 648  "Tlepolemus — yes, that man destroyed holy Ilion
 649  through the folly of one proud man, Laomedon,
 650  who paid his good service with a hard word
 651  and refused the horses he had come so far for.
 652  But for you, I say your portion here from me is slaughter
 653  and black doom: broken under my spear you will give
 654  the glory to me, and your life to Hades of the famed colts."
 655  So spoke Sarpedon; and Tlepolemus lifted his ash spear,
 656  and the two long spears sprang from their hands
 657  in the same instant. Sarpedon struck him full in the neck,
 658  and the point passed through, bringing anguish;
 659  and murky night covered his eyes.
 660  And Tlepolemus's long spear had struck the left thigh:
 661  the point tore through, raging,
 662  and grazed the bone — but his father still warded off ruin.
 663  Then his brilliant companions bore godlike Sarpedon
 664  out of the fighting; and the long spear dragging from his thigh
 665  weighed on him; no one noticed it, no one thought
 666  in their haste to draw the ash spear from his thigh
 667  so he might stand: such toil they had in tending him.
 668  And on the other side the strong-greaved Achaeans bore
 669  Tlepolemus out of the fighting. Brilliant Odysseus marked it,
 670  his enduring heart holding, and his spirit blazed up;
 671  and he weighed it then in his mind and heart,
 672  whether to press on after the son of loud-thundering Zeus
 673  or take the lives of more of the Lycian rank and file.
 674  But it was not fated for great-hearted Odysseus
 675  to kill the strong son of Zeus with the sharp bronze;
 676  so Athena turned his fury against the mass of the Lycians.
 677  There he took Coeranus, and Alastor, and Chromius,
 678  Alcandrus and Halius and Noëmon and Prytanis;
 679  and brilliant Odysseus would have killed still more Lycians,
 680  if great Hector of the flashing helmet had not been quick to see.
 681  He strode through the front-fighters helmed in flashing bronze,
 682  bringing terror to the Danaans; and Sarpedon, Zeus's son,
 683  rejoiced at his coming, and spoke a piteous word:
 684  "Son of Priam — do not leave me lying here
 685  a prize for the Danaans. Stand over me! Afterward, if it must be,
 686  let life leave me in your city — since it was not to be
 687  that I should come home to my own dear fatherland
 688  and gladden my dear wife and my little son."
 689  So he spoke; and Hector of the flashing helmet answered him nothing,
 690  but swept past, straining to thrust the Argives back
 691  with all speed, and take the lives of many.
 692  Then his brilliant companions laid godlike Sarpedon
 693  under a beautiful oak of aegis-bearing Zeus,
 694  and strong Pelagon, who was his dear companion,
 695  pushed the ash spear out through his thigh;
 696  and his spirit left him, and a mist poured down over his eyes.
 697  Then he breathed again: the breath of the North Wind[29]
 698  blowing round him brought back to life the spirit he had gasped away.
 699  Meanwhile the Argives, under Ares and bronze-helmed Hector,
 700  neither turned and ran for the black ships
 701  nor stood up to them in fight, but always backward
 702  they gave ground, once they learned Ares was with the Trojans.
 703  Whom first, whom last did they strip of his arms,[30]
 704  Hector, Priam's son, and brazen Ares?
 705  Godlike Teuthras first, and Orestes, driver of horses,
 706  and Trechus the Aetolian spearman, and Oenomaus,
 707  and Helenus, Oenops' son, and Oresbius of the glancing waist-guard,
 708  who lived in Hyle, hoarding his wealth with care,
 709  on the shore of the Cephisian lake; and near him other
 710  Boeotians lived, and held a rich district of land.

 711  Now when the goddess white-armed Hera marked the two of them
 712  killing Argives in the strong encounter,
 713  at once she spoke winged words to Athena:
 714  "Shame! Unwearied one, child of aegis-bearing Zeus —
 715  empty, then, was the word we pledged to Menelaus,
 716  that he should sack well-walled Ilion before returning,
 717  if we let baneful Ares rage on like this.
 718  Come — let the two of us also remember our rushing courage."
 719  So she spoke, and the goddess grey-eyed Athena did not disobey.
 720  Hera went to and fro harnessing the golden-fronted horses —
 721  Hera, eldest goddess, daughter of great Cronos;
 722  and Hebe quickly set on the chariot the curved wheels,[31]
 723  bronze, eight-spoked, at the two ends of the iron axle.
 724  Their felloe is imperishable gold, and over it
 725  bronze tires are fitted, a wonder to see;
 726  the naves that circle at either side are silver,
 727  and the car is slung on plaited straps of gold
 728  and silver, with a double rail running round it.
 729  From it the pole ran out, of silver; and on its end
 730  she bound the beautiful golden yoke, and cast on it
 731  the beautiful golden breast-straps; and under the yoke Hera
 732  led the swift-footed horses, hungry for strife and the war-cry.
 733  And Athena, daughter of aegis-bearing Zeus,
 734  let fall her soft robe on her father's floor,[32]
 735  the many-colored robe she herself had made and worked with her hands,
 736  and put on the tunic of Zeus the cloud-gatherer,
 737  and armed herself in his gear for tearful war.
 738  Around her shoulders she cast the tasseled aegis,[33]
 739  terrible, crowned on every side with Rout;
 740  on it is Strife, on it Valor, on it blood-chilling Pursuit,
 741  and on it the Gorgon's head, the dread monster's,
 742  dreadful and grim, the portent of aegis-bearing Zeus.
 743  On her head she set the golden helmet, double-ridged,
 744  four-bossed, fitted with the fighting men of a hundred cities.
 745  Into the flaming chariot she stepped, and gripped the spear —
 746  heavy, huge, compact — with which she breaks the ranks of men,
 747  of heroes, when she of the mighty father is angered with them.
 748  And Hera swiftly touched the horses with the whip;
 749  and self-moved the gates of heaven groaned open, kept by the Seasons,[34]
 750  to whom great heaven and Olympus are entrusted,
 751  to roll the thick cloud aside, or set it back.
 752  There, straight through, they held the goaded horses;
 753  and they found the son of Cronos sitting apart from the other gods
 754  on the topmost peak of many-ridged Olympus.
 755  There the goddess white-armed Hera halted the horses
 756  and questioned Zeus most high, the son of Cronos:
 757  "Father Zeus, have you no anger at Ares for these brute works —
 758  how great a host of the Achaeans he has destroyed, and what men,
 759  recklessly, in no kind of order? It is grief to me; and meanwhile
 760  at their ease Cypris and Apollo of the silver bow
 761  enjoy it, having let loose this madman who knows no law.
 762  Father Zeus — will you be angry at all with me if I
 763  strike Ares a bitter blow and chase him out of the fighting?"
 764  Answering her spoke Zeus the cloud-gatherer:
 765  "Go to it: set the driver of the spoil on him — Athena,
 766  who beyond all others is used to bringing him hard pain."
 767  So he spoke, and the goddess white-armed Hera did not disobey.
 768  She whipped the horses on; and the two flew, nothing loath,
 769  between the earth and the starry sky.
 770  As far as a man can see into the haze with his eyes,[35]
 771  sitting on a lookout, gazing over the wine-dark sea,
 772  so far at a single bound spring the gods' high-neighing horses.
 773  And when they came to Troy and the two flowing rivers,
 774  where Simois and Scamander join their streams,
 775  there the goddess white-armed Hera halted the horses,
 776  loosed them from the car, and poured thick mist around them;
 777  and Simois made ambrosia spring up for them to graze.
 778  And the two went with steps like shy doves' in their gait,
 779  hot to bring help to the Argive men.
 780  And when they came to where the most and the best of them
 781  stood packed around the might of Diomedes, breaker of horses,
 782  like lions that eat flesh raw
 783  or wild boars, whose strength does not give out,
 784  there the goddess white-armed Hera stood and shouted,
 785  in the likeness of great-hearted Stentor of the bronze voice,[36]
 786  whose shout was as loud as fifty other men's:
 787  "Shame, Argives! Vile disgraces — admired only for your looks!
 788  While brilliant Achilles still came out to war,[37]
 789  the Trojans never ventured past the Dardanian gates:
 790  they feared his massive spear too much.
 791  Now they fight far from the city, at the hollow ships."
 792  So speaking she stirred the fury and heart of every man.
 793  And the goddess grey-eyed Athena sprang to Tydeus' son.
 794  She found that lord beside his horses and chariot,
 795  cooling the wound Pandarus's arrow had made.
 796  For the sweat was wearing him under the broad strap
 797  of his round shield — it wore him, and his arm was tired;
 798  he was lifting the strap and wiping off the dark-clouded blood.
 799  The goddess laid her hand on his horses' yoke and spoke:
 800  "Little like himself, the son Tydeus got![38]
 801  Tydeus was a small man in build — but a fighter.
 802  Even that time I would not let him fight
 803  or flash to the front — when he came apart from the Achaeans
 804  as a messenger to Thebes, among the Cadmeans in their numbers,
 805  and I told him to feast in their halls at his ease —
 806  even then, keeping that strong heart of his, as always,
 807  he challenged the young Cadmean men, and won everything
 808  with ease: such a helper was I to him.
 809  And you — here I stand beside you, and guard you,
 810  and bid you with good will: fight the Trojans!
 811  But either the weariness of many charges has soaked your limbs,
 812  or some spiritless fear is holding you. Then you are no
 813  son of Tydeus, the war-minded son of Oeneus' line."
 814  Answering her spoke strong Diomedes:
 815  "I know you, goddess, daughter of aegis-bearing Zeus;
 816  so I will tell you my word freely, and hide nothing.
 817  No spiritless fear is holding me, no hanging back:
 818  I am still keeping the commands you laid on me yourself.
 819  You would not let me fight face to face with the blessed gods —
 820  the others; but if Aphrodite, Zeus's daughter,
 821  came into the fighting, her I might wound with the sharp bronze.
 822  That is why I now give ground myself, and why I ordered
 823  all the other Argives to fall in here around me:
 824  for I know it is Ares who is lording it over the battle."
 825  Then answered him the goddess grey-eyed Athena:
 826  "Diomedes, Tydeus' son, delight of my heart —
 827  no: fear neither Ares for that, nor any other
 828  of the immortals: such a helper am I to you.
 829  Come — at Ares first! Drive the single-hoofed horses at him
 830  and strike him at close quarters. No awe of rushing Ares —
 831  that raving thing, that made evil, that turncoat,
 832  who just now stood promising me and Hera to his face
 833  that he would fight the Trojans and help the Argives —
 834  and now he runs with the Trojans, and has forgotten all of it."
 835  So speaking she pushed Sthenelus back off the chariot,
 836  hauling him with her hand — and he leapt down smartly —
 837  and the goddess mounted the car beside brilliant Diomedes,
 838  blazing for the fight; and the beechwood axle groaned aloud[39]
 839  under the weight: it carried a dread goddess, and the best of men.
 840  Pallas Athena took up the whip and the reins,
 841  and at Ares first she drove the single-hoofed horses.
 842  He was in the act of stripping gigantic Periphas,
 843  far the best of the Aetolians, Ochesius' shining son —
 844  blood-stained Ares was stripping him. And Athena
 845  put on the helmet of Hades, so that massive Ares should not see her.[40]
 846  But when Ares, bane of mortals, saw brilliant Diomedes,
 847  he let gigantic Periphas lie where he was,
 848  there where he had first killed him and taken his life,
 849  and made straight for Diomedes, breaker of horses.
 850  And when they were close, advancing on one another,
 851  Ares lunged first, over the yoke and the horses' reins,
 852  with his bronze spear, raging to take his life;
 853  but the goddess grey-eyed Athena caught it in her hand
 854  and thrust it up over the car to spend itself on air.
 855  Second, Diomedes, good at the war-cry, drove in
 856  with his bronze spear; and Pallas Athena rammed it home
 857  into the lowest belly, where the waist-guard girded him:
 858  there he stabbed and struck, and tore the fair flesh open,
 859  and wrenched the spear out again. And brazen Ares bellowed
 860  as loud as nine thousand men, or ten thousand,[41]
 861  cry out in war when they join the strife of the war-god.
 862  And trembling took hold of Achaeans and Trojans alike
 863  in their fear: so loud was the bellow of Ares insatiate of war.
 864  As dark air shows out of clouds
 865  when after heat a foul stormwind rises,
 866  so brazen Ares showed to Diomedes, Tydeus' son,
 867  as he went up with the clouds into the wide sky.
 868  Swiftly he came to the gods' seat, steep Olympus,
 869  and sat down beside Zeus, son of Cronos, aching at heart,
 870  and showed the immortal blood running down from the wound,
 871  and whimpering he spoke winged words:
 872  "Father Zeus, have you no anger, seeing these brute works?
 873  Always we gods endure the most shuddering pains
 874  by one another's devices, when we bring favor to men.
 875  And with you we are all at war: for you fathered the mad,
 876  accursed girl whose mind is forever on lawless deeds.
 877  All the other gods on Olympus
 878  obey you; each one of us is subject to you;
 879  but her you never check by word or deed —
 880  you let her run, because you bore this destroying child yourself.
 881  Now she has set the son of Tydeus, overweening Diomedes,
 882  raging against the immortal gods.
 883  First he wounded Cypris, close in, on the hand at the wrist;
 884  then he charged at me — at me! — like more than man.
 885  My quick feet carried me out of it; otherwise I should long
 886  be suffering agonies there among the ghastly heaps of dead,
 887  or living strengthless from the strokes of the bronze."
 888  Glaring at him darkly, Zeus the cloud-gatherer answered:
 889  "Do not sit beside me and whimper, turncoat.[42]
 890  Most hateful to me are you of all the gods who hold Olympus:
 891  strife is always dear to you, and wars, and battles.
 892  You have your mother Hera's temper — unbearable,
 893  past yielding; I can scarcely break her with words;
 894  and so I think it is by her promptings you suffer this.
 895  Still, I will not bear to see you long in pain:
 896  you are of my stock; your mother bore you to me.
 897  But had you been born so destroying from any other god,
 898  long since you would have lain lower than the sons of Uranus."
 899  So he spoke, and told Paiëon to heal him;
 900  and Paiëon sprinkled pain-killing drugs upon the wound
 901  and healed him: for he was not of mortal make.
 902  As when fig-juice thickens the white milk at a stir,[43]
 903  liquid before, and it curdles fast as a man turns it,
 904  so quickly he healed rushing Ares.
 905  And Hebe bathed him and dressed him in graceful clothing,
 906  and he sat down beside Zeus, son of Cronos, exulting in his glory.
 907  And the two came back to the house of great Zeus —
 908  Hera of Argos and Athena of Alalcomenae —
 909  having stopped the bane of mortals from his slaughter of men.

Notes

[1] (5.1-8) The poem's first aristeia — a single hero's sustained blaze of
    supremacy — opens with literal blaze: Athena kindles fire from
    Diomedes' armor, like "the star of late summer" (Sirius, whose rising
    brings fever-heat; the same star will light Achilles in Book 22).
    The scene-type recurs for Agamemnon (11), Patroclus (16), and
    Achilles (19-22); it will not be re-footnoted.

[2] (5.9-24) The book's first vignette is a priest's sons — and Hephaestus
    saves the survivor "so that his old priest should not be wholly broken
    with grief." A god honoring his priest's grief is the Chryses
    situation of Book 1 in miniature, here granted mercy.

[3] (5.31) "Ares, Ares, bane of mortals, blood-stained stormer of walls"
    (Ἆρες Ἄρες βροτολοιγὲ μιαιφόνε τειχεσιπλῆτα) — the famous triple
    invocation; repeated verbatim by Apollo at 455. The epithets are now
    fixed: βροτολοιγός "bane of mortals," μιαιφόνος "blood-stained,"
    θοῦρος "rushing," χάλκεος "brazen."

[4] (5.51-54) "Artemis herself had taught him... But Artemis was no help
    to him then" — the recurring pathos-pattern of the divine gift that
    fails its owner at the killing moment (cf. the seer's sons at 148-151,
    whose father "read no dreams for them at their going").

[5] (5.59-64) Phereclus, the shipwright who built Paris's ships "that
    began the evil" (ἀρχεκάκους) — the craftsman of the war's first cause,
    loved by Athena, killed in her war. His genealogy is a craft-pedigree:
    son of Tecton ("Carpenter"), Harmon's son ("Joiner"). Aristarchus
    athetized 64 (apparatus).

[6] (5.69-71) Theano — Antenor's wife and (in Book 6) priestess of Athena
    in Troy — reared her husband's bastard "like her own dear children,
    to please her husband": a one-line window into the social workings of
    concubinage and legitimacy in the poem's households.

[7] (5.82-83) "over his eyes closed purple death, and fate the
    overpowering" — the grandest of the death-formulas, reserved for
    moments of special weight; πορφύρεος is the dark surge-color of a
    swelling sea. Fixed rendering hereafter.

[8] (5.127-132) Athena lifts the mist so Diomedes can tell god from man,
    and issues the standing order that structures the whole book: fight
    no immortal — except Aphrodite. His exact obedience (he yields to
    Apollo at 443, cites the order verbatim at 815-824) is what marks
    his god-fighting as sanctioned service rather than hybris — until
    she herself revokes the order at 825-834.

[9] (5.148-151) Eurydamas "read no dreams for them at their going" — the
    dream-interpreter who could not (or did not) read his own sons'
    deaths; the same irony as note [4], sharpened to a profession.

[10] (5.192-216) Pandarus's self-revelation: eleven new chariots at home
     under dust-cloths, the father's advice refused, the horses spared
     their fodder — thrift and half-measures, ending in the bowman's
     curse on his own bow. The poem gives the truce-breaker of Book 4 a
     small-souled domestic interior just before killing him.

[11] (5.249-250) Zenodotus athetized Sthenelus's counsel of retreat
     (apparatus, Line 249-250: ath. Zen.) — perhaps as unworthy of a
     hero. Kept: the exchange sets up Diomedes' refusal ("It is not in
     my blood," 253) and the horse-raid plan.

[12] (5.265-272) The horses of Tros: Zeus's compensation to the mortal
     father for carrying off Ganymede — the poem's matter-of-fact note
     that gods pay for what they take. Anchises' stealth breeding from
     Laomedon's stock supplies Aeneas's team; their theft at 323-327
     re-enacts the pedigree.

[13] (5.290-296) Athena steers the spear through Pandarus's mouth,
     severing "his tongue at the root": the oath-breaker and boaster of
     Book 4 is killed through the organ of his boasting. The poem does
     not say "poetic justice"; it merely aims the spear.

[14] (5.302-304) "two men could not carry it, such as mortals are now"
     (οἷοι νῦν βροτοί εἰσι) — first occurrence of the diminished-present
     formula: the narrator measures his heroes against his own lesser
     age. It recurs at 12.383, 12.449, 20.287.

[15] (5.330) "Cypris" (Κύπρις) — Aphrodite's cult-name from Cyprus, used
     in the Iliad only in this book (330, 422, 458, 760, 883). Kept as
     the name wherever the Greek uses it.

[16] (5.339-342) Ichor: the poem invents divine hematology on the spot —
     gods bleed a different fluid because "they eat no bread, they drink
     no gleaming wine." Wounded, a god is proved bloodless and deathless
     at once; the theology is delivered as physiology.

[17] (5.370-371) Dione — Aphrodite's mother here, otherwise almost absent
     from Greek epic; in Hesiod Aphrodite is born of the sea-foam from
     Uranus's severed flesh, motherless. The Iliad's genealogy makes her
     a daughter of Zeus with a mother whose name is the feminine of Zeus
     (Diōnē ~ Dios). At Dodona, Dione was worshipped as Zeus's consort.

[18] (5.382-404) Dione's consolation-catalogue — mortals have wounded
     gods before: Ares chained thirteen months in a bronze jar by the
     giant twins Otus and Ephialtes; Hera shot in the breast and Hades
     shot "at Pylos among the dead" by Heracles. These are shards of
     older, wilder myth preserved nowhere else; "at Pylos" was already
     disputed in antiquity (the town? or "at the gate" — πύλῳ — of the
     dead?). Paiëon, the gods' own healer (distinct from Apollo in this
     poem), closes the paradigm — and will be needed again at 899.
     Consolation-by-precedent ("others have endured; endure") is the
     poem's standard comfort, used on gods exactly as on men.

[19] (5.406-415) Dione's veiled prophecy: the god-fighter "does not live
     long; his children do not climb to his knees and call him papa" —
     with Diomedes' wife Aegialeia imagined waking the house with her
     mourning. The Iliad lets the warning hang unfulfilled (Diomedes
     survives the poem); later tradition made Aegialeia faithless on his
     return — a grim afterlife for Aphrodite's grudge that the poem does
     not know or does not say.

[20] (5.428-430) "Not to you, my child, are given the works of war" —
     Zeus's gentle, smiling demarcation of spheres; among the most
     quoted lines of the poem in antiquity (Cicero renders it in Latin,
     apparatus at Line 428). The smile, and the fact that it is
     Aphrodite's only wound in the poem, keep the scene comedy.

[21] (5.436-442) Three times he charged — the fourth time "like more
     than man" (δαίμονι ἶσος) — and the god's warning: "never of one
     kind / are the race of immortal gods and of men who walk the
     earth." The poem's theology in a couplet, and the planting of its
     most fateful rhythm: the same three-then-fourth pattern, with the
     same phrase, will kill Patroclus at the hands of this same god
     (16.702-711, 784-793).

[22] (5.449-453) The eidōlon: Apollo fashions a phantom Aeneas, and both
     armies fight over an image while the man heals in the temple. The
     poem's first phantom — a device more at home in the Odyssey and in
     later Helen-legend — dropped here without comment.

[23] (5.471-492) Sarpedon's rebuke of Hector: the great statement of the
     ally's claim — "It is we who do the fighting — we, your mere
     allies," from a man with nothing at stake here, whose wife and
     little son wait in Lycia. It is the seed of his famous speech on
     why kings must fight (12.310-328), and the wife and son return in
     his death-scene (16.431-461). First full appearance of the poem's
     noblest Trojan-side figure.

[24] (5.529-532) "Where men feel shame, more are saved than slain" —
     Agamemnon's couplet on αἰδώς, the battlefield ethic of mutual shame
     that holds lines together; quoted and reused throughout antiquity
     as the classic statement of shame-culture soldiering (it recurs at
     15.561-564).

[25] (5.541-549) The twins Crethon and Orsilochus, with the river
     Alpheius three generations back — note the grandfather Ortilochus
     and grandson Orsilochus: the printed text itself varies the name's
     spelling across one genealogy (Zenodotus regularized both to
     Ortilochus; apparatus, Lines 542-549). Translated as printed,
     variation and all.

[26] (5.576-579) Pylaemenes, the Paphlagonian captain, is killed here by
     Menelaus — and at 13.658-659 he will walk, alive and weeping,
     behind his own son's corpse: the most famous continuity slip in the
     Iliad, noticed since antiquity ("even Homer nods" is Horace's
     phrase for it). The translation of course leaves both passages
     as they stand.

[27] (5.592-593) Enyo, war-goddess ("sacker of cities," 333), leading
     with Ares and carrying Tumult (Κυδοιμός), "the shameless spirit of
     carnage" — the war-demons of 4.440-443 gaining a new member.
     Personifications capitalized, per convention.

[28] (5.638-651) Heracles' sack of Troy, one generation before this war:
     Laomedon (Priam's father) promised divine horses for the rescue of
     his daughter Hesione and cheated the hero of his wage; six ships
     sufficed. The story — Troy already once destroyed for a Laomedon's
     bad faith — shadows the present war with precedent; the poem
     glances at it again at 7.452-453 and 21.441-457.

[29] (5.696-698) Sarpedon's swoon: "his spirit left him... then he
     breathed again" — ψυχή here is literally breath, gasped out and
     blown back by the North Wind. The physiology of the Homeric soul,
     caught in the act of almost leaving.

[30] (5.703-704) "Whom first, whom last did they strip of his arms...?"
     — the narrator's roll-call question, first occurrence; a formula
     for the turning tide, reused at 8.273, 11.299, 16.692 (there
     addressed, devastatingly, to Patroclus himself).

[31] (5.722-732) Hebe assembles the chariot of the gods — eight-spoked
     bronze wheels on an iron axle, gold felloes, bronze tires, silver
     naves and pole — the poem's most loving machine-description, in
     present tenses, as of divine property that simply is. Hebe
     ("Youth"), cupbearer of Book 4, here shows a mechanic's hands;
     at 905 she bathes Ares. The Seasons (749) mind the gates.

[32] (5.734-735) Athena "let fall her soft robe on her father's floor,
     the many-colored robe she herself had made" — the goddess of
     weaving strips her own handiwork to put on her father's tunic for
     war. Zenodotus athetized the couplet (apparatus, Line 734-735);
     kept — the gesture is the whole meaning.

[33] (5.738-744) The aegis unfurled — its fullest description: crowned
     with Rout, set with Strife, Valor, blood-chilling Pursuit, and the
     Gorgon's head, "the portent of aegis-bearing Zeus" (cf. 2.447-449
     for its golden tassels). The golden helmet "fitted with the
     fighting men of a hundred cities" (744) was obscure already in
     antiquity — big enough for the armies of a hundred cities, or
     adorned with them in relief; the translation keeps the strangeness.

[34] (5.749-751) The gates of heaven, "self-moved," groan open — kept by
     the Seasons (Ὧραι), doorkeepers whose "gate" is the thick cloud
     they roll aside or set back. Sky-weather as divine architecture.

[35] (5.770-772) The horizon-leap: as far as a watchman on a headland
     can see into the haze over the wine-dark sea, so far the divine
     horses clear at a single bound. Longinus quotes the lines (On the
     Sublime 9.5) as the measure of Homeric grandeur — "the universe is
     not big enough for their second stride"; the apparatus records the
     citation at Line 772.

[36] (5.785-786) Stentor of the bronze voice, "as loud as fifty other
     men" — the origin of "stentorian." Only here in the poem; ancient
     scholars knew further verses about him (apparatus at 2.609
     mentions them): a famous figure of whom the Iliad keeps only the
     shout.

[37] (5.788-791) Hera's taunt measures the plot: while Achilles fought,
     the Trojans never left their gates; now they fight at the ships.
     The absent hero is the book's invisible unit of measure — the same
     arithmetic Achilles himself will state at 9.352-355. (Echo note.)

[38] (5.800-813) Athena retells the Tydeus embassy — the same story
     Agamemnon told at 4.370-400 as a goad, now told by the goddess who
     was there ("such a helper was I to him"), with the detail that she
     had forbidden Tydeus to fight and been disobeyed with impunity.
     The father's licensed recklessness frames the son's licensed
     obedience: Diomedes' defense (818-824) is that he was following
     her orders exactly. (Echo note.)

[39] (5.838-839) "the beechwood axle groaned aloud under the weight: it
     carried a dread goddess, and the best of men." Aristarchus
     athetized the couplet (apparatus, Line 838-839) — divine weight
     straining mortal carpentry was evidently too physical for him.
     Kept: it is the book's theology of embodied gods in one image.

[40] (5.844-845) The helmet of Hades — the cap of invisibility (Hades'
     name was heard as "the Unseen One"): Athena puts it on so that
     Ares cannot see her, god blinding god. Only here in Homer; later
     myth gives it to Perseus.

[41] (5.859-863) The wounded Ares bellows "as loud as nine thousand
     men, or ten thousand... in war" — the war-god's pain measured in
     the units of his own element. Both armies tremble at a sound they
     cannot place.

[42] (5.888-898) Zeus to Ares: "Most hateful to me are you of all the
     gods who hold Olympus: strife is always dear to you, and wars, and
     battles" — repeating verbatim Agamemnon's words to Achilles at
     1.176-177. The poem's supreme father passes on a mortal king's
     rebuke, god-scale: the quarrel of Book 1 was a theomachy in
     miniature, and this theomachy is a family quarrel. "Turncoat"
     (ἀλλοπρόσαλλος, 831, 889) — Ares' new epithet, "the one who leaps
     to either side." And the threat: born of any other god, Ares would
     lie "lower than the sons of Uranus" — beneath the imprisoned
     Titans, in the pit below Tartarus. (Echo note.)

[43] (5.899-906) Paiëon heals the war-god, and the simile is from the
     dairy: as fig-juice curdles milk, the wound knits — the homeliest
     image in the poem laid against divine flesh. Hebe bathes him, and
     he sits by Zeus "exulting in his glory" — a line repeated from
     1.405 (of Briareus), and athetized here by Aristarchus for that
     reason (apparatus, Line 906). The unrepentant swagger of the
     freshly healed is, either way, perfect Ares.
