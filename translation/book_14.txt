BOOK 14

The Deception of Zeus: Hera borrows the girdle of Aphrodite, buys
Sleep with a Grace, and Zeus sleeps on Gargaron while the sea-god
turns the battle.

TRANSLATOR'S COMMENTARY

The Dios Apatē — the Deception of Zeus — is the Iliad's great comic
interlude, and its comedy is engineering: every scene is a tool
fetched for the next. Hera sees Poseidon working the lines and Zeus
sitting on Ida (153-158); what follows is a bathing and dressing
scene built exactly like an arming scene (note [9]); a lie to
Aphrodite that borrows the deep past — Oceanus "begetting of the
gods" and mother Tethys quarreling — as cover story (note [10]);
the loan of the kestos, the embroidered band that holds "love, and
desire, and the trysting-talk — persuasion, that steals the wits
even of the close-thinkers," a line Aristotle quoted (note [10]);
the recruitment of Sleep, who once before put Zeus under for Hera's
sake and barely survived it — saved only by Night, "subduer of gods
and men," the one power Zeus fears to displease (note [12]); and the
payment, negotiated upward from golden furniture to a Grace, sworn
by Styx with one hand on the earth and one on the shining sea, the
Titans under Tartarus called to witness (note [13]).

The seduction itself is the poem at its most audaciously funny —
Zeus woos his wife with a catalogue of seven prior conquests,
mothers and offspring itemized, ending "not even you yourself, as I
love you now" (317-328; ancient critics athetized the whole list as
indecorous, note [16]) — and then, without warning, at its most
beautiful: the golden cloud, and the earth flowering beneath them,
"dewy lotus, and crocus, and hyacinth, thick and soft, that held
them up off the ground" — the sacred marriage older than the
comedy, glittering dew falling from the cloud (note [18]). Zeus
sleeps; Sleep runs to Poseidon; and the last third of the book is
the counterattack the nap purchases: the armor sorted by size to
the men who can use it, Ajax's boulder spinning Hector "like a top"
and putting him, vomiting blood, out of the poem until Zeus wakes,
and a chain of vaunts — grief answering grief — that ends with
Ilioneus's head raised on a spear "like a poppy-head," the poem's
own earlier poppy (8.306) grown monstrous (note [29]).

Our printed Greek lacks line 269 — the naming of Pasithea in Hera's
offer, an addition in only part of the tradition; the numbering
jumps 268 to 270, the line is translated in note [13], and Sleep
names her anyway at 276: the third transmission-gap this project
reproduces (with 9.458-461 and 11.543). Register notes: 14.82-83
reuse 4.349-350 verbatim ("the fence of your teeth"); 14.508 reuses
the fixed Muse-invocation; the ford-of-Xanthus formula (433-434) is
laid down for reuse at 21.1-2.

 108  young or old — and I would welcome him gladly."

 111  to listen, and each of you does not bristle with resentment
 112  because by birth I am the youngest among you.
 113  I too claim my descent from a noble father,
 114  Tydeus, whom the heaped earth covers now at Thebes.[5]
 115  For to Portheus three blameless sons were born,
 116  and they lived in Pleuron and steep Calydon:
 117  Agrius, and Melas, and the third was the horseman Oeneus,
 118  my father's father — in valor the best of them.
 119  Oeneus stayed there; but my father settled at Argos,
 120  a wanderer: so, I suppose, Zeus and the other gods willed it.
 121  He married a daughter of Adrastus, and lived in a house
 122  rich in substance: he had wheat-bearing fields enough,
 123  and many orchards of trees stood round about,
 124  and he had flocks in plenty; and he surpassed all the Achaeans
 125  with the spear. You will have heard all this — that it is true.
 126  So you cannot say my stock is base and cowardly
 127  and dishonor the word I speak, if I speak it well.
 128  Come: let us go to the battle, wounded as we are. We must.
 129  There, let us hold ourselves out of the combat,
 130  beyond spear-cast, lest anyone take wound upon wound;
 131  but the others we will drive in and send to it — those who before,
 132  indulging their tempers, have stood apart and would not fight."
 133  So he spoke; and they heard him well, and obeyed,
 134  and set off; and the lord of men Agamemnon led them.
 135  But the famed earth-shaker kept no blind man's watch:
 136  he came after them in the likeness of an old man,[6]
 137  and took the right hand of Agamemnon, Atreus' son,
 138  and speaking winged words he addressed him:
 139  "Son of Atreus — now, I think, Achilles' ruinous heart
 140  is rejoicing in his chest, watching the slaughter
 141  and the rout of the Achaeans; for there is no sense in him, none.
 142  Well — let him perish just so; may a god strike him lame.
 143  But with you the blessed gods are not yet wholly angry:
 144  the day is coming, I think, when the leaders and lords of the Trojans
 145  will raise the dust of the wide plain, and you yourself will watch them
 146  running for the city, away from the ships and the huts."
 147  So speaking he gave a great shout, and swept off across the plain.
 148  Loud as nine thousand men cry out, or ten thousand,
 149  in war, when they join the strife of the war-god,
 150  so great was the voice the lord earth-shaker sent
 151  out of his chest; and into each Achaean's heart he threw
 152  great strength, to war and fight without ceasing.[7]
 153  Now Hera of the golden throne looked out and saw them,
 154  standing on her peak of Olympus; and at once she knew
 155  her own brother — her husband's brother too — bustling
 156  through the battle where men win glory, and her heart was glad;
 157  and she saw Zeus, seated on the topmost peak
 158  of Ida of the many springs, and he was hateful to her heart.
 159  Then ox-eyed lady Hera fell to pondering
 160  how she might trick the mind of aegis-bearing Zeus;[8]
 161  and this to her mind seemed the best counsel:
 162  to array herself with care and go to Ida —
 163  in hope that he might desire to lie in love
 164  against her body, and she might pour a sleep, harmless and warm,
 165  over his eyelids and over his close counsels.
 166  She went to her chamber, which her dear son Hephaestus
 167  had built her, fitting the close doors to their posts
 168  with a secret bolt no other god could open.
 169  There she went in, and closed the shining doors.
 170  With ambrosia first she washed from her desirable body
 171  every stain, and anointed herself with rich oil,
 172  ambrosial, soft, and scented for her especially:
 173  if it were so much as stirred in the bronze-floored house of Zeus,
 174  its breath reached earth and heaven alike.
 175  With this she anointed her fair body, and combed her hair,
 176  and with her own hands plaited the shining braids,
 177  beautiful, ambrosial, streaming from her immortal head.
 178  And she put on the ambrosial robe that Athena
 179  had smoothed and worked for her, and set with many figures,
 180  and pinned it across her breast with brooches of gold.
 181  She belted it with a girdle hung with a hundred tassels,
 182  and in her pierced earlobes she hung the earrings,
 183  three-dropped, mulberry-dark; and great grace shone from her.
 184  And over all, brilliant among goddesses, she veiled herself
 185  in a headdress lovely and new, white as the sun,
 186  and beneath her shining feet she bound fair sandals.[9]
 187  And when she had set all this adornment about her body,
 188  she went from the chamber, and calling Aphrodite
 189  apart from the other gods she spoke her word:
 190  "Would you do something for me, dear child, if I ask it?
 191  Or will you refuse me, holding it against me in your heart
 192  that I defend the Danaans, and you the Trojans?"
 193  Then Aphrodite, daughter of Zeus, answered her:
 194  "Hera, eldest goddess, daughter of great Cronos,
 195  say what is in your mind. My heart bids me do it,
 196  if do it I can — if it is a thing that can be done."
 197  And with guile in her heart the lady Hera answered:
 198  "Give me now love, and desire — the power with which
 199  you master all the immortals, and mortal men.
 200  For I am going to the ends of the generous earth, to see
 201  Oceanus, the begetting of the gods, and mother Tethys,
 202  who reared me well in their own halls and cherished me,
 203  taking me from Rhea, when wide-seeing Zeus put Cronos
 204  down beneath the earth and the barren sea.
 205  I go to see them, and to loose their endless quarrel:
 206  for a long time now they have held apart from one another,
 207  from bed and from love, since anger fell upon their hearts.
 208  If with words I could persuade those two dear hearts,
 209  and bring them back to their bed, to be joined in love,
 210  forever I would be called dear to them, and revered."
 211  Then laughter-loving Aphrodite answered her:
 212  "It cannot be, and it would not be right, to refuse your word:
 213  you sleep in the arms of Zeus, and Zeus is highest."
 214  She spoke, and loosed from her breast the pierced band,
 215  embroidered, where all her enchantments lie worked:
 216  in it is love, in it desire, in it the trysting-talk —
 217  persuasion, that steals the wits even of the close-thinkers.[10]
 218  She put it in her hands, and spoke a word, and named her:
 219  "There: take this band and put it away in the fold
 220  of your breast — embroidered: everything is worked into it. And I tell you
 221  you will not come back unaccomplished, whatever your heart intends."
 222  So she spoke; and ox-eyed lady Hera smiled,
 223  and smiling put it away in the fold of her breast.

 234  as once before you heard my word, so now again
 235  obey me; and I will owe you thanks all my days.
 236  Put Zeus's shining eyes to sleep for me beneath his brows,
 237  the moment I have lain beside him in love.
 238  And I will give you gifts: a fair throne, imperishable forever,
 239  golden, which my own son Hephaestus the crook-footed
 240  will make with all his craft; and under it he will set a footstool

 243  "Hera, eldest goddess, daughter of great Cronos,
 244  any other of the gods, the everlasting-born,
 245  I would easily put under — even the streams of the river
 246  Oceanus, who is the begetting of them all;
 247  but Zeus, the son of Cronos — I would not come near him,
 248  much less put him to sleep, unless he himself commanded.
 249  For once before now a command of yours taught me my lesson,
 250  the day when that high-hearted son of Zeus
 251  sailed from Ilion, having sacked the Trojans' city.
 252  I laid the mind of aegis-bearing Zeus asleep,
 253  poured sweet about him; and you devised evils in your heart,
 254  rousing the blasts of punishing winds over the sea,
 255  and carried Heracles away to well-peopled Cos,
 256  far from all his friends. And Zeus, waking, was furious,
 257  hurling gods this way and that through the house; and me above all
 258  he hunted, and would have flung me from the sky into the sea, gone forever,
 259  had Night not saved me — Night, subduer of gods and men.
 260  To her I came running; and he checked himself, for all his anger:
 261  he stood in awe of doing what swift Night would hate.
 262  And now you ask me to do this other impossible thing."
 263  Then ox-eyed lady Hera answered him:
 264  "Sleep, why do you brood on such things in your heart?
 265  Do you think wide-seeing Zeus will rage for the Trojans
 266  as he raged that time over Heracles, his own son?
 267  Come — I will give you one of the younger Graces:
 268  marry her, and let her be called your wife."

 284  to Lecton, where they first left the sea; and over dry land

 293  on lofty Ida; and Zeus the cloud-gatherer saw her.
 294  And the moment he saw her, desire wrapped his close mind round,
 295  as on the day they first mingled in love,
 296  going to the bed again and again behind their dear parents' backs.[15]
 297  He stood before her, and spoke a word, and named her:
 298  "Hera — where are you rushing, that you come down from Olympus
 299  like this — with no horses near, no chariot to mount?"
 300  And with guile in her heart the lady Hera answered:
 301  "I am going to the ends of the generous earth, to see
 302  Oceanus, the begetting of the gods, and mother Tethys,
 303  who reared me well in their own halls and cherished me;
 304  I go to see them, and to loose their endless quarrel:
 305  for a long time now they have held apart from one another,
 306  from bed and from love, since anger fell upon their hearts.
 307  My horses stand at the foot of Ida of the many springs,
 308  ready to carry me over dry land and water both.
 309  It is on your account I have come down from Olympus now —
 310  so that you would not be angry with me afterward, if I went
 311  in silence to the house of deep-flowing Oceanus."
 312  Answering her spoke Zeus the cloud-gatherer:
 313  "Hera — you can set out for there later just as well.
 314  Come, let the two of us go to bed, and turn to love.
 315  For never has desire for goddess or for woman
 316  so flooded and mastered the heart inside my chest —
 317  not when I loved Ixion's wife,[16]
 318  who bore Peirithous, the equal of the gods in counsel;
 319  nor Danaë of the lovely ankles, Acrisius' daughter,
 320  who bore Perseus, most admired of all men;
 321  nor the daughter of far-famed Phoenix,
 322  who bore me Minos and godlike Rhadamanthys;
 323  nor Semele, nor Alcmene in Thebes —
 324  and she bore Heracles, my strong-hearted son,
 325  while Semele bore Dionysus, a joy to mortals;
 326  nor when I loved the lady Demeter of the lovely braids,
 327  nor glorious Leto — no, nor you yourself:
 328  none as I love you now, and sweet desire takes hold of me."
 329  And with guile in her heart the lady Hera answered:
 330  "Most dread son of Cronos, what a word you have spoken![17]
 331  If now you long to lie down in love
 332  on the peaks of Ida, where everything stands open to view —
 333  how would it be if some one of the everlasting gods
 334  saw us asleep, and going among all the gods
 335  told them? I could not rise from that bed and simply
 336  walk home to your house; it would be a scandal.
 337  But if you will — if that is truly your heart's pleasure —
 338  you have a chamber, which your dear son Hephaestus
 339  built you, fitting the close doors to their posts:
 340  let us go and lie there, since the bed is your desire."
 341  Answering her spoke Zeus the cloud-gatherer:
 342  "Hera, have no fear that any god or man
 343  will see: such a cloud will I wrap around us,
 344  a golden one. Not even Helios could spy us through it,
 345  and his light is the sharpest sight there is."
 346  He spoke, and the son of Cronos caught his wife in his arms;
 347  and beneath them the bright earth put out fresh grass,
 348  and dewy lotus, and crocus, and hyacinth,
 349  thick and soft, that held them up off the ground.
 350  In that they lay together, and drew over them a cloud,
 351  fair and golden; and glittering dew-drops fell away from it.[18]

 353  broken by sleep and love, his wife in his arms;

 356  Standing close, he spoke winged words to him:
 357  "Now, Poseidon, help the Danaans with a will,[19]
 358  and hand them the glory — at least a little while, while Zeus
 359  still sleeps; for I have wrapped him in a soft daze,
 360  and Hera has tricked him into bed, into love."

 362  and Poseidon, stirred still more to defend the Danaans,
 363  sprang far out among the foremost at once, and called:
 364  "Argives! Are we to concede the victory again to Hector,
 365  Priam's son — let him take the ships and win the glory?
 366  That is his own claim and his boast, because Achilles
 367  sits by the hollow ships with anger in his heart.
 368  But Achilles will not be missed so very much
 369  if the rest of us rouse ourselves to defend each other.
 370  Come then, let us all do as I say:
 371  let us dress ourselves in the biggest and best shields
 372  in the army, and hide our heads in helmets
 373  blazing all over, and take the longest spears in our hands,
 374  and go. And I will lead; and I say Hector, Priam's son,
 375  will not stand us, however hot he burns.
 376  And whatever staunch fighter has a small shield at his shoulder,
 377  let him give it to a worse man, and get into a bigger."[20]

 379  And the kings themselves, wounded as they were, marshaled them —
 380  Tydeus' son, and Odysseus, and Atreus' son Agamemnon —

 382  the good man got into good armor, and gave the worse to the worse.
 383  And when they had clothed their bodies in the flashing bronze

 385  holding in his heavy hand a terrible thin-edged sword
 386  like a lightning-flash — a thing no man may meet
 387  in the grim combat: dread holds men back from it.[21]
 388  And on the other side glorious Hector marshaled the Trojans.
 389  Then it was that they stretched the most terrible strife of war,
 390  blue-maned Poseidon and glorious Hector,
 391  one defending the Trojans, the other the Argives;
 392  and the sea washed up to the huts and ships
 393  of the Argives, and the armies met with a great war-shout.
 394  Not so loud does the sea's wave roar against the land,[22]
 395  driven up out of open water by the hard North Wind;
 396  not so loud the bellow of blazing fire
 397  in mountain glens, when it rises to burn the timber;
 398  not so loud the wind through the high-crowned oaks —
 399  the wind at its loudest, when its rage is up and roaring —
 400  as the voice of Trojans and Achaeans then,
 401  crying terror, when they sprang upon each other.
 402  At Ajax first glorious Hector cast
 403  his spear, as Ajax stood turned full toward him, and did not miss:
 404  he hit where the two straps lay stretched across the chest,
 405  one for the shield, one for the silver-studded sword;
 406  the two of them saved the soft flesh. And Hector was angered
 407  that his swift missile had flown from his hand in vain,
 408  and drew back into the band of his companions, dodging doom.
 409  But as he went back, great Telamonian Ajax
 410  took up a stone — one of the many that rolled there
 411  under the fighters' feet, props for the swift ships —
 412  and struck his chest, over the shield-rim, close to the neck,
 413  and set him spinning like a top with the blow: he reeled full circle.[23]
 414  As when an oak goes down under the stroke of father Zeus,
 415  torn out by the roots, and the terrible smell of sulphur
 416  rises off it, and courage fails whoever sees it
 417  from close by — for hard is the bolt of great Zeus:
 418  so Hector's fury dropped in an instant to the dust.
 419  His hand let go the spear, and shield and helmet
 420  crumpled down on him, and his armor, intricate with bronze, clashed about him.
 421  With a great shout the sons of the Achaeans ran in,
 422  hoping to drag him off, and hurled their spears
 423  thick and fast; but no one could wound the shepherd of the people
 424  with thrust or throw: before that, the best men ringed him round —
 425  Polydamas, and Aeneas, and brilliant Agenor,
 426  and Sarpedon, leader of the Lycians, and blameless Glaucus;
 427  and of the rest not one neglected him, but they held
 428  their round shields before him. And his companions
 429  lifted him in their arms and carried him out of the toil,
 430  until they reached his swift horses, which stood behind
 431  the battle and the fighting with their charioteer and the intricate chariot;
 432  these carried him toward the city, groaning heavily.
 433  But when they came to the ford of the fair-flowing river,[24]
 434  eddying Xanthus, whom immortal Zeus begot,
 435  there they laid him from the chariot onto the ground, and poured
 436  water over him; and he got his breath, and looked up with his eyes,
 437  and sitting on his knees he vomited dark blood;
 438  then sank back again to the ground, and both his eyes
 439  were covered in black night: the blow still broke his spirit.
 440  And the Argives, when they saw Hector going off,
 441  leapt at the Trojans all the harder, and remembered their war-craft.
 442  There first of all the swift Ajax, Oïleus' son,
 443  sprang in and wounded Satnius with his sharp-pointed spear —
 444  Enops's son, whom the blameless naiad nymph bore
 445  to Enops as he herded by the banks of Satnioeis.[25]
 446  Oïleus' spear-famed son came close and stabbed him
 447  in the flank; he tumbled backward, and around him
 448  Trojans and Danaans joined the strong encounter.
 449  Polydamas the spear-shaker came to avenge him,
 450  Panthous's son, and hit Prothoenor, Areïlycus's son,
 451  in the right shoulder; through the shoulder the massive spear
 452  drove; and falling in the dust he clutched the earth in his palm.
 453  And Polydamas vaunted terribly over him, crying far:
 454  "There — again! From the massive hand of the great-hearted
 455  son of Panthous the javelin did not leap in vain:
 456  one of the Argives caught it in his flesh; and I think
 457  he will lean on it for a staff as he walks down into Hades."[26]
 458  So he spoke, and grief came over the Argives at his vaunting,
 459  and most of all he stirred the heart of war-minded Ajax,
 460  Telamon's son — for the man fell closest to him.
 461  Quickly he cast at the other with his shining spear as he drew back.
 462  Polydamas himself escaped black doom,
 463  springing aside; but Antenor's son Archelochus
 464  caught it — for the gods had counseled his destruction.
 465  It struck him where head and neck join,
 466  at the topmost bone of the spine, and sheared both tendons through;
 467  and his head and mouth and nostrils hit the ground
 468  long before his shins and knees, as he fell.[27]
 469  And Ajax in his turn called out to blameless Polydamas:
 470  "Think it over, Polydamas, and tell me truly:
 471  is this man not a worthy exchange for Prothoenor
 472  dead? He seems to me no base man, of no base line:
 473  a brother of horse-taming Antenor,
 474  or a son — the family look sits close upon him."
 475  He spoke knowing it well; and grief seized the Trojans' hearts.
 476  Then Acamas, standing over his brother, wounded with the spear
 477  Promachus the Boeotian, who was hauling the body by the feet.
 478  And Acamas vaunted terribly over him, crying far:
 479  "Argives — arrow-braggarts — never sated with threats![28]
 480  Not for us alone, I promise you, the toil
 481  and the misery: you too will be killed like this, in your turn.
 482  Look how your Promachus sleeps, beaten down
 483  by my spear — so my brother's blood-price should not go
 484  long unpaid. That is why a man prays for a kinsman
 485  left in his halls: to ward off ruin for him."
 486  So he spoke, and grief came over the Argives at his vaunting,
 487  and most of all he stirred the heart of war-minded Peneleos.
 488  He went for Acamas; and Acamas did not stand the charge
 489  of lord Peneleos; and Peneleos wounded Ilioneus,
 490  son of Phorbas rich in flocks, whom Hermes loved
 491  beyond all the Trojans, and gave him wealth;
 492  and to him his mother had borne one only son, Ilioneus.
 493  Him he stabbed beneath the brow, at the roots of the eye,
 494  and pushed the eyeball out; straight through the eye
 495  and through the nape the spear drove, and he sank down,
 496  stretching out both his hands. And Peneleos, drawing
 497  his sharp sword, hewed through the middle of the neck, and struck
 498  to the ground head and helmet together; and the massive spear
 499  still stood in the eye; and holding it up like a poppy-head[29]
 500  he displayed it to the Trojans, and spoke in triumph:
 501  "Trojans — take a message for me to the dear father
 502  and the mother of illustrious Ilioneus: let them wail in their halls.
 503  For neither will the wife of Promachus, Alegenor's son,
 504  rejoice to meet her dear husband coming home, that day
 505  when we young men of the Achaeans sail back from Troy."
 506  So he spoke; and trembling took the limbs of every one of them,[30]
 507  and each man looked about him for escape from sheer destruction.
 508  Tell me now, Muses, who keep your homes on Olympus —[31]
 509  who was first of the Achaeans to lift the bloody spoils
 510  of men, once the famed earth-shaker had tilted the battle?
 511  Ajax first, Telamon's son, wounded Hyrtius,
 512  Gyrtius' son, leader of the strong-hearted Mysians;
 513  and Antilochus stripped the life from Phalces and Mermerus;
 514  and Meriones killed Morys and Hippotion,
 515  and Teucer brought down Prothoon and Periphetes;
 516  and Atreus' son next wounded Hyperenor, shepherd of the people,
 517  in the flank, and the bronze drank his bowels through,
 518  tearing in; and his soul through the stabbed wound
 519  came hurrying out, and darkness covered his eyes.
 520  But the most were killed by the swift Ajax, Oïleus' son:
 521  for there was no one like him for speed of foot in the chase
 522  when men broke and ran, when Zeus drove the panic in them.[32]

NOTES

[1] (14.1-8) The book opens on the smallest possible hinge: Nestor,
    still in the hut of Book 11, hears the noise change pitch —
    "drinking though he was" — sets down the cup, borrows his son's
    shield (the son has his father's: the family's gear swapped
    down the generations), and steps outside into the catastrophe.
    Machaon is left with the wine and the promised bath: the
    wounded man's war is a warm tub.

[2] (14.16-22) The old man's indecision as ground-swell: the open
    sea darkening "with soundless swell, boding the quick paths of
    the shrill winds — only boding: it neither rolls forward nor to
    either side, until some deciding blast comes down from Zeus."
    Deliberation rendered as weather that has not chosen; among the
    subtlest interior similes in the poem — the mind as sea before
    the decision that will move it, and the decision, as always,
    filed under Zeus.

[3] (14.27-40) The council of the wounded: Diomedes, Odysseus,
    Agamemnon, "leaning on their spears," come up merely to watch —
    the poem's commanders reduced to spectators with walking-
    sticks. The parenthesis on the camp's terraced ship-rows
    (30-36) is the Iliad's clearest picture of its own stage-set:
    the beach too small for the fleet, the ships in tiers filling
    "the whole long mouth of the shore."

[4] (14.82-102) Agamemnon's third flight-proposal (cf. 2.139,
    9.26) — this time under fire, ships to be dragged seaward mid-
    battle — meets the poem's fiercest rebuke, reusing 4.349-350
    verbatim: "what a word has escaped the fence of your teeth!"
    Odysseus's indictment is total: the wish for "some other army,
    a shabby one"; the credo of the war-generation ("for whom Zeus
    has given it, from youth right down to old age, to wind the
    skein of hard wars, till we perish every one" — war as spun
    wool, the life-thread and the war-thread one); the flat
    imperative "Silence!" (σίγα); and the tactical point that
    settles it — armies do not fight while their transport is
    leaving. Agamemnon's answer is the best thing he does in the
    poem: "you have caught me hard... Now let there be a man with
    better counsel than mine — young or old — and I would welcome
    him gladly."

[5] (14.109-132) Diomedes claims the floor by genealogy — Portheus,
    Agrius, Melas, Oeneus; Tydeus the wanderer who married
    Adrastus's daughter and lies "under the heaped earth at
    Thebes" — the poem's fullest version of his family tree,
    deployed for the right to advise elders. The advice itself is
    modest and correct: go down, stay out of range, and shame the
    shirkers in. The youngest speaks last and best (cf. 9.31-49).

[6] (14.135-152) Poseidon as anonymous old man, taking Agamemnon by
    the right hand: the gesture of pledge. His curse on Achilles —
    "let him perish just so; may a god strike him lame" — is the
    harshest thing said of Achilles in the poem, and it is said by
    a god fighting for Achilles' own side. (Zenodotus's text added
    a line making the disguise Phoenix — tidier and much less
    interesting.) Then the shout of "nine thousand men, or ten
    thousand": divine encouragement measured in decibels, repeating
    the strength-verse used of Strife at 11.11-12.

[7] (14.148-152) See above — the shout-lines close with the same
    formula that opened Book 11's Strife-scene: the gods' rallying
    voices are interchangeable parts, and the poem knows it.

[8] (14.153-165) The plan, stated with the poem's usual candor
    about Hera: she saw her brother-in-law working "and her heart
    was glad"; she saw her husband "and he was hateful to her
    heart." The deception is her own invention from desire's raw
    materials: body, sleep, and the borrowed girdle. The Dios
    Apatē is the one stretch of the Iliad where the plot of Zeus
    is successfully counter-plotted — by his wife, with comedy's
    weapons.

[9] (14.166-186) The toilette is built exactly like an arming
    scene — the sequence of items (wash, anoint, robe, girdle,
    earrings, veil, sandals) laid out with the same formulaic
    machinery as greaves-corslet-sword-shield-helmet-spear (cf.
    3.330-338), ending in the same clause that ends armings. Hera
    arms for bed. Every item has its maker or provenance (the
    chamber and bolt by Hephaestus, the robe worked by Athena) —
    divine materiel, like Agamemnon's corslet or Achilles' spear.
    The scent of the oil reaching earth and heaven; the veil
    "white as the sun"; the earrings "three-dropped, mulberry-
    dark": the poem's richest inventory of female adornment.

[10] (14.187-223) The lie to Aphrodite runs on real cosmology:
    Oceanus "the begetting of the gods" and mother Tethys — an
    alternative theogony in which the world-river, not Cronos or
    Zeus, is origin (Sleep repeats it at 246: "the begetting of
    them all"; ancient readers from Plato and Crates on treated
    these lines as Homer's cosmological fragment). The kestos
    himas — the "pierced, embroidered band" — carries the poem's
    finest abstraction-list: "in it is love, in it desire, in it
    the trysting-talk — persuasion, that steals the wits even of
    the close-thinkers" (ὀαριστύς again — the intimacy-word the
    poem put on the front line at 13.291; here in its home
    register). Aristotle quotes the couplet in the Nicomachean
    Ethics (1149b) on the coercive power of desire. Aphrodite's
    consent-line — "you sleep in the arms of Zeus, and Zeus is
    highest" (213) — was athetized in antiquity; it is also,
    dramatically, the girdle working before it is even lent:
    persuasion stealing the wits of the goddess of persuasion.

[11] (14.225-230) The flight-path: Olympus - Pieria - Emathia -
    the Thracian snow-mountains - Athos - Lemnos, feet never
    touching ground. Divine travel in the Iliad is always mapped
    (cf. 13.20-33): the geography is real and sequential, a
    northern-Aegean arc drawn at a goddess's speed.

[12] (14.231-262) Sleep, "brother of Death" (the pairing returns
    with terrible weight at 16.672, carrying Sarpedon home). His
    refusal recalls the poem's deepest backstory: after Heracles
    sacked Troy, Hera used Sleep against Zeus and blew the hero to
    Cos; Zeus woke "hurling gods this way and that through the
    house," and Sleep survived only by running to Night, "subduer
    of gods and men" — "he checked himself: he stood in awe of
    doing what swift Night would hate." The one being before whom
    Zeus restrains himself is primordial darkness; the theology is
    older than Olympus, glimpsed once, through a frightened minor
    god's memory. Note too the escalation of fees: golden throne
    and footstool (furniture, the standard divine honorarium)
    refused; a Grace accepted — Sleep bargains like a craftsman
    who knows his labor's worth.

[13] (14.267-276) Our printed Greek lacks line 269 — "Pasithea, for
    whom you long always, all your days" — the naming of the
    offered Grace inside Hera's speech; the line stands in only
    part of the manuscript tradition (an addition in several
    copies, as the apparatus records), and the numbering here
    jumps 268 to 270. Nothing is lost but an anticipation: Sleep
    himself names Pasithea at 276, with the longing-clause intact.
    This is the third genuine transmission-gap this translation
    reproduces (9.458-461; 11.543). The oath Sleep demands is the
    great one: by Styx, one hand on the earth, one on the shining
    sea, "so that all may be witnesses between us, the gods below
    with Cronos" — the defeated elder gods, "under Tartarus, who
    are called the Titans" (279), summoned as notaries: the old
    world-order exists, in the Iliad, to witness contracts.

[14] (14.283-291) Landfall at Lecton, "and the treetops shook
    beneath their feet"; then Sleep in the tallest fir on Ida,
    "in the likeness of the clear-voiced mountain bird the gods
    call chalkis, and men call kymindis." The double-name motif
    (Briareus/Aegaeon 1.403, Batieia/Myrine 2.813) — the universe
    bilingual, and the poet claiming the gods' dictionary — here
    attached to a real bird (likely a hawk-owl) waiting in a tree
    to watch a seduction.

[15] (14.292-296) Zeus sees her, "and the moment he saw her, desire
    wrapped his close mind round, as on the day they first mingled
    in love, going to the bed again and again behind their dear
    parents' backs." The marriage's beginning as adolescent
    secrecy — brother and sister evading Cronos and Rhea — is the
    poem's one glimpse of the primal domestic scene, and it makes
    the seduction work: what Hera has staged is his memory of
    their youth.

[16] (14.313-328) The Leporello-catalogue: seven loves — Ixion's
    wife (mother of Peirithous), Danaë (Perseus), Europa the
    daughter of Phoenix (Minos and Rhadamanthys), Semele
    (Dionysus, "a joy to mortals" — his only Iliadic mention as
    Zeus's son), Alcmene (Heracles), Demeter, Leto — each with
    issue noted, ending "no, nor you yourself: none as I love you
    now." As courtship, an itemized list of adulteries addressed
    to one's wife is catastrophically tactless; as enchantment-
    symptom it is exact — the girdle's power made audible as
    babble. Aristophanes and Aristarchus athetized all twelve
    lines as unseemly; antiquity laughed and kept them; so does
    the printed text.

[17] (14.330-340) Hera's answer opens with the register's fixed
    outrage-line ("Most dread son of Cronos, what a word you have
    spoken!" = 1.552) — deployed now in mock-modesty: what if some
    god saw us and told? The counter-offer (Hephaestus's chamber
    with the secret bolt) is a trap avoided by Zeus's own
    escalation: the golden cloud.

[18] (14.346-353) The hieros gamos: "beneath them the bright earth
    put out fresh grass, and dewy lotus, and crocus, and hyacinth,
    thick and soft, that held them up off the ground." Earth
    flowering under the union of Sky-god and his queen — a fossil
    of fertility-religion set blooming in the middle of the war-
    poem; the golden cloud rains glittering dew. Then the plainest
    possible clause: "So the father slept." The plot of the Iliad
    from 8.473 onward hangs suspended inside a nap.

[19] (14.354-360) Sleep reports to Poseidon in person — "I have
    wrapped him in a soft daze" — divine conspiracy with courier
    service. The alliance of Sleep and the sea-god frees the
    battle for exactly one Achaean surge, on borrowed time.

[20] (14.364-382) Poseidon's tactical speech, with its remarkable
    order: swap armor by capability — "whatever staunch fighter
    has a small shield at his shoulder, let him give it to a worse
    man, and get into a bigger... the good man got into good
    armor, and gave the worse to the worse." Ancient critics
    athetized (376-377); soldiers will recognize the first
    recorded redistribution of kit by fighting value, ordered by
    a god and executed by three wounded kings.

[21] (14.384-387) Poseidon leads with "a terrible thin-edged sword
    like a lightning-flash — a thing no man may meet in the grim
    combat: dread holds men back from it." The god's blade is not
    said to kill anyone: its work is categorical, a boundary
    (θέμις) — mortals simply may not engage it.

[22] (14.389-401) The triple negative simile: not the sea's wave
    under the North Wind, not fire in the mountain glens, not the
    wind in the high oaks roars so loud as the two armies' voice.
    The poem's own sound-engineering: three natural crescendos
    stacked and overtopped — and all three (wave, fire, wind) are
    the similes this battle has been using for its charges, now
    outshouted by the real thing.

[23] (14.402-420) The duel of the day: Hector's spear defeated by
    the crossing-point of two straps — the war of equipment again
    — and Ajax's answer with one of the ship-props: the stone
    "set him spinning like a top." Then the great oak simile:
    struck by the bolt of Zeus, torn out by the roots, "and the
    terrible smell of sulphur rises off it, and courage fails
    whoever sees it from close by." The lightning-smell is the
    most sensorily exact divine detail in the poem — and its
    theology cuts: Hector, whose whole warrant is Zeus's bolt,
    falls like a tree struck by it, while Zeus sleeps.

[24] (14.433-439) The retreat to "the ford of the fair-flowing
    river, eddying Xanthus, whom immortal Zeus begot" — the line-
    pair that will open Book 21, laid down here; the river-god's
    paternity registered in passing. Hector's revival is clinical
    concussion: water poured over him, breath recovered, "sitting
    on his knees he vomited dark blood," relapse, "black night"
    again. He is out of the poem until 15.239 — and it takes
    Apollo, on Zeus's woken orders, to put him back.

[25] (14.442-448) Satnius, son of Enops and "the blameless naiad
    nymph" who bore him "as Enops herded by the banks of
    Satnioeis": a river-bank begetting, a boy named for the
    river, killed in the mud of a different one. The smallest
    pastoral obituary in the poem.

[26] (14.449-457) Polydamas's vaunt: the spear in Prothoenor's
    shoulder becomes "a staff... as he walks down into Hades" —
    death as an old man's stroll, cane provided by the killer.
    The counselor's wit is colder than the warriors'. The vaunt-
    chain that follows (458 = 486: "grief came over the Argives /
    Trojans at his vaunting") is built as strict alternation —
    grief answering grief, kill for kill, four boasts in sequence:
    the poem's mechanics of escalation laid bare.

[27] (14.463-474) Archelochus, taking the spear meant for
    Polydamas "for the gods had counseled his destruction":
    struck at the join of head and neck, "and his head and mouth
    and nostrils hit the ground long before his shins and knees."
    Ajax's forensic vaunt — no base man, "a brother of horse-
    taming Antenor, or a son — the family look sits close upon
    him" — identifies the corpse by resemblance: kinship legible
    in a dead face, the poem's grimmest use of family likeness.

[28] (14.476-485) Acamas over Promachus: "arrow-braggarts" for
    the Argives; then the blood-price logic said straight — "so
    my brother's blood-price (ποινή) should not go long unpaid.
    That is why a man prays for a kinsman left in his halls: to
    ward off ruin for him." The economy of vengeance (cf. 13.659,
    where Harpalion's death has no ποινή) stated as household
    prudence: brothers are insurance.

[29] (14.489-505) Ilioneus: only son of Hermes-loved, flock-rich
    Phorbas — the eye put out at its roots, the spear through eye
    and nape, the head severed and lifted, spear and all, "like a
    poppy-head" (κώδεια, the seed-capsule). At 8.306-308 a dying
    boy's head drooped "as a poppy in a garden... heavy with seed
    and the rains of spring"; here the poppy returns as a thing
    brandished. Peneleos completes it with the poem's cruelest
    message home: tell his father and mother "to wail in their
    halls" — and Promachus's wife will not rejoice either, when
    the young men sail home. The taunt's real addressees are the
    two houses where the news will arrive; the Iliad's wars are
    always fought, at last, in the rooms of the parents and the
    wives.

[30] (14.506-507) After the poppy-head, no answering vaunt:
    "trembling took the limbs of every one of them, and each man
    looked about him for escape." The chain of reciprocal boasts
    (note [26]) ends because one image exceeded the genre. Even
    this poem's warriors have a limit, and Peneleos found it.

[31] (14.508-510) The fixed Muse-invocation (= 2.484, 11.218) for
    the rout's tally — "who was first of the Achaeans to lift the
    bloody spoils of men" (ἀνδράγρια, "man-spoils," a word that
    occurs nowhere else): full epic formality summoned for a
    plunder-list, as the tide turns on Poseidon's tilt.

[32] (14.511-522) The tally: Hyrtius, Phalces, Mermerus, Morys,
    Hippotion, Prothoon, Periphetes — and Hyperenor, whose death
    at Menelaus's hands is recalled with strange precision at
    17.24-28 (there with an insult-backstory the narrative here
    lacks; the poem's ledgers do not always reconcile). The last
    word goes to the lesser Ajax: "there was no one like him for
    speed of foot in the chase when men broke and ran" — the
    book's closing epitaph is for a sprinter of the pursuit, the
    specialist of the broken field: the Deception of Zeus ends
    with the poem's ugliest hero doing the one thing he does
    best, to men who can no longer fight back.
