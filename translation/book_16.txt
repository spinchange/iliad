BOOK 16

The Patrocleia: Patroclus goes out in Achilles' armor, quenches the
fire, kills Sarpedon — and dies at the wall, struck by a god, a boy,
and Hector, in that order.

TRANSLATOR'S COMMENTARY

Book 16 is the poem's pivot, and it knows it: the narrator turns
eight times to address Patroclus by name — "you answered, Patroclus,
horseman" — an intimacy of grammar shown to no one else in the Iliad
(note [2]). Every promissory note the epic has issued since Book 9
is called in. The dark-spring tears reuse Agamemnon's simile from
9.14-15; Patroclus repeats Nestor's plan of 11.794-803 nearly word
for word, and the narrator stamps it before Achilles can answer:
"So he spoke, begging — the great fool: it was his own death and
evil doom he was begging for" (46-47, note [4]). Achilles' consent
comes wrapped in the conditions that will be violated one by one —
drive them from the ships, then come back; do not push on toward
Ilion, "lest one of the everlasting gods step in: the worker-from-
afar, Apollo, loves them" (93-94) — the death spoken as a warning by
the man who will mourn it (note [6]). The Dodona prayer asks two
things, and "the father granted one, and refused the other" (note
[18]).

At the center, the poem's theology pays its highest price. Zeus
watches his son Sarpedon come against Patroclus and asks Hera
whether he may save him; her answer — every god has sons in this
war — holds the door of fate shut from inside, and the compromise
is the poem's most beautiful invention: Sleep and Death, the twin
brothers, to carry the body home to Lycia for tomb and grave-stone,
"for that is the honor-right of the dead" (457, 675). Zeus consents
and rains blood; Apollo washes the body in the river and hands it
to the twins — the lines Zenodotus deleted and athetized, and the
scene painted on the Euphronios krater three centuries later
(notes [25], [35]). Between father and corpse the poem sets its
most pitiless couplet: the extra hour of glory Zeus grants
Patroclus is deliberated over his head like a sentence (note [34]).

The death itself is built as an un-arming: Apollo, hidden in mist,
strikes the flat-handed blow, and the arming-scene of line 130 runs
backwards — helmet rolling under the hooves ("before this it was
not lawful for that helmet to be fouled with dust"), spear
shattered, shield down, corslet loosed by the god's own hands (note
[41]); then Euphorbus's dart from behind, and only third, Hector,
into the belly. The dying prophecy counts the killers in order and
adds Hector to the list of the dead (note [44]); the soul-flight
lines — "wailing its fate, leaving manhood and youth" — are fixed
here for their verbatim return at 22.361-363, where Hector will
speak a dying prophecy of his own. The book ends with Hector
answering a corpse and the immortal horses, "the gods' shining
gifts to Peleus," carrying Automedon out of his reach.

   1  So they fought on around the well-benched ship;
   2  and Patroclus stood beside Achilles, shepherd of the people,
   3  letting fall hot tears like a spring of dark water[1]
   4  that pours its black stream down a goat-proof cliff.
   5  And swift-footed brilliant Achilles saw him, and pitied him,
   6  and speaking winged words he addressed him:
   7  "Why are you in tears, Patroclus — like a little girl
   8  running at her mother's side, begging to be carried,
   9  clutching her skirt, dragging at her hurrying steps,
  10  and looking up at her in tears until she lifts her:
  11  that is you, Patroclus — spilling soft tears just so.
  12  Have you some news for the Myrmidons, or for me myself?
  13  Some message out of Phthia that you alone have heard?
  14  They say Menoetius, Actor's son, is still living,
  15  and Peleus, Aeacus' son, lives among his Myrmidons —
  16  the two whose deaths would grieve us most of all.
  17  Or are you mourning for the Argives, watching them perish
  18  by the hollow ships for their own high-handedness?
  19  Speak out: hide nothing. Then we will both know."
  20  Then, groaning heavily, you answered, Patroclus, horseman:[2]
  21  "Achilles, son of Peleus, far greatest of the Achaeans,
  22  do not be angry: such grief has overmastered the Achaeans.
  23  For all the men who before were our best
  24  lie by the ships, hit from afar or stabbed close.
  25  Hit is Tydeus' son, strong Diomedes;
  26  stabbed is Odysseus, famed with the spear; and Agamemnon;
  27  and hit too is Eurypylus, by an arrow in the thigh.
  28  Around these men the healers with their many drugs
  29  are busy, tending their wounds. But you, Achilles — there is no dealing with you.
  30  May such anger never take me as this anger you nurse,
  31  cursed in your valor! What good will any late-born man get of you[3]
  32  if you will not beat back shameful ruin from the Argives?
  33  Pitiless man: the horseman Peleus was no father of yours,
  34  nor Thetis your mother — the grey sea bore you,
  35  and the sheer cliffs, so unbending is the mind in you.
  36  But if in your heart you are dodging some prophecy,[4]
  37  something your lady mother has told you from Zeus,
  38  then send me out at least, and let the rest of the army
  39  of Myrmidons follow, and I may be a light to the Danaans;
  40  and give me your own armor to buckle on my shoulders,
  41  so that the Trojans may take me for you, and hold back
  42  from the fighting, and the warlike sons of the Achaeans catch their breath,
  43  worn as they are; for the breathing-spaces of war are few.
  44  Fresh men, with one shout, could easily push men weary
  45  of battle back on the city, away from the ships and the huts."
  46  So he spoke, begging — the great fool: it was his own
  47  death and evil doom he was begging for.
  48  And deeply troubled, swift-footed Achilles answered him:
  49  "Ah, Zeus-sprung Patroclus — what a thing you have said.
  50  I care for no prophecy, none that I know of,
  51  nor has my lady mother told me anything from Zeus.
  52  But this bitter grief keeps coming at my heart and spirit:[5]
  53  when a man is minded to rob his own equal
  54  and take his prize back, just because he outranks him in power.
  55  That is my bitter grief; I have suffered it to the heart.
  56  The girl the sons of the Achaeans picked out as my prize —
  57  won with my own spear when I sacked her walled city —
  58  her the lord Agamemnon took back out of my hands,
  59  Atreus' son, as if I were some vagrant without honor.
  60  But we will let that be — a thing done and past. It seems there was
  61  no way, after all, to be angry in my heart forever;
  62  and yet I said I would not lay my wrath aside
  63  until the moment the war-shout and the fighting reached
  64  my own ships. So: you put my famous armor on your shoulders,
  65  and lead the war-loving Myrmidons into the fight,
  66  since the dark cloud of Trojans has closed hard
  67  around the ships, and the Argives are penned against
  68  the surf-line of the sea, and hold only a little strip
  69  of ground; and the whole city of the Trojans has come out
  70  emboldened — because they do not see the face of my helmet
  71  blazing near them. Soon enough they would be running
  72  and choking the gullies with their dead, if the lord Agamemnon
  73  knew how to deal kindly with me. Instead they besiege the camp.
  74  For no spear rages in the hands of Diomedes,
  75  Tydeus' son, to beat destruction off the Danaans;
  76  and I have not yet heard the voice of Atreus' son
  77  shouting from his hated head. It is man-slaughtering Hector's
  78  voice that breaks around them, calling the Trojans on; and they
  79  hold the whole plain with their war-cry, beating the Achaeans in battle.
  80  Even so, Patroclus — beat the ruin off the ships:
  81  fall on them with all your power, or they will burn
  82  the ships with blazing fire and take away our own dear homecoming.
  83  But obey me — here is the whole end of my word, laid in your mind —
  84  so that you may win me great honor and glory[6]
  85  in the eyes of all the Danaans, and they send back
  86  the beautiful girl, and bring shining gifts besides:
  87  once you have driven them from the ships, come back. And if
  88  the loud-thunderer, Hera's lord, grants you your hour of glory,
  89  do not go lusting after battle apart from me
  90  against the war-loving Trojans — you will strip my honor thinner;
  91  and do not, exulting in the war and the slaughter,
  92  killing Trojans, lead the way to Ilion:
  93  one of the everlasting gods from Olympus may step in —
  94  the worker-from-afar, Apollo, loves them dearly.
  95  Turn back, once you have set the light of safety in the ships,
  96  and leave the rest to cut each other down across the plain.
  97  For — O father Zeus, Athena, Apollo — would[7]
  98  that not one Trojan might escape death, of all there are,
  99  and not one Argive; and that we two alone might slip destruction,
 100  so that alone we might undo Troy's holy coronal of towers."
 101  So the two of them spoke such things to one another.
 102  But Ajax could hold no longer: the missiles pressed him too hard.[8]
 103  The mind of Zeus was breaking him, and the proud Trojans
 104  with their throwing; and the bright helmet at his temples
 105  rang terribly under the blows — struck and struck again
 106  on its well-made cheek-pieces; and his left shoulder ached,
 107  steady so long under the glittering shield; yet they could not
 108  shake him loose for all the missiles they leaned against him.
 109  And always he labored, gasping hard, the sweat streaming
 110  down from every limb of him, and he could nowhere
 111  catch his breath: everywhere evil was propped on evil.
 112  Tell me now, Muses, who keep your homes on Olympus —[9]
 113  how the fire first fell on the ships of the Achaeans.
 114  Hector, coming close, struck the ash spear of Ajax
 115  with his great sword, behind the point at the socket,
 116  and sheared it clean off; and Telamonian Ajax
 117  brandished a docked shaft, useless in his hand, while far away
 118  the bronze point rang on the ground where it fell.
 119  And Ajax knew in his blameless heart, and shuddered at it:
 120  the work of gods — that Zeus who thunders on high was shearing off
 121  all the plans of his fighting, and willed victory to the Trojans.
 122  He drew back out of range. And onto the swift ship they hurled
 123  unwearying fire; and the quenchless flame poured over it at once.
 124  So the fire wrapped the stern of the ship. And Achilles
 125  struck both his thighs and spoke to Patroclus:
 126  "Up, Zeus-sprung Patroclus, master of horses!
 127  I see the rush of consuming fire at the ships.
 128  Before they take them, and there is no escaping left —
 129  get into the armor, quick, and I will gather the men."
 130  So he spoke; and Patroclus armed himself in the flashing bronze.[10]
 131  First he set the greaves along his shins,
 132  fine greaves, fitted with silver ankle-pieces;
 133  second, he put the corslet about his chest,
 134  the intricate, star-bright corslet of the swift-footed son of Aeacus.
 135  Around his shoulders he threw the sword, silver-studded,
 136  bronze; and after that the shield, great and heavy;
 137  and on his powerful head he set the well-made helmet,
 138  horsehair-crested; the plume nodded terribly above it.
 139  And he took two stout spears, fitted to his grip —
 140  only the spear he did not take of Aeacus' blameless son:
 141  heavy, huge, compact — no other of the Achaeans
 142  could swing it; Achilles alone knew how to swing it —
 143  the Pelian ash that Chiron gave his dear father
 144  from the peak of Pelion, to be death for heroes.
 145  The horses he told Automedon to yoke, and quickly —
 146  the man he honored most after Achilles breaker of men,
 147  and trustiest at his post to await the shout of battle.
 148  So Automedon led the swift horses under the yoke,
 149  Xanthus and Balius, the pair who flew with the winds:[11]
 150  the harpy Podarge conceived them by the West Wind
 151  as she grazed in a meadow beside the stream of Ocean.
 152  And in the side-traces he put blameless Pedasus,
 153  whom Achilles brought home when he took Eëtion's city:
 154  a horse who, mortal as he was, kept pace with immortal horses.
 155  And Achilles went down the huts and armed the Myrmidons,
 156  all of them, in their war-gear. And they, like wolves[12]
 157  that eat flesh raw, hearts full of unspeakable courage,
 158  who have brought down a great antlered stag in the mountains
 159  and tear it, and every muzzle among them is red with blood;
 160  and in a pack they go to a spring of dark water
 161  to lap with their thin tongues the black surface-water,
 162  belching up clotted gore; and the heart in their chests
 163  is fearless still, though every belly is stretched:
 164  such were the leaders and lords of the Myrmidons
 165  streaming round the brave aide of the swift-footed son of Aeacus;
 166  and among them stood Achilles the warlike,
 167  urging on the horses and the shield-bearing men.
 168  Fifty were the swift ships that Achilles,[13]
 169  dear to Zeus, led to Troy, and in each one
 170  fifty men, companions at the oar-benches;
 171  and he made five men leaders, and trusted them
 172  to command, while he himself in his great power was king.
 173  One line was led by Menesthius of the glittering corslet,
 174  the son of Spercheius, the sky-fed river:
 175  Peleus' daughter bore him, beautiful Polydora,
 176  a woman bedded with a god, to tireless Spercheius —
 177  though in name to Borus, Perieres' son, who wed her
 178  openly before men, and gave bride-gifts past counting.
 179  The second was led by warlike Eudorus, born
 180  out of wedlock: Polymele, beautiful in the dance,
 181  bore him — Phylas's daughter. Strong Hermes, the slayer of Argus,
 182  fell in love with her, his eyes catching her among the singers
 183  in the dancing-choir of Artemis of the golden distaff, the huntress-crier;
 184  and straightway he climbed to her upper room and lay with her
 185  in secret — Hermes the gracious — and she gave him a shining son,
 186  Eudorus, quick beyond all men to run, and a fighter.
 187  And when Eileithyia of the hard labors had brought him
 188  out into the light, and he had seen the rays of the sun,
 189  the strong might of Echecles, Actor's son,
 190  led her home to his house, once he had given countless bride-gifts;
 191  and old Phylas reared the boy well, and cherished him,
 192  wrapping him round with love, as though he were his own son.
 193  The third was led by warlike Peisander,
 194  Maemalus's son, who outshone all the Myrmidons
 195  in spear-fighting, after Peleus' son's own companion.
 196  The fourth the old horseman Phoenix led;
 197  the fifth, Alcimedon, Laerces' blameless son.
 198  Now when Achilles had set them all with their leaders,
 199  duly marshaled, he laid this hard word upon them:[14]
 200  "Myrmidons — let no man forget for me the threats
 201  you made beside the swift ships against the Trojans
 202  all through my wrath, when every one of you accused me:
 203  'Cruel son of Peleus — your mother reared you on bile!
 204  Pitiless — holding your companions by the ships against their will!
 205  Let us at least sail home again in our seagoing ships,
 206  since this ruinous anger has fallen on your heart.'
 207  So you would gather and mutter at me. Now here it is —
 208  the great work of battle you were lusting for, in plain sight.
 209  Now let each man fight the Trojans with a stout heart."
 210  So speaking he stirred the fury and the heart of every man,
 211  and the ranks locked tighter, once they heard their king.
 212  As when a man fits close the stones of a high house's wall,[15]
 213  building against the strength of the winds,
 214  so close were fitted the helmets and the bossed shields:
 215  buckler pressed on buckler, helmet on helmet, man on man,
 216  and the horsehair crests on the bright ridges touched
 217  as they nodded, so close they stood to one another.
 218  And in front of them all two men armed themselves,
 219  Patroclus and Automedon, of one mind between them:
 220  to fight in the forefront of the Myrmidons. But Achilles
 221  went into his hut, and lifted the lid of a chest,
 222  beautiful, intricate, that silver-footed Thetis[16]
 223  had put aboard his ship to carry, filled with tunics
 224  and wind-proof cloaks and thick-piled rugs.
 225  And in it lay a wrought cup: no other man
 226  drank the fire-bright wine from it, and to no god
 227  did he pour from it, except to Zeus the father.
 228  This he took from the chest and cleansed with sulphur
 229  first, then washed it in fair streams of water,
 230  and washed his own hands, and drew off the fire-bright wine.
 231  Then he prayed, standing in the middle of the yard, and poured the wine
 232  looking up into heaven; and Zeus who delights in thunder marked him well:
 233  "Zeus — lord — Dodonaean — Pelasgian — dweller far off,[17]
 234  ruling over wintry Dodona; and around you the Selloi
 235  live, your interpreters, men of unwashed feet, who sleep on the ground.
 236  Once before, when I prayed, you heard my word:
 237  you did me honor, and struck the Achaean army hard.
 238  So now again accomplish this desire for me.
 239  I myself will stay in the gathering-place of the ships,
 240  but I am sending out my companion, with many Myrmidons,
 241  to fight. Send glory out with him, wide-seeing Zeus,
 242  and make the heart bold in his chest, so that even Hector
 243  may learn whether our aide knows how to make war
 244  alone — or whether his hands rage invincible only
 245  when I myself go into the grind of the war-god.
 246  But when he has driven the fighting and the war-cry from the ships,
 247  then let him come back to me at the swift ships unhurt,
 248  with all his armor, and his close-fighting companions."
 249  So he spoke in prayer, and Zeus the counselor heard him;
 250  and the father granted him one thing, and refused the other:[18]
 251  that Patroclus should push the war and battle back from the ships
 252  he granted; and refused that he come back safe out of the fighting.
 253  So Achilles, having poured and prayed to Zeus the father,
 254  went into his hut again and laid the cup in the chest,
 255  and came out and stood before the hut; for his heart still longed
 256  to watch the dread struggle of Trojans and Achaeans.
 257  And they, armed all together with great-hearted Patroclus,
 258  marched until they charged the Trojans, thinking great things.
 259  At once they came pouring out like roadside wasps[19]
 260  that boys are forever provoking, forever tormenting —
 261  wasps with their nests along the road — as boys will,
 262  the little fools: they make a common evil for many.
 263  If some traveling man going by then stirs the wasps
 264  without meaning it, out they come with fearless hearts,
 265  every one flying at him, defending their young:
 266  with their heart and courage the Myrmidons then
 267  came pouring from the ships; and the quenchless roar rose.
 268  And Patroclus called to his companions with a great shout:
 269  "Myrmidons, companions of Achilles, Peleus' son —
 270  be men, my friends; remember your rushing courage;
 271  so we may honor Peleus' son, who is far the best
 272  of the Argives by the ships — he, and his close-fighting aides —
 273  and so that Atreus' son, wide-ruling Agamemnon,
 274  may know his blindness: that he honored not at all the best of the Achaeans."[20]
 275  So speaking he stirred the fury and the heart of every man,
 276  and they fell on the Trojans in one mass; and around them the ships
 277  echoed terribly under the shouting of the Achaeans.
 278  But the Trojans, when they saw the stout son of Menoetius,
 279  himself and his aide beside him, blazing in their armor,
 280  the heart was shaken in all of them, and the battle-lines wavered:
 281  they thought the swift-footed son of Peleus by the ships
 282  had thrown away his wrath and taken back friendship;
 283  and each man looked about him for escape from sheer destruction.
 284  Then Patroclus first cast with his shining spear,
 285  straight into the middle, where the most men were driven
 286  in confusion around the stern of the ship of great-hearted
 287  Protesilaus, and he struck Pyraechmes — who led the Paeonians,[21]
 288  masters of the chariot, out of Amydon, from the wide-flowing Axius —
 289  in the right shoulder; and he went down on his back in the dust
 290  with a groan, and around him his companions panicked,
 291  the Paeonians: for Patroclus had driven the panic among them all,
 292  killing their leader, who was their best in battle.
 293  He drove them from the ships, and quenched the blazing fire;
 294  and the ship was left there, half-burnt. And the Trojans fled
 295  with an unearthly din, and the Danaans poured after them
 296  along the hollow ships; and the roar rose unabating.
 297  As when from the high crest of a great mountain
 298  Zeus who gathers the lightning moves a close-packed cloud,
 299  and all the lookout-peaks show clear, and the jutting headlands,
 300  and the glens; and from heaven the measureless air breaks open:
 301  so the Danaans, having thrust the enemy fire from the ships,
 302  caught their breath a little. But there was no pause in the war:
 303  for the Trojans, before the Achaeans dear to Ares, were not yet
 304  turned in headlong flight away from the black ships —
 305  they still stood facing them, giving ground from the ships only by need.
 306  Then man killed man, as the battle scattered,
 307  among the leaders. First the stout son of Menoetius
 308  hit Areïlycus in the thigh, just as he turned,
 309  with his sharp-pointed spear, and drove the bronze clean through;
 310  the spear shattered the bone, and he came down on his face
 311  to the ground. Then warlike Menelaus wounded Thoas
 312  where the chest showed bare beside the shield, and unstrung his limbs.
 313  And Phyleus' son, watching Amphiclus as he charged in,
 314  was quicker: he lunged and hit the top of his leg,
 315  where a man's muscle is thickest, and around the spear-point
 316  the sinews were torn through; and darkness covered his eyes.
 317  And of Nestor's sons, one — Antilochus — stabbed Atymnius
 318  with his sharp spear, and drove the bronze point through his flank;
 319  he fell forward. But Maris, close in, rushed Antilochus
 320  with his spear, in fury for his brother,
 321  standing in front of the corpse; but godlike Thrasymedes
 322  was quicker: he lunged before he could stab — and did not miss —
 323  straight at the shoulder; and the spear-point tore the arm's base
 324  away from the muscles, and cracked the bone clean through:
 325  he fell with a thud, and darkness covered his eyes.
 326  So those two, beaten down by two brothers,
 327  went to Erebus — the brave companions of Sarpedon,
 328  spearmen sons of Amisodarus, the man who reared
 329  the raging Chimaera, an evil to many men.[22]
 330  And Ajax, Oïleus' son, sprang on Cleobulus
 331  and took him alive, tangled in the crush; but there and then
 332  he unstrung his strength, driving the hilted sword through his neck:
 333  the whole blade ran hot with blood, and down over his eyes
 334  came purple death, and fate the overpowering.
 335  Peneleos and Lycon ran together: with their spears
 336  they had missed each other, both casts thrown in vain,
 337  so they ran together again with swords. There Lycon
 338  hacked at the ridge of the horsehair-crested helmet,
 339  and the sword shattered at the hilt; but Peneleos struck
 340  his neck below the ear, and the whole blade sank in,
 341  and only skin held: the head lolled sideways, and his limbs were unstrung.
 342  And Meriones, overtaking Acamas on quick feet,
 343  stabbed his right shoulder as he was mounting his chariot;
 344  he crashed from the car, and mist poured down over his eyes.
 345  And Idomeneus stabbed Erymas in the mouth
 346  with the pitiless bronze: the bronze spear passed straight through,
 347  up under the brain, and split the white bones;
 348  his teeth were shaken out, and both his eyes
 349  filled up with blood; and blood through his open mouth
 350  and through his nostrils he blew as he gaped; and death's black cloud wrapped round him.
 351  These, then, were the Danaan leaders, and each killed his man.
 352  As ravaging wolves fall on lambs or on kids,
 353  snatching them out from the flocks, when in the mountains
 354  a shepherd's carelessness has let them scatter — the wolves see,
 355  and swiftly seize the young whose hearts have no courage:
 356  so the Danaans fell on the Trojans; and the Trojans remembered
 357  the harsh clamor of rout, and forgot their rushing courage.
 358  And the great Ajax strained forever to cast his spear
 359  at Hector of the bronze helmet; but Hector, in his war-craft,
 360  his broad shoulders covered by the bull's-hide shield,
 361  watched the whistle of arrows and the thud of javelins.
 362  Truly he knew the tide of the battle had turned;
 363  but even so he stood, and tried to save his loyal companions.
 364  As when a cloud comes from Olympus into heaven[23]
 365  out of the clear upper air, when Zeus stretches the squall,
 366  so the shouting and the rout came from the ships;
 367  and in no good order they crossed back. Hector's own swift horses
 368  carried him off, armor and all; and he left the Trojan army
 369  penned against their will by the dug ditch;
 370  and in the ditch many swift chariot-teams
 371  snapped the pole at the tip and abandoned their lords' cars.
 372  But Patroclus followed, calling hard to the Danaans,
 373  with evil intent for the Trojans; and they in their shrieking rout
 374  choked every path, once their lines were cut; and high up the dust-squall
 375  scattered under the clouds, and the whole-hoofed horses strained
 376  back toward the city, away from the ships and the huts.
 377  And wherever Patroclus saw the most men driven in confusion,
 378  there he steered with a shout; and men kept falling under his axles
 379  face-first from their chariots, and the cars went clattering over.
 380  Straight across the ditch the swift horses leapt —
 381  the immortal horses the gods gave Peleus as shining gifts —
 382  straining onward; and his heart cried out for Hector,
 383  hot to strike him; but Hector's swift horses carried him away.
 384  And as the whole black earth is weighed down under a storm
 385  on a day of harvest-season, when Zeus pours down
 386  his most violent water, when in rage he turns bitter
 387  against men who by force render crooked judgments in the assembly
 388  and drive justice out, careless of the gods' vengeance:
 389  all their rivers rise in flood as they run,
 390  and the torrents cut the hillsides into gullies,
 391  and down to the darkening sea they roar headlong
 392  from the mountains, groaning huge, and the works of men are diminished:
 393  so huge was the groaning of the Trojan mares as they ran.
 394  And Patroclus, when he had cut off the front battle-lines,
 395  turned and penned them back against the ships, and would not let them
 396  climb to the city for all their straining, but in the space between
 397  the ships and the river and the high wall
 398  he swept and killed, and exacted the blood-price for many.[24]
 399  There first he hit Pronous with his shining spear
 400  where the chest showed bare beside the shield, and unstrung his limbs:
 401  he fell with a thud. Then Thestor, Enops's son —
 402  his second rush — Thestor, who sat hunched
 403  in his polished chariot, struck out of his wits, the reins
 404  slipped from his hands: Patroclus came close and stabbed him
 405  through the right jaw, and drove the spear on through his teeth,
 406  and hauled him by the spear-shaft over the chariot-rail — as a man
 407  sitting on a jutting rock hauls a sacred fish
 408  out of the sea with his line and his glittering bronze:
 409  so he hauled him gaping from the chariot on the shining spear
 410  and tumbled him down on his face; and the life left him as he fell.
 411  Then Erylaus, running in — he struck him with a stone
 412  full mid-skull, and the whole head split in two
 413  inside the heavy helmet; and he dropped face-first
 414  to the ground, and death that breaks the spirit poured round him.
 415  Then one upon another — Erymas and Amphoterus and Epaltes,
 416  Tlepolemus Damastor's son, and Echius and Pyris,
 417  Ipheus and Euippus and Polymelus, Argeas' son —
 418  he brought them all, in heaps, to the earth that feeds so many.
 419  But Sarpedon, when he saw his companions of the unbelted tunics[25]
 420  broken under the hands of Patroclus, Menoetius's son,
 421  called out, rebuking the godlike Lycians:
 422  "Shame, Lycians! Where are you running? Now be quick — against him.
 423  I will meet this man myself, and learn
 424  who it is that conquers here — who has worked the Trojans
 425  so much evil already, and unstrung the knees of many good men."
 426  He spoke, and leapt with his weapons from the chariot to the ground;
 427  and Patroclus on the other side, seeing him, sprang from his car.
 428  And they — like hook-clawed, crook-beaked vultures
 429  screaming great cries on a high rock-face as they fight —
 430  so they rushed screaming at one another.
 431  And the son of crooked-counseled Cronos saw them, and pitied,
 432  and spoke to Hera, his sister and his wife:
 433  "Ah me — my Sarpedon, dearest to me of men,
 434  is fated to be broken under Patroclus, Menoetius's son.
 435  And my heart is divided as I ponder it:
 436  whether to snatch him living out of the tearful battle
 437  and set him down in the rich country of Lycia,
 438  or to break him now under the hands of Menoetius's son."
 439  Then ox-eyed lady Hera answered him:
 440  "Most dread son of Cronos, what a word you have spoken!
 441  A man — a mortal, long since doomed to his fate —
 442  and you mean to release him back out of harsh-sounding death?
 443  Do it. But we other gods will not all approve you.
 444  And I will tell you another thing — lay it away in your mind:
 445  if you send Sarpedon living to his own house,
 446  consider: some other god may likewise choose
 447  to send his dear son out of the strong encounter —
 448  for many sons of the immortals fight around Priam's great city,
 449  and you will set loose terrible resentment among their fathers.
 450  But if he is dear to you — if your heart grieves for him —
 451  then let him be broken in the strong encounter
 452  under the hands of Patroclus, Menoetius's son;
 453  but when his soul and his life have left him,
 454  send Death to carry him, and painless Sleep,
 455  until they come to the country of wide Lycia;
 456  and there his brothers and kinsmen will give him burial
 457  with a tomb and a grave-stone: for that is the honor-right of the dead."
 458  So she spoke, and the father of men and gods did not refuse her;
 459  but he rained down drops of blood upon the earth,
 460  honoring his dear son, whom Patroclus was about
 461  to destroy in deep-soiled Troy, far from his fatherland.
 462  And when they were close, advancing on one another,
 463  Patroclus struck far-famed Thrasymelus,
 464  the brave aide of lord Sarpedon,
 465  in the lower belly, and unstrung his limbs.
 466  And Sarpedon, casting second, missed his man
 467  with the shining spear, but wounded the horse Pedasus[26]
 468  in the right shoulder; and he screamed away his life,
 469  and went down crying in the dust, and his spirit flew from him.
 470  The two yoke-horses reared apart, the yoke creaked,
 471  and the reins fouled over them, with the trace-horse lying in the dust.
 472  But spear-famed Automedon found the remedy:
 473  drawing the thin-edged sword from his massive thigh,
 474  he sprang and cut away the trace-horse, and did not fumble;
 475  the pair righted themselves and pulled taut in the harness;
 476  and the two men came together again in heart-devouring strife.
 477  Then Sarpedon missed again with his shining spear:
 478  over Patroclus's left shoulder the point of the spear
 479  passed, and did not strike him. But Patroclus in his turn
 480  rose with the bronze; and his shot did not go in vain from his hand,
 481  but struck where the beating heart is closed in the midriff.
 482  He fell as an oak falls, or a white poplar,[27]
 483  or a towering pine, that carpenters up in the mountains
 484  cut down with fresh-whetted axes to be a ship's timber:
 485  so he lay stretched out before his horses and chariot,
 486  bellowing, clawing at the blood-soaked dust.
 487  As a lion kills a bull, coming in among the herd —
 488  a fiery, great-hearted bull among the shambling cattle —
 489  and it perishes groaning under the lion's jaws:
 490  so under Patroclus the leader of the Lycian shield-men
 491  raged as he was killed; and he called his dear companion by name:
 492  "Glaucus, old friend — fighter among men — now above all
 493  you must be a spearman and a daring man of war;
 494  now let evil battle be your desire, if you are quick at all.
 495  First go everywhere and rouse the leaders of the Lycians
 496  to fight for Sarpedon's body;
 497  and then fight for me yourself with the bronze.
 498  For I will be your shame and your hanging head
 499  all your days, forever after, if the Achaeans
 500  strip my armor here where I fell in the gathering-place of the ships.
 501  Hold on with all your strength, and drive on the whole army."
 502  As he was speaking so, the end that is death covered him —
 503  his eyes and his nostrils. And Patroclus, setting his heel on his chest,
 504  drew the spear out of his flesh, and the midriff followed it:
 505  he pulled out his life and the spear-point together.[28]
 506  And the Myrmidons held the snorting horses there,
 507  plunging to bolt, now they had left their masters' chariot.
 508  But on Glaucus came terrible grief at the sound of that voice,
 509  and his heart was churned, that he could not help him.
 510  He gripped his own arm and pressed it hard, for the wound
 511  wore at him — the wound Teucer's arrow had dealt him as he stormed
 512  the high wall, beating harm away from his companions.
 513  And he spoke in prayer to Apollo who strikes from afar:[29]
 514  "Hear me, lord — you who are somewhere in the rich country of Lycia
 515  or here in Troy: you have power everywhere to hear
 516  a man in grief, as grief has come on me now.
 517  For I have this powerful wound; my arm is pierced
 518  with sharp pains through and through, and the blood
 519  will not dry; my shoulder is heavy under it;
 520  I cannot hold my spear steady, cannot go
 521  and fight the enemy. And the best of men has perished —
 522  Sarpedon, son of Zeus; and Zeus does not defend even his own child.
 523  But you, lord — heal me this powerful wound;
 524  lull the pains, and give me strength, so I may call
 525  my Lycian companions and drive them to the fighting,
 526  and fight myself for the body of the dead man."
 527  So he spoke in prayer, and Phoebus Apollo heard him.
 528  At once he stilled the pains, and on the grievous wound
 529  he dried the dark blood, and threw strength into his heart.
 530  And Glaucus knew it in his heart, and was glad
 531  that the great god had heard his prayer so quickly.
 532  First he went everywhere and roused the leaders of the Lycians
 533  to fight for Sarpedon's body;
 534  then he went with long strides after the Trojans —
 535  after Polydamas, Panthous's son, and brilliant Agenor,
 536  and after Aeneas, and Hector of the bronze helmet;
 537  and standing close he spoke winged words:
 538  "Hector — now you have wholly forgotten your allies,[30]
 539  who for your sake, far from their friends and their fatherland,
 540  breathe out their lives; and you will not help them.
 541  Sarpedon lies dead — the leader of the Lycian shield-men,
 542  who kept Lycia safe by his judgments and his strength:
 543  brazen Ares has broken him on Patroclus's spear.
 544  Friends, stand by him! Feel the outrage in your hearts,
 545  or the Myrmidons will strip his armor and defile his body,
 546  in anger for all the Danaans we have killed,
 547  the men our spears cut down beside the swift ships."
 548  So he spoke; and grief seized the Trojans, head to foot,
 549  unbearable, not to be borne down — for he was a stay of their city,
 550  foreigner though he was: many men had followed him,
 551  and he himself was their best in battle.
 552  They went straight for the Danaans, hot; and Hector led them,
 553  angered for Sarpedon. But the Achaeans were driven on
 554  by the shaggy heart of Patroclus, Menoetius's son:
 555  he spoke first to the two Ajaxes, eager as they already were:
 556  "You two Ajaxes — now let your pleasure be defense,
 557  the men you were among men before — or braver.
 558  The man lies dead who first leapt inside the wall of the Achaeans:
 559  Sarpedon. If only we could take him and defile him,
 560  and strip the armor from his shoulders, and break
 561  some companion of his defending him, with the pitiless bronze."
 562  So he spoke; and they themselves were furious to beat the enemy off.
 563  And when on both sides they had strengthened their battle-lines,
 564  Trojans and Lycians, Myrmidons and Achaeans,
 565  they clashed together to fight around the dead man's body
 566  with a terrible war-shout; and the men's armor rang loud.
 567  And Zeus stretched a deadly night over the strong encounter,[31]
 568  so that around his dear son the deadly toil of battle might be waged.
 569  And the Trojans at first pushed back the bright-eyed Achaeans,
 570  for a man was hit who was by no means the worst of the Myrmidons:
 571  brilliant Epeigeus, son of great-hearted Agacles,[32]
 572  who was king once in well-peopled Budeum —
 573  before: but having killed a noble cousin,
 574  he came a suppliant to Peleus and silver-footed Thetis,
 575  and they sent him to follow Achilles, breaker of men,
 576  to Ilion of the fine colts, to fight the Trojans.
 577  He was laying hold of the corpse when glorious Hector
 578  struck him with a stone full on the head; and the whole head split in two
 579  inside the heavy helmet; and he dropped face-first
 580  over the dead man, and death that breaks the spirit poured round him.
 581  And grief came over Patroclus for his fallen companion,
 582  and he swept straight through the front-fighters like a swift hawk
 583  that sends the daws and starlings scattering:
 584  straight so, Patroclus, master of horses, you swept
 585  at the Lycians and Trojans, with anger in your heart for your companion.
 586  And he struck Sthenelaus, Ithaemenes' dear son,
 587  on the neck with a stone, and tore the tendons from it;
 588  and the front-fighters gave ground, and glorious Hector.
 589  As far as the carry of a long throwing-spear
 590  a man sends flying, testing his strength in the games
 591  or in war, against enemies who break the spirit:
 592  so far the Trojans gave, and the Achaeans pushed them.
 593  Then Glaucus first, the leader of the Lycian shield-men,
 594  turned, and killed great-hearted Bathycles,
 595  the dear son of Chalcon, whose home was in Hellas —
 596  a man who stood out among the Myrmidons for riches and wealth:
 597  him Glaucus stabbed full in the chest with his spear,
 598  wheeling suddenly, just as the man's pursuit had caught him.
 599  He fell with a thud; and close grief took the Achaeans,
 600  so good a man went down; and the Trojans rejoiced greatly,
 601  and came and stood in a mass around him. But the Achaeans
 602  did not forget their courage: they carried their fury straight at them.
 603  Then Meriones took a Trojan fighter, helmed in bronze —
 604  Laogonus, daring son of Onetor, who was priest
 605  of Idaean Zeus, and honored like a god by the people:
 606  he struck him below the jaw, under the ear; and quickly the spirit
 607  went out of his limbs, and the hateful darkness took him.
 608  And Aeneas let fly his bronze spear at Meriones,
 609  hoping to catch him as he came on under his shield;
 610  but he saw it coming and dodged the bronze spear,
 611  ducking forward; and behind him the long spear
 612  stuck fast in the ground, and the butt-end stood quivering;
 613  and there at last massive Ares stilled its fury —
 614  and the point of Aeneas's spear went quivering
 615  into the earth, hurled in vain from his massive hand.
 616  And Aeneas was angered at heart, and called out:
 617  "Meriones — dancer though you are, my spear[33]
 618  would have stopped your dancing for good, if I had only hit you."
 619  And spear-famed Meriones answered him:
 620  "Aeneas — strong as you are, it is hard for you
 621  to quench the fury of every man who stands
 622  against you in defense. You too were made mortal.
 623  And if I hit you square in the middle with the sharp bronze,
 624  strong as you are, and sure of your hands, you would quickly
 625  give the triumph to me, and your soul to Hades of the famed colts."
 626  So he spoke; and the stout son of Menoetius rebuked him:
 627  "Meriones — you are a brave man: why speechify?
 628  Old friend, the Trojans will not back away from the corpse
 629  for insults. The earth will hold down some of them first.
 630  In hands lies the outcome of war; of words, in council.
 631  So there is no need to pile up speech, but to fight."
 632  So speaking he led the way, and the other followed, a man like a god.
 633  And from them — as the din of woodcutting men rises
 634  in the mountain glens, and the sound carries far:
 635  so from the wide-wayed earth their pounding rose,
 636  of bronze and leather and well-made oxhide shields,
 637  stabbed at with swords and double-curved spears.
 638  And by now not even a discerning man could have known
 639  brilliant Sarpedon, wrapped from his head clean down
 640  to the soles of his feet in missiles and blood and dust.[34]
 641  And they crowded round the corpse always — as flies
 642  in a farmstead buzz about the brimming milk-pails
 643  in the season of spring, when the milk wets the vessels:
 644  so they crowded round the corpse. And Zeus never once
 645  turned his shining eyes away from the strong encounter,
 646  but gazed down at them always, and pondered in his heart,
 647  brooding long over the killing of Patroclus:[35]
 648  whether now, in the strong encounter, over godlike
 649  Sarpedon, glorious Hector should cut him down
 650  with the bronze, and strip the armor from his shoulders —
 651  or whether he should swell the sheer toil for still more men.
 652  And as he pondered, this seemed to him the better course:
 653  that the brave aide of Achilles, Peleus' son,
 654  should drive the Trojans and Hector of the bronze helmet
 655  back toward the city once more, and take the life from many.
 656  And in Hector first he put a strengthless heart:
 657  he climbed into his chariot and turned to flee, and called
 658  the other Trojans to fly; for he knew the sacred scales of Zeus.
 659  Then not even the powerful Lycians stood, but panicked
 660  all of them, when they saw their king struck through the heart,
 661  lying under the pile of dead — for many had fallen
 662  over him, once the son of Cronos stretched the strife taut.
 663  And the Achaeans stripped the armor from Sarpedon's shoulders,
 664  bronze and glittering; and the stout son of Menoetius
 665  gave it to his companions to carry to the hollow ships.
 666  And then Zeus the cloud-gatherer spoke to Apollo:[36]
 667  "Up now, dear Phoebus: go cleanse Sarpedon of the dark
 668  clotted blood, out of the range of the missiles, and carry him
 669  far away, and wash him in the running river,
 670  and anoint him with ambrosia, and put immortal clothing round him;
 671  and send him to be carried by swift escorts,
 672  the twin brothers Sleep and Death, who quickly
 673  will set him down in the rich country of wide Lycia.
 674  There his brothers and kinsmen will give him burial
 675  with a tomb and a grave-stone: for that is the honor-right of the dead."
 676  So he spoke, and Apollo did not disobey his father,
 677  but went down from the mountains of Ida into the dread din,
 678  and at once lifted brilliant Sarpedon out of the missiles,
 679  and carried him far away, and washed him in the running river,
 680  and anointed him with ambrosia, and put immortal clothing round him;
 681  and he sent him to be carried by swift escorts,
 682  the twin brothers Sleep and Death, who quickly
 683  set him down in the rich country of wide Lycia.
 684  But Patroclus, calling to his horses and Automedon,[37]
 685  went after the Trojans and Lycians — and was greatly blinded,
 686  the fool: had he kept the word of Peleus' son,
 687  he would have escaped the evil doom of black death.
 688  But always the mind of Zeus is stronger than men's:
 689  he sends even the brave man running, and strips away victory
 690  easily — and then again himself drives a man to fight;
 691  and it was he who now put the fury in Patroclus's chest.
 692  Then whom first, whom last did you strip of his arms,
 693  Patroclus, when the gods were calling you deathward?
 694  Adrestus first, and Autonous, and Echeclus,
 695  and Perimus, Megas's son, and Epistor, and Melanippus,
 696  and after them Elasus, and Mulius, and Pylartes:
 697  these he took; and the rest were each man minded to run.
 698  And then the sons of the Achaeans would have taken high-gated Troy
 699  by the hands of Patroclus — such was his raging spear-charge —
 700  had not Phoebus Apollo taken his stand on the well-built tower,
 701  with deadly thoughts for him, siding with the Trojans.
 702  Three times Patroclus set his foot on the angle of the high wall,
 703  and three times Apollo slapped him back and away,
 704  thrusting at the shining shield with his immortal hands.
 705  But when for the fourth time he came on like more than man,
 706  the god shouted terribly, and spoke winged words:
 707  "Back, Zeus-sprung Patroclus! It is not your portion
 708  that the proud Trojans' city fall to your spear —
 709  no, nor to Achilles', who is a far better man than you."
 710  So he spoke; and Patroclus gave back a long way,
 711  dodging the wrath of Apollo who strikes from afar.
 712  But Hector held his whole-hoofed horses at the Scaean Gates,
 713  divided: whether to drive back into the confusion and fight,
 714  or shout for the army to gather inside the wall.
 715  And as he pondered, Phoebus Apollo came and stood by him[38]
 716  in the likeness of a man young and strong,
 717  Asius — the uncle of horse-taming Hector,
 718  own brother of Hecuba, and son of Dymas,
 719  who lived in Phrygia on the streams of Sangarius;
 720  in his likeness Apollo, son of Zeus, spoke to him:
 721  "Hector, why have you dropped out of the fighting? It is not right.
 722  Would that I were as much stronger than you as I am weaker:
 723  then you would soon and bitterly regret this skulking from the war.
 724  Come — drive your strong-hoofed horses at Patroclus,
 725  and you may take him yet, and Apollo hand you the glory."
 726  So speaking, the god went back into the toil of men;
 727  and glorious Hector ordered war-minded Cebriones
 728  to lash the horses into the battle. And Apollo
 729  sank into the crowd, and hurled an evil rout
 730  among the Argives, and handed glory to the Trojans and Hector.
 731  And Hector let the other Danaans be — killed none of them —
 732  but drove his strong-hoofed horses straight at Patroclus;
 733  and Patroclus, facing him, sprang from his chariot to the ground
 734  with his spear in his left hand; and in the other he caught up a stone,
 735  jagged marble, glittering — his hand just closed around it —
 736  and planted himself and hurled it, not shrinking long from the man;
 737  and his missile was not wasted: he struck Hector's charioteer —[39]
 738  Cebriones, a bastard son of far-famed Priam —
 739  with the reins in his hands, full on the forehead with the sharp stone.
 740  The stone crushed both brows together; the bone did not hold;
 741  and his eyes dropped to the ground in the dust
 742  there at his feet; and like a diver he plunged
 743  from the well-wrought chariot, and the life left his bones.
 744  And mocking him you spoke, Patroclus, horseman:
 745  "Well, well — how nimble the man is! What easy tumbling!
 746  If ever he found himself on the fish-teeming sea,
 747  this man could feed a whole crew, diving for oysters,
 748  leaping overboard even in rough weather —
 749  as easily as he tumbles now from his chariot onto the plain.
 750  So: there are acrobats among the Trojans too."
 751  So speaking he went for the hero Cebriones
 752  with the spring of a lion who, ravaging the farmsteads,
 753  has been struck in the chest — and his own courage kills him:
 754  so, Patroclus, you leapt on Cebriones, blazing.
 755  And Hector on the other side sprang from his chariot to the ground;
 756  and the two fought over Cebriones like two lions
 757  that on a mountain's peaks, over a slain hind,
 758  both of them hungry, fight with towering hearts:
 759  so over Cebriones the two masters of the war-shout,
 760  Patroclus, Menoetius's son, and glorious Hector,
 761  strained to hew each other's flesh with the pitiless bronze.
 762  Hector had him by the head, and would not let go;
 763  Patroclus, opposite, held a foot; and around them the rest,
 764  Trojans and Danaans, joined the strong encounter.
 765  As the East Wind and the South contend with one another[40]
 766  in the mountain glens, to shake the deep timber —
 767  oak and ash and smooth-barked cornel-tree,
 768  that hurl their long slim branches each against each
 769  with a prodigious noise, and the crash of them breaking:
 770  so Trojans and Achaeans, springing at one another,
 771  cut men down, and neither side thought of ruinous flight.
 772  And around Cebriones many sharp spears stood fixed,
 773  and winged arrows leaping from the bowstrings,
 774  and many great stones battered against the shields
 775  of the men fighting round him. And he in the whirl of dust
 776  lay mighty in his mightiness, his horsemanship all forgotten.
 777  So long as the sun bestrode the middle heaven,[41]
 778  so long the missiles of both sides struck home, and the people fell;
 779  but when the sun turned toward the unyoking of oxen,
 780  then, beyond their portion, the Achaeans proved the stronger.
 781  They dragged the hero Cebriones out of the missiles,
 782  out of the Trojans' clamor, and stripped the armor from his shoulders;
 783  and Patroclus, with evil intent for the Trojans, charged them.
 784  Three times he charged then, the equal of swift Ares,
 785  screaming terribly, and three times he killed nine men.
 786  But when for the fourth time he came on like more than man —
 787  then, Patroclus, the end of your life showed clear:
 788  for Phoebus met you in the strong encounter,
 789  terrible. And Patroclus did not see him coming through the confusion,
 790  for he came against him wrapped in thick mist,
 791  and stood behind him, and struck his back and broad shoulders
 792  with the flat of his hand; and his eyes spun.
 793  And Phoebus Apollo knocked the helmet from his head:[42]
 794  it rolled ringing away beneath the horses' hooves,
 795  the socketed helmet, and the plumes were fouled
 796  with blood and dust. Before this it was not lawful
 797  for that horsehair-crested helmet to be fouled with dust:
 798  it guarded the head and the gracious brow of a godlike
 799  man, Achilles. But then Zeus gave it to Hector
 800  to wear on his own head; and his destruction was near him.
 801  And the long-shadowed spear was shattered whole in his hands,
 802  heavy, huge, compact, helmeted in bronze; and from his shoulders
 803  the fringed shield with its baldric dropped to the ground;
 804  and the lord Apollo, son of Zeus, loosed the corslet off him.
 805  Blindness took his mind; his shining limbs were unstrung;
 806  he stood dazed. And from behind, at close range, a Dardanian
 807  struck him with a sharp spear between the shoulders:
 808  Euphorbus, Panthous's son, who outshone all men of his age[43]
 809  at the spear, at horsemanship, and in the race on foot;
 810  indeed he had already brought down twenty men from their horses,
 811  though he came the first time with his chariot, still learning war.
 812  He it was who first sent a missile into you, Patroclus, horseman,
 813  and did not break you; and he ran back and merged with the crowd,
 814  snatching the ash spear out of the wound — he did not stand
 815  against Patroclus, even unarmed as he was, in the combat.
 816  And Patroclus, broken by the god's blow and the spear,
 817  drew back into the band of his companions, dodging doom.
 818  But Hector, when he saw great-hearted Patroclus
 819  drawing back, wounded by the sharp bronze,
 820  came close on him through the ranks, and stabbed with his spear
 821  into the pit of his belly, and drove the bronze clean through:
 822  he fell with a thud, and hugely grieved the Achaean army.
 823  As a lion overpowers a tireless boar in combat[44]
 824  when the two fight, towering-hearted, on a mountain's peaks
 825  over a little spring, both set on drinking,
 826  and the lion by main strength breaks the boar as he gasps:
 827  so the son of Menoetius, killer of many, was robbed
 828  of his life at close quarters by Hector, Priam's son, with the spear;
 829  and vaunting over him Hector spoke winged words:
 830  "Patroclus — no doubt you said you would raze our city,
 831  and take from the women of Troy the day of freedom,
 832  and carry them off in your ships to your dear fatherland —
 833  fool! Before them the swift horses of Hector
 834  stride full-stretch to the fighting; and I with my spear
 835  am chosen out among the war-loving Trojans, I who beat off
 836  the day of necessity for them. But you — the vultures will eat you here.
 837  Poor wretch: not even Achilles, brave as he is, was any use to you,
 838  who stayed behind, I suppose, and gave you strict orders as you left:
 839  'Do not come back to me, Patroclus, master of horses,
 840  back to the hollow ships, before you have torn the bloody
 841  tunic around the chest of man-slaughtering Hector.'
 842  So he must have talked to you — and persuaded your witless heart."
 843  And with the little strength left you, you answered, Patroclus, horseman:
 844  "Boast your fill now, Hector. Yours is the victory —[45]
 845  given by Zeus, the son of Cronos, and by Apollo: they broke me
 846  easily; for they themselves took the armor from my shoulders.
 847  Twenty men like you could have come against me,
 848  and all of them would have died here, broken under my spear.
 849  No — deadly fate has killed me, and Leto's son,
 850  and of men, Euphorbus. You are only the third at my stripping.
 851  And I will tell you another thing — lay it away in your mind:
 852  you yourself have no long life left. Already
 853  death stands close beside you, and fate the overpowering:
 854  you will be broken under the hands of Achilles, Aeacus' blameless son."
 855  Even as he spoke, the end that is death covered him;
 856  and his soul, flying from his limbs, went down to the house of Hades,
 857  wailing its fate, leaving its manhood and its youth.
 858  And glorious Hector spoke to him — though he was dead:
 859  "Patroclus — why prophesy my sheer destruction?
 860  Who knows whether Achilles, the son of lovely-haired Thetis,
 861  may not be hit first by my spear, and lose his own life?"
 862  So speaking, he set his heel on him and drew the bronze spear
 863  out of the wound, and shoved him backward off the spear.
 864  And at once he went with the spear after Automedon,
 865  the godlike aide of the swift-footed son of Aeacus —
 866  hot to strike him; but the swift horses carried him away,
 867  the immortal horses the gods gave Peleus as shining gifts.

NOTES

[1] (16.2-11) Patroclus's tears reuse Agamemnon's simile from
    9.14-15, our English kept — "like a spring of dark water that
    pours its black stream down a goat-proof cliff": the general
    wept so for his army's ruin; the friend weeps so for the same
    army now. Achilles answers with the poem's tenderest teasing:
    the little girl running at her mother's skirt, begging to be
    carried — mockery whose every detail is affection, and whose
    image (the child who will not be put down until she is lifted)
    is exactly what Patroclus is about to ask for and receive.

[2] (16.20) "you answered, Patroclus, horseman" — the narrator's
    apostrophe, used of Patroclus eight times in this book (20,
    584-587, 693-695, 744-746, 754-756, 787-789, 812-814, 843-845)
    — far more than anyone else receives in the whole poem
    (elsewhere chiefly Menelaus). The grammar itself breaks
    neutrality: the singer cannot keep the third person for this
    man. Antiquity already read the device as pathos; nothing else
    in the Iliad marks a character as loved by the poem itself.

[3] (16.29-35) "you and your cursed valor" — αἰναρέτη, a word
    invented for this line (excellence-in-disaster, valor that
    curses); then the great disowning: "the horseman Peleus was no
    father of yours, nor Thetis your mother — the grey sea bore
    you, and the sheer cliffs." The gentlest man in the poem
    delivers its harshest genealogy (he had feared to hear a
    smaller version at 11.654); Patroclus's twin charges — pitiless
    (νηλεές) and unbending (ἀπηνής) — are the exact vocabulary the
    poem will need again for Achilles in Book 24.

[4] (16.36-47) The plan repeats Nestor's lines (11.794-803) nearly
    verbatim, our English kept — the fatal scheme now carried by
    its victim, in the first person ("send me out... give me your
    own armor"). The narrator's seal, before Achilles can answer:
    "So he spoke, begging — the great fool (μέγα νήπιος): it was
    his own death and evil doom he was begging for." The poem's
    strongest use of its fool-formula: not blindness to a detail,
    but a prayer with its object inverted.

[5] (16.49-63) Achilles denies the prophecy excuse — no oracle,
    nothing from his mother (though 9.410-416 told us of the two
    fates; the poem lets the contradiction stand as suppression
    rather than ignorance) — and restates the grievance in the
    quarrel's oldest words: the prize taken back "as though I were
    some vagrant without rights" (ἀτίμητον μετανάστην, the phrase
    of 9.648). Then the concession that has already expired: "I
    said I would not lay my wrath aside until the war-shout reached
    my own ships" — it has; the fire is minutes away.

[6] (16.64-96) The conditions. Drive them from the ships; win me
    honor so they return the girl "and bring shining gifts
    besides" (the Book 9 offer, still wanted — the wrath's economy
    intact); and then the double interdiction: come back; do not
    lead toward Ilion, "one of the everlasting gods may step in —
    the worker-from-afar, Apollo, loves them dearly." Achilles
    speaks his friend's actual death as a hypothesis, naming the
    god. Zenodotus compressed 93-96 into a single line ("lest
    Hector of the flashing helmet catch you stripped bare") —
    tidier, and deaf to the point: the danger Achilles fears is
    not a man.

[7] (16.97-100) The prayer that ends the speech: may every Trojan
    die, and every Argive, "and that we two alone might slip
    destruction, so that alone we might undo Troy's holy coronal
    of towers." Zenodotus and Aristarchus athetized the lines
    (Aristarchus noting that Zenodotus "suspected" them — ancient
    readers already heard how much the wish contains). It is the
    wrath's logical terminus: the army abolished, the war reduced
    to the two of them — spoken to the man whose death will prove
    they were never separable from the rest.

[8] (16.102-111) Ajax's last stand at the ship, told in pure
    physiology: the helmet ringing "struck and struck again," the
    left shoulder aching under the shield, the gasping, the sweat
    that "could nowhere catch its breath" — and the summary line
    ancient critics loved to quote: "everywhere evil was propped
    on evil" (κακὸν κακῷ ἐστήρικτο). The bravest man in the army
    is described entirely from inside his fatigue.

[9] (16.112-124) The fixed Muse-invocation (= 2.484) — summoned
    not for a catalogue but for a single fact: "how the fire first
    fell on the ships." This is the flare Zeus has waited for
    since 15.599-600. Hector shears the head off Ajax's ash spear
    — the docked shaft brandished "useless in his hand" — and Ajax
    reads the theology correctly and withdraws; the flame pours
    over Protesilaus's ship (the war's first casualty again,
    15.705); and Achilles, watching, sees exactly what Zeus sees,
    and slaps his thighs: "Up, Zeus-sprung Patroclus!" One ship
    burns for one book: the pivot's mechanism, precise as a clock.

[10] (16.131-145) The arming — the register's standard sequence
    (greaves, corslet, sword, shield, helmet, spears) with one
    exception the poem underlines: "only the spear he did not take
    of Aeacus' blameless son... the Pelian ash that Chiron gave
    his dear father from the peak of Pelion, to be death for
    heroes." No other Achaean can even swing it. Zenodotus omitted
    the lines; they are the scene's whole meaning: the disguise is
    complete except for the one thing that is Achilles — and the
    poem measures Patroclus by the weapon he cannot lift, exactly
    as it will measure Hector by the armor he cannot fill.

[11] (16.145-155) The team: Xanthus and Balius, foaled by the
    harpy Podarge to the West Wind in the meadow by Ocean —
    wind-grandsons; and in the side-traces "blameless Pedasus,"
    the war-prize from Eëtion's city, "who, mortal as he was, kept
    pace with immortal horses." The poem builds the team as it
    built the expedition: an immortal pair with one mortal running
    beside them, keeping up. The trace-horse's death (467-471) is
    the book's first fatality among Achilles' own.

[12] (16.156-164) The Myrmidon wolf simile — the pack at its most
    physical in Homer: raw flesh, muzzles red, "belching up
    clotted gore," the thin tongues lapping black spring-water,
    bellies stretched and hearts still fearless. The dark spring
    of Patroclus's tears (3-4) returns as the wolves' watering
    hole: the book's imagery runs on dark water.

[13] (16.168-198) The Catalogue of Myrmidons: fifty ships, fifty
    men each, five battalions — a miniature of Book 2, complete
    with erotic biographies. Menesthius, son of the river
    Spercheius by Peleus's daughter Polydora ("though in name" a
    mortal's son — the double-paternity formula); and Eudorus, the
    παρθένιος, born of Hermes' love for Polymele, seen dancing in
    Artemis's choir — seduction, secret bed, bride-price,
    grandfather's rearing: a complete romance in twelve lines.
    Phoenix (Book 9's tutor) commands the fourth battalion;
    Alcimedon (who will matter at 17.466ff) the fifth. The muster
    the wrath had parked since Book 2 stands up again.

[14] (16.200-210) Achilles quotes his own army's grumbling back
    at them — "Cruel son of Peleus — your mother reared you on
    bile... Let us at least sail home" — the only place we hear
    the Myrmidons' opinion of the wrath, and it matches
    Patroclus's ("pitiless," "cruel"). Achilles repeats it
    without rebuttal and points them at the work: the speech of a
    commander who knows exactly what has been said of him and
    considers the account now settled.

[15] (16.212-218) The wall-builder simile (close-set stones
    against the wind) introduces the phalanx lines repeated from
    13.131-133, our English kept: "buckler pressed on buckler,
    helmet on helmet, man on man." There the formation was
    defensive despair; here it is an offensive spring compressed —
    the same order, opposite potential energy.

[16] (16.220-233) The cup: from the chest Thetis packed — the
    poem's one glimpse of a mother provisioning her son for war
    (tunics, wind-proof cloaks, rugs) — a cup reserved for one
    man and one god: "no other man drank from it, and to no god
    did he pour from it, except to Zeus the father." Sulphur,
    clean water, washed hands: the private liturgy of a prince
    who deals with heaven bilaterally. No other hero in the poem
    has a personal cult protocol.

[17] (16.233-248) The Dodona prayer: "Zeus — lord — Dodonaean —
    Pelasgian — dweller far off" — the oldest oracle in Greece
    (Herodotus agrees), with its astonishing priesthood: "the
    Selloi, your interpreters, men of unwashed feet, who sleep on
    the ground" — holiness as permanent contact with the earth
    (ancient scholars debated Selloi/Helloi, whence "Hellenes").
    Achilles prays past every Olympian courtesy to something
    older and darker than Olympus, in the correct contract-form
    ("Once before, when I prayed, you heard my word") — the
    Book 1 plague answered him; now he asks the second favor:
    glory for Patroclus, and his safe return "with all his armor,
    and his close-fighting companions."

[18] (16.249-253) "And the father granted him one thing, and
    refused the other." The poem's most economical sentence of
    doom: prayer processed as paperwork, one clause approved, one
    denied, no notification issued. Achilles will stand at his
    hut believing both clauses filed.

[19] (16.259-267) The wasp simile — with the poet's editorial
    inside it: boys provoke the roadside nest "as boys will, the
    little fools: they make a common evil for many." The second
    child-image of the day's fighting (the sand-castle god,
    15.362); this one indicts the war itself — someone poked the
    nest, and the passer-by gets stung. The Myrmidons ("ant-men"
    by folk-etymology) fight under insect colors: wasps, then
    flies (641-644).

[20] (16.269-283) Patroclus's field-speech turns the private
    grievance public: fight "so that Atreus' son... may know his
    blindness (ἄτην), that he honored not at all the best of the
    Achaeans" — the register's ἄτη, deployed by the wrath's
    gentlest partisan. And the disguise works precisely once, in
    the aggregate: the Trojans do not identify the man; they
    identify the armor and infer the reconciliation — "each man
    looked about him for escape from sheer destruction." The
    armor is the argument.

[21] (16.287-305) Pyraechmes of the Paeonians — mustered at 2.848
    with the same river-formula ("out of Amydon, from the wide-
    flowing Axius") — is the first kill, and the tactical one:
    the fire is quenched, "and the ship was left there, half-
    burnt" (ἡμιδαής, a word occurring nowhere else — the poem
    coins a term for its own precise scorch-line). The clearing
    simile that follows — Zeus moving one cloud off a peak, "and
    the prodigious upper air breaks open from heaven" — measures
    the relief exactly: light, but "no pause in the war."

[22] (16.317-330) The sons of Nestor kill the sons of Amisodarus
    — "the man who reared the raging Chimaera": the monster of
    Bellerophon's tale (6.179-183) resurfaces as someone's
    livestock, one genealogical step from the battlefield. The
    paired-brothers kill (Antilochus and Thrasymedes against
    Atymnius and Maris) is the book's small mirror of its great
    theme: companionship as a fighting unit.

[23] (16.364-394) The rout at the ditch — Hector's horses carry
    him clear while the army snaps its chariot-poles behind him —
    rises into the great storm simile of divine justice: Zeus's
    worst autumn deluge falls "when in rage he turns bitter
    against men who by force render crooked judgments in the
    assembly and drive justice out, careless of the gods'
    vengeance." Nowhere else does the Iliad's Zeus mind human
    justice at all — this is the Zeus of Hesiod's Works and Days,
    surfacing for nine lines to make the routed army's panic a
    moral weather. That the flood "diminishes the works of men"
    while the crooked judges sit indoors is the simile's quiet
    sting.

[24] (16.399-419) The angler: Thestor, huddled witless in his
    chariot, is hooked through the jaw and hauled "gaping" over
    the rail "as a man sitting on a jutting rock hauls a sacred
    fish out of the sea with his line and his glittering bronze."
    The most horrifying transformation in the poem's killing-
    repertoire — a man landed as a catch — done with a
    fisherman's leisure. ("Sacred fish": ancient guesses ranged
    from a species to a fish grown temple-fat; the adjective's
    unplaceability is half its power.) Then the nine-name sweep,
    "in heaps, to the earth that feeds so many."

[25] (16.419-461) Sarpedon sees his "companions of the unbelted
    tunics" (the Lycians' native dress, without the Greek war-
    belt) going down, and the poem mounts its highest scene: Zeus
    asks Hera whether he may save his son. Her answer is the
    constitution of the poem's universe: "Do it. But we other
    gods will not all approve... many sons of the immortals are
    fighting around the great city of Priam" — fate as the
    equality of bereavement; if one father cheats, all may. The
    compromise she offers becomes the poem's tenderest machinery:
    Death and painless Sleep to carry him home, "with a tomb and
    a grave-stone: for that is the honor-right of the dead" (τὸ
    γὰρ γέρας ἐστὶ θανόντων — γέρας, the quarrel's own word for
    honor's portion, given its final meaning). Zeus consents and
    "rained down drops of blood upon the earth, honoring his dear
    son": the sky's grief, the blood-dew of 11.53 made personal.
    Zenodotus struck the entire dialogue out (432-458) — deleting
    the theology of the poem to spare Zeus the tears.

[26] (16.462-477) The duel's first exchange kills an aide and a
    horse: Sarpedon's miss finds Pedasus, and the mortal trace-
    horse "screamed away his life" — the poem's only equine
    casualty, and the death that previews everything: the mortal
    yoked beside immortals dies; the immortal pair tangle and
    rear; and it takes Automedon's sword — cutting the dead
    weight loose — to right the team. The allegory is left
    entirely to the reader.

[27] (16.477-507) Sarpedon falls with the shipwright simile
    repeated from 13.389-391 (our English kept) — Asius's death-
    tree now felling the man whose Book 12 speech justified every
    death in the poem — and then the bellowing bull under the
    lion. His dying speech is not of Lycia, wife, or son, but of
    the body and the shame-economy: "I will be your disgrace and
    your hanging head all your days... if the Achaeans strip my
    armor here." Patroclus's extraction of the spear is the
    grimmest anatomical clause in Homer: "he pulled out his life
    and the spear-point together" — the midriff following the
    bronze, soul and weapon in one motion.

[28] (16.508-533) Glaucus's prayer pays off the wound Teucer dealt
    him at 12.387-391 (concealed then; crippling now) and briefly
    indicts heaven itself: "the best of men has perished —
    Sarpedon, son of Zeus; and Zeus does not defend even his own
    child." Apollo — hearer "everywhere" of "a man in grief" —
    stills the pain and dries the blood: the god acting in his
    oldest capacity, healer, minutes before he acts in his
    darkest.

[29] (16.538-553) Glaucus's rebuke of Hector states the allied
    grievance (men dying "far from their friends and their
    fatherland" for a city not theirs) and the city's debt:
    Sarpedon "kept Lycia safe by his judgments and his strength"
    — δίκῃσι, the justice-word, in the obituary of the man the
    justice-storm (385-393) did not protect. The Trojan grief-
    line is the poem's tribute to the foreigner: "he was a stay
    of their city, foreigner though he was."

[30] (16.567-570) Zeus stretches "a deadly night over the strong
    encounter, so that around his dear son the deadly toil of
    battle might be waged" — darkness as paternal mourning-veil,
    the counterpart of the blood-rain: the god cannot save him,
    so he dims the lights over the body.

[31] (16.570-582) — see note [32].

[32] (16.570-607) The battle over the body opens with Epeigeus:
    once a king in Budeum, then a cousin-killer, then a suppliant
    of Peleus and Thetis, then Achilles' man, now dead across
    Sarpedon's corpse — the third homicide-exile biography in
    four books (Medon, Lycophron), and the closest to Patroclus's
    own (23.85-90). The Myrmidon court at Phthia absorbs killers
    and re-employs them; the poem keeps burying them in order.
    Bathycles (richest of the Myrmidons) and Laogonus (priest of
    Idaean Zeus) follow — wealth and priesthood, the two
    exemptions that never exempt.

[33] (16.617-633) The dancer taunt: Aeneas mocks Meriones' dodge
    as Cretan dancing (Crete's dancers were proverbial); Meriones
    answers with mortality-arithmetic; and Patroclus's rebuke
    closes the exchange with the poem's own poetics of action:
    "In hands lies the outcome of war; of words, in council. So
    there is no need to pile up speech, but to fight." The
    gentlest speaker in the epic states the genre's law — battle
    is not a debate — and the poem, which loves its taunts,
    lets him.

[34] (16.633-646) The signature of this battle: Sarpedon's body
    made anonymous — "not even a discerning man could have known
    brilliant Sarpedon, wrapped from his head clean down to the
    soles of his feet in missiles and blood and dust" — while
    around it the armies cluster "as flies in a farmstead buzz
    about the brimming milk-pails in the season of spring." Homer
    dares the smallest, homeliest life against the largest death:
    spring, milk, plenty — and men as flies. The corpse the flies
    swarm cannot be told from the field it lies on; Apollo's bath
    (note [36]) exists to reverse exactly this.

[35] (16.644-660) Zeus's gaze never leaves the fight, "brooding
    long over the killing of Patroclus: whether now... or whether
    he should swell the sheer toil for still more men." The
    decision — more — is mercy indistinguishable from sentence:
    the extra hour of glory is granted the way the prayer was
    half-granted (note [18]). Hector's panic is knowledge: "he
    knew the sacred scales of Zeus" — the balance of 8.69 and
    22.209 felt from inside, tipping.

[36] (16.666-683) The funeral of Sarpedon: Apollo lifts him from
    the missiles, washes him in the running river, anoints him
    with ambrosia, wraps him in immortal clothes, and gives him
    to "the twin brothers Sleep and Death" for the journey home
    to Lycia — Zeus's orders repeated in execution almost word
    for word, as the poem's rituals must be. Zenodotus athetized
    the whole passage; against him stand the gentlest lines any
    death receives in the Iliad — and the scene's afterlife: on
    the Euphronios krater (Athens, c. 515 BC), winged Sleep and
    Death lift the bleeding Sarpedon while Hermes watches — the
    single most famous painted image of the Iliad in ancient
    art. The promise of 14.231 ("Sleep, the brother of Death")
    completes itself: the pair Hera bribed with a Grace now
    carry her rival's son to burial. What the honor-right of the
    dead looks like, the poem shows once, here, done by gods.

[37] (16.684-711) The turn: "the fool (νήπιος): had he kept the
    word of Peleus' son, he would have escaped the evil doom of
    black death. But always the mind of Zeus is stronger than
    men's." The apostrophized roll-call follows ("whom first,
    whom last did you strip of his arms, Patroclus, when the gods
    were calling you deathward?" — the fixed register question
    turned, for the first time, on the poem's beloved), then the
    thrice-and-fourth at the wall's very angle — Apollo slapping
    the shield back with immortal hands — and the warning that
    grades all mortals in one line: not your portion, "no, nor
    Achilles', who is a far better man than you." Patroclus backs
    off "dodging the wrath of Apollo" — μῆνιν, the poem's title-
    word, now the god's.

[38] (16.712-726) Apollo as Asius — Hecuba's brother, the third
    bearer of the name (the doomed captain of Book 12-13; the
    disguise of Book 10's era) — turns Hector around with a
    taunt at his skulking. The god who has just saved the wall
    now aims its defender at Patroclus: the same power working
    both sides of the same death.

[39] (16.726-752) Cebriones: the stone crushes both brows, "and
    his eyes dropped to the ground in the dust there at his
    feet; and like a diver he plunged" — the diver of 12.385
    (our phrase kept), now with the eyes watching from the
    ground. Patroclus's mock-eulogy — the oyster-diver who
    "could feed a whole crew... even in rough weather... there
    are acrobats among the Trojans too" — is the war's blackest
    comedy, and its placement is the point: the poem's kindest
    man, one god-blow from death, is granted the gallows-wit of
    the doomed. He has become, for thirty lines, Achilles.

[40] (16.756-778) Over the body: Hector by the head, Patroclus
    by the foot — the tug-of-war of the whole poem in one grip —
    while East and South Wind thrash the mountain timber
    overhead. And beneath it, the line that outlives the
    battle: Cebriones "in the whirl of dust lay mighty in his
    mightiness, his horsemanship all forgotten" (κεῖτο μέγας
    μεγαλωστί, λελασμένος ἱπποσυνάων). The Odyssey will use the
    verse of Achilles himself (24.39-40): the poem's ultimate
    epitaph, first spent on a charioteer.

[41] (16.777-790) The clock: sun at mid-heaven, stalemate; sun
    at "the unyoking of oxen," and the Achaeans prevail "beyond
    their portion" (ὑπὲρ αἶσαν — the day's ration of fate
    overdrawn). Thrice nine men fall; the fourth rush comes
    "like more than man" (δαίμονι ἶσος — the register phrase,
    twice in this book); "then, Patroclus, the end of your life
    showed clear." The formula-system itself has been counting:
    three-and-the-fourth is the poem's rhythm of trespass
    (5.436-439, 16.702-705), and the fourth time always finds
    the god.

[42] (16.790-807) The un-arming. Apollo, wrapped in mist, strikes
    the flat-handed blow — the only time a god personally strikes
    a mortal dead-on in the poem — and then dismantles the arming
    scene of 131-139 in reverse: helmet rolling under the hooves
    ("Before this it was not lawful for that horsehair-crested
    helmet to be fouled with dust: it guarded the head and the
    gracious brow of a godlike man, Achilles. But then Zeus gave
    it to Hector to wear on his own head; and his destruction
    was near him" — the armor-transfer chain announced with its
    invoice attached); the spear shattered — the one he could
    carry; the shield down; "and the lord Apollo, son of Zeus,
    loosed the corslet off him." A god undresses the man for
    death. ἄτη — blindness — takes his wits; he stands dazed,
    naked in the crowd: the poem's most terrifying image of what
    it is to lose divine favor — not to be struck, but to be
    unwrapped.

[43] (16.806-817) Euphorbus: the beautiful new fighter — best of
    his age with spear, horse, and foot, twenty men down in his
    first campaign, "still learning war" — spears the dazed man
    from behind and runs, "and did not stand against Patroclus,
    even unarmed as he was." He will die for it at 17.9-60,
    where his blood wets hair "like the Graces'." Fame followed
    him strangely: Pythagoras claimed Euphorbus's soul as a
    former life of his own, and proved it (the story goes) by
    recognizing the shield Menelaus took, hung in Hera's temple
    — the poem's minor duelist became antiquity's test-case for
    transmigration.

[44] (16.818-844) Hector, third, into the belly. His vaunt
    invents a quotation — Achilles supposedly ordering Patroclus
    not to return "before you have torn the bloody tunic around
    the chest of man-slaughtering Hector... So he must have
    talked to you" — the exact inversion of the truth we heard
    (87-96: come back; avoid the wall; fear Apollo). Hector's
    tragedy is compressed into his reconstruction of an order
    that was its opposite: he cannot imagine the restraint that
    was Achilles' actual love, and so misreads the man he must
    soon face.

[45] (16.843-869) The dying prophecy: "You are only the third at
