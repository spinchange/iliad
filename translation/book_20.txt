BOOK 20

Zeus opens the gates and the gods go down to war — then sit and
watch, while Achilles begins the killing that will not stop until
Hector: Aeneas saved for empire, Hector saved for Book 22, and
twelve men in a row not saved at all.

TRANSLATOR'S COMMENTARY

Book 20 begins the single continuous battle — one afternoon long,
four books wide — that runs from here to Hector's death. It opens
with the poem's fullest divine mobilization: Themis summons every
god to assembly, rivers and nymphs included (only Ocean stays
home), and Zeus, whose ban of Book 8 kept the gods in the stands,
now reverses it: go down, help whichever side you love. His reasons
are the poem's theology in two lines: "they matter to me, even as
they perish" — and the fear that Achilles, unchecked, "may storm
the wall itself, ahead of what is fated" (note [2]). Fate, it
turns out, needs defending; that is what gods are for this
afternoon. The mobilization produces the Iliad's greatest seismic
picture — Zeus thundering above, Poseidon shaking the mountain
roots below, and Hades leaping from his throne with a scream, lest
the crust crack open and his "terrible, mouldering houses, which
the very gods abhor" stand naked to the daylight (note [5]) — and
then, superbly, anticlimax: the gods pair off, look at one
another, and sit down. The Theomachy is postponed to Book 21; what
we get instead is men fighting under divine spectatorship.

The core of the book is the duel that cannot happen: Achilles
against Aeneas — pushed into the ring by Apollo wearing the voice
of Lycaon (whose own meeting with Achilles, in Book 21, will be
very different). Aeneas answers Achilles' magnificent taunting
with the longest genealogy in the poem: Dardanus to Erichthonius —
whose three thousand mares bore to the North Wind foals that ran
over wheat-ears without breaking them — to Tros, to Ganymede
taken up for his beauty, down the twin branches to Hector and to
himself (note [15]). It is a speech about why he cannot die here,
and the poem agrees: when the boulder and the sword are about to
decide it, Poseidon — a Greek partisan — saves Troy's enemy prince,
because "it is destined for him to escape, so that the race of
Dardanus not perish seedless": "the might of Aeneas shall be king
over the Trojans — and his children's children" (note [19]). The
apparatus records the variant Strabo knew, "his race shall rule
over all" — the reading Rome preferred; Virgil's epic grows out of
this rescue. Hector too is saved — once by Apollo's word, once by
Apollo's mist, after Athena turns his spear back with a breath —
and Achilles' frustrated cry over him repeats, word for word, what
Diomedes shouted at Hector in Book 11: "Dog! Again you have run
out from under death" (note [29]).

Everyone the gods do not save, dies. The book's last movement is
the first panel of Achilles' aristeia, and this translation keeps
its full brutality: Iphition's split skull and the chariots that
shred him; Polydorus — Priam's youngest, forbidden the war,
showing off his running — holding his own bowels; Tros of the
refused supplication, "not a sweet-tempered man, not mild of mind"
(the litotes that measures what Achilles has become; the theme
will climax with Lycaon in 21 and Hector in 22); Deucalion
beheaded with the marrow spurting from his spine. The closing
similes — forest fire, and oxen threshing barley — end the book
mid-carnage, blood on the axle, "spattering his invincible hands
with gore": no cadence, because the aristeia has only begun.

 385  under snowy Tmolus, in the rich district of Hyde.
 386  As he came straight on, brilliant Achilles caught him with the spear
 387  full in the middle of the head; and the whole skull split in two,
 388  and he fell with a thud; and brilliant Achilles cried over him:
 389  "Lie there, son of Otrynteus — most terrifying of all men!
 390  Here is your death. Your birth was by the Gygaean lake,
 391  where your father's estate lies, by fish-filled
 392  Hyllus and eddying Hermus."
 393  So he cried in triumph; and darkness covered his eyes,
 394  and the wheel-rims of Achaean chariots tore him apart
 395  in the front of the struggle. And after him, Demoleon —[27]
 396  a stout warder-off of battle, Antenor's son —
 397  he stabbed in the temple, through the bronze-cheeked helmet.
 398  The bronze helmet did not hold it: straight through
 399  drove the point, hungry, and broke the bone; and the brain
 400  was all spattered inside it. He broke him in mid-charge.
 401  Then Hippodamas: as he sprang down from his horses
 402  and fled before him, he stabbed him in the back with the spear;
 403  and he gasped out his life, bellowing — as a bull
 404  bellows when he is dragged for the Heliconian lord,
 405  young men dragging him, and the earth-shaker delights in them:
 406  so, as he bellowed, the proud life left his bones.
 407  Then with his spear he went for godlike Polydorus,[28]
 408  Priam's son. His father had forbidden him to fight,
 409  because among his children he was youngest-born
 410  and dearest; and he beat them all at running.
 411  And just then, in his boy's folly, showing off the speed of his feet,
 412  he was darting through the front-fighters — till he lost his dear life.
 413  As he flashed past, swift-footed brilliant Achilles hit him: the javelin
 414  square in the back, where the golden clasps of his war-belt
 415  fastened, and the corslet doubled over;
 416  and the spear-point held straight on through, out beside the navel,
 417  and he dropped to his knees with a scream, and a cloud of darkness
 418  wrapped him round, and he clutched his bowels to him with his hands as he sank.
 419  And when Hector saw his brother Polydorus[29]
 420  clutching his bowels in his hands, sinking to the earth,
 421  a mist poured down over his eyes. He could no longer bear
 422  to range apart; he came out against Achilles,
 423  shaking his sharp spear, like a flame. And Achilles —
 424  the instant he saw him, he sprang, and spoke his word of triumph:
 425  "Near me — the man who beyond all others has stung my heart,
 426  who killed my honored companion! No longer now
 427  shall we cower from each other down the lanes of war."
 428  He spoke, and glaring darkly he said to brilliant Hector:
 429  "Come closer — and come the sooner to the borders of destruction."
 430  And unshaken, Hector of the flashing helmet answered him:
 431  "Son of Peleus — do not hope to frighten me with words,
 432  as if I were a silly child, since I too know well enough
 433  how to sling insults, and how to speak outrage.
 434  I know that you are brave, and that I am far weaker than you.
 435  But these things lie on the knees of the gods:
 436  whether I, the weaker man, may still take the life out of you
 437  with a cast of my spear — since my weapon too has been sharp before now."
 438  He spoke, and poised and hurled his spear; and Athena
 439  turned it back from glorious Achilles with a breath —
 440  the gentlest breathing; and it came back to brilliant Hector
 441  and fell there in front of his feet. And Achilles
 442  leapt on him, furious, wild to kill him,
 443  shouting his terrible shout. But Apollo snatched Hector away —
 444  easily, as a god does — and hid him in thick mist.
 445  Three times swift-footed brilliant Achilles charged
 446  with his bronze spear, and three times he struck the deep mist.
 447  But when for the fourth time he came on like more than man,
 448  with a terrible shout he spoke winged words at him:
 449  "Dog! Again you have run out from under death — and yet the evil
 450  came close. Now once more Phoebus Apollo has pulled you out —
 451  to him you must pray whenever you go into the thud of javelins.
 452  I will finish you yet, meeting you later,
 453  if any god anywhere stands helper to me too.
 454  For now I will go after the others, whoever I can catch."
 455  So speaking, he stabbed Dryops full in the neck with the javelin,[30]
 456  and he crashed down in front of his feet. He left him there,
 457  and Demuchus, Philetor's son, a good man and a big one,
 458  he stopped with a spear-cast to the knee; and then
 459  he took his life away with a stroke of the great sword.
 460  Then Laogonus and Dardanus, the two sons of Bias:
 461  rushing both, he tumbled them from their horses to the ground,
 462  one with a spear-throw, one with the sword at close quarters.
 463  Then Tros, Alastor's son — who came at him to clasp his knees,
 464  hoping he might spare him, take him and let him go alive,
 465  might pity a man of his own age and not kill him —
 466  the fool: he did not know there would be no persuading.
 467  This was not a sweet-tempered man, not mild of mind,
 468  but all raw fury. Tros reached with his hands for his knees,
 469  desperate to plead; and he stabbed him in the liver with the sword,
 470  and the liver slid out, and the black blood pouring from it
 471  filled the fold of his tunic; and darkness covered his eyes,
 472  and the life failed. And next, standing over Mulius, he drove
 473  the spear into his ear, and out through the other ear
 474  the bronze point passed. Then Agenor's son Echeclus:
 475  he struck him full mid-head with the hilted sword,
 476  and the whole blade ran hot with blood, and down over his eyes
 477  came purple death, and fate the overpowering.
 478  Then Deucalion: just where the tendons of the elbow
 479  join, there he pierced him through the arm
 480  with the bronze point; and he stood and waited, his arm hanging heavy,
 481  looking at death before him; and with the sword Achilles struck his neck
 482  and sent head and helmet flying far together; and the marrow
 483  spurted up out of the spine, and the trunk lay stretched on the earth.
 484  Then he went after the blameless son of Peiros —[31]
 485  Rhigmus, who had come from deep-soiled Thrace:
 486  him he hit square with the javelin, and the bronze stuck in his belly,
 487  and he crashed from his chariot. And Areithous his aide,
 488  as he wheeled the horses around, he stabbed in the back
 489  with the sharp spear, and thrust him from the car; and the horses ran wild.
 490  As unearthly fire rages through the deep glens
 491  of a parched mountain, and the deep forest burns,
 492  and the wind, driving it everywhere, whirls the flame around:
 493  so everywhere he stormed with his spear, like more than man,
 494  harrying the men he killed; and the black earth ran with blood.
 495  And as when a man yokes broad-browed bull oxen
 496  to tread white barley on a well-built threshing-floor,
 497  and quickly the grain is husked under the feet of the bellowing oxen:
 498  so under great-hearted Achilles the single-hoofed horses
 499  trampled corpses and shields together; and with blood the axle
 500  beneath was all spattered, and the rails that ran round the car,
 501  struck by the drops thrown up from the horses' hooves
 502  and from the wheel-rims. And on he pressed to win glory —
 503  the son of Peleus — spattering his invincible hands with gore.

NOTES

[1] (20.1-12) The opening apostrophe — "around you, son of
    Peleus" — puts the poet's own voice in the second line: the
    book that begins Achilles' aristeia begins by addressing
    him. Themis, Right herself, is the summoner (her one act in
    the poem), and the invitation-list is total: every river
    but Ocean (who anchors the world and cannot leave it),
    every nymph of grove and spring and meadow. The whole
    animate landscape is called in to watch what Achilles does
    to the plain it lives in.

[2] (20.13-30) Zeus's convocation speech is the poem's theology
    at its most compressed. Asked why he has called them, he
    answers: "they matter to me, even as they perish" (μέλουσί
    μοι ὀλλύμενοί περ) — care and destruction in one clause,
    the whole divine economy of the Iliad. He will watch from a
    fold of Olympus and "delight my mind" — the god as
    spectator at the theater he financed — but the license he
    gives the gods is practical: without divine interference on
    both sides, Achilles "may storm the wall itself, ahead of
    what is fated" (ὑπέρμορον). Fate in Homer is not
    self-enforcing: it can be jumped, and the gods are deployed
    here as its police. The Book 8 ban is formally dissolved;
    the last movement of the poem runs with all gods on the
    field.

[3] (20.31-40) The order of battle: five gods to the ships
    (Hera, Athena, Poseidon, Hermes, Hephaestus), five to the
    Trojans (Ares, Apollo, Artemis, Leto, Xanthus the river,
    with Aphrodite). Ares arrives as "Ares of the flashing
    helmet" — Hector's fixed epithet, lent to the war-god this
    once, as if the poem were already transferring Hector's
    identity to the god he follows. Xanthus fights for the city
    through whose plain he flows — the enlistment Book 21 will
    take terribly seriously.

[4] (20.41-53) Before the gods engage: pure Achilles-effect —
    Achaeans exultant, "terrible trembling" in every Trojan
    limb. When they do engage, the poem gives us war-noise as
    divine antiphony: Athena shouting from the ditch and the
    headlands, Ares "like a black stormwind" screaming from the
    citadel and Callicolone — the battlefield turned into a
    theater with two crying gods for a chorus.

[5] (20.54-66) The cosmic quake. Zeus thunders above, Poseidon
    shakes "the boundless earth and the steep heads of the
    mountains," Ida trembles root to peak — and below, the
    poem's single glimpse into the underworld from the inside:
    Aïdoneus leaping from his throne with a scream, terrified
    the crust will split "and his houses stand exposed to
    mortals and immortals — terrible, mouldering houses, which
    the very gods abhor." Death's kingdom described only by
    the horror of its possible exposure: the gods themselves
    cannot bear to look at what they consign men to. Longinus
    quoted these lines as the summit of the sublime.

[6] (20.67-74) The pairings — Poseidon/Apollo, Athena/Enyalius,
    Hera/Artemis, Hermes/Leto, Hephaestus/Xanthus — a formal
    duel-card that Book 21 will actually play out. The river
    is introduced with the poem's fullest instance of divine
    double-naming: "whom the gods call Xanthus, and men
    Scamander" — the world has two vocabularies, and the poem
    knows both.

[7] (20.75-85) Apollo picks Aeneas as the delaying weapon and
    wears the voice of Lycaon, Priam's son — a savage irony
    held in reserve: the real Lycaon will meet Achilles in
    Book 21 and become the poem's great emblem of unsparable
    life. The taunt is perfectly aimed: dinner-table threats
    "over your wine" against the sober fact of Achilles.

[8] (20.86-102) Aeneas's answer is a veteran's risk-assessment:
    he has run from Achilles before, off Ida, in the raid that
    sacked Lyrnessus and Pedasus (the raid that took Briseis —
    the poem's pre-history everywhere this afternoon), and he
    names the real problem: "always one of the gods is beside
    him, beating ruin away." His conditional — with a level
    field, "he would not beat me so easily" — is exactly the
    proposition the duel will test and the gods will refuse to
    let conclude.

[9] (20.103-111) Apollo's counter is genealogical arithmetic:
    Aphrodite outranks Thetis ("yours is a child of Zeus; his,
    of the old man of the sea") — therefore fight. The
    syllogism is divine snobbery, and false as tactics: rank
    of mother decides nothing on the plain, as the shield of
    Hephaestus is about to demonstrate. But it works — on
    "the shepherd of the people," the poem's title for the
    responsible man goaded into an irresponsible duel.

[10] (20.112-131) Hera's alarm council. Her speech swings
    characteristically from tactics (turn Aeneas back) to
    prestige (let Achilles "know that they love him — the best
    of the immortals — and that windy nothings" defend Troy);
    lines 125-128, with their weaving Fate ("whatever Fate
    spun for him with her thread at his birth"), were
    athetized by Aristarchus. Her closing maxim earns its
    place in the book of divine epiphanies: "Gods seen in
    their true shapes are hard to bear" (χαλεποὶ δὲ θεοὶ
    φαίνεσθαι ἐναργεῖς).

[11] (20.132-143) Poseidon's restraint doctrine — "we are far
    the stronger" is his reason not to fight: overwhelming
    force can afford to sit down. "Leave the war to men" is
    the book's quiet thesis: the gods' battle is theater; the
    men's is real. His trigger-conditions (if Ares or Apollo
    start, or if they hold Achilles back) keep the Theomachy
    loaded for Book 21.

[12] (20.144-155) The gods take their seats — on the wall of
    Heracles, the rampart Trojans and Athena built a
    generation ago so Heracles could escape the sea-monster
    sent against Laomedon's Troy (the Hesione story, surfacing
    only here). The Greek gods watch this Trojan war from the
    fortification of the last one: Troy's past salvation
    reused as the grandstand for its destruction. Both
    parties sit "deliberating" — and Zeus, alone, "seated on
    high, commanded" the war on: the only spectator who wants
    the show to start.

[13] (20.156-175) The champions meet, and Achilles gets the
    book's great lion simile — not a hunting lion but a hunted
    one: "a raider, whom men are wild to kill — a whole
    district, gathered," heedless until wounded, then foaming,
    groaning, lashing his own ribs with his tail, "whipping
    himself on to fight," driving in "to see whether he will
    kill some man, or die himself in the front of the crowd."
    The simile makes self-destruction part of the charge:
    kill or die are equal outcomes inside it — which is,
    since Book 18, precisely Achilles' own arithmetic.

[14] (20.178-198) Achilles' taunt runs through every motive a
    rational Aeneas could have — the succession ("Priam would
    not lay that prize in your hand: he has sons; and he is
    steady"), the land-grant (the temenos of orchard and
    plowland) — Aristarchus athetized 180-186, but the
    political satire is sharp and early: the poem knows about
    princes who fight for estates. Then the memory: the
    cattle-raid chase down Ida, Lyrnessus wrecked, "the women
    captive, their day of freedom gone." It ends with the
    registered proverb, reused verbatim from Menelaus at
    17.30-32: "When the thing is done, even the fool learns."

[15] (20.199-258) Aeneas's genealogy — the longest pedigree in
    the poem, and the poem's most self-aware speech about
    speech. The frame (200-202: "do not hope to frighten me
    with words, as if I were a silly child") will be repeated
    verbatim by Hector at 431-433 — the Trojan princes share
    a script against Achilles. Then the lineage: Dardanus,
    son of Zeus; Erichthonius, richest of men, whose three
    thousand mares bore to Boreas twelve foals that ran over
    wheat-ears without breaking them and along the crests of
    the breakers — the poem's most weightless image, horses
    of wind bred from the hill-pastures of Troy; Tros;
    Ganymede, "most beautiful of mortal men," taken up to
    pour Zeus's wine; then the split into the two houses —
    Ilus to Laomedon to Priam and Hector; Assaracus to Capys
    to Anchises to Aeneas. Two first-cousin lines, and the
    survival of one is being decided this hour. The coda is
    the poem's finest statement on language: "The tongue of
    mortals is a twisty thing... whatever sort of word you
    speak, that is the sort you will hear" — and the simile
    of the wrangling women in the street ("and much is true,
    and much is not: the anger orders that too"), athetized
    by Aristarchus (251-255), too humanly observed to lose.
    Then, superbly: "let us taste one another with our
    bronze-fitted spears."

[16] (20.259-272) The shield test. Aeneas's spear beats two
    folds and stops in the gold — and the narrator scolds
    Achilles for flinching ("the fool: he did not consider...
    that the glorious gifts of the gods do not lie down easily
    under mortal men"): even its owner does not yet trust
    divine craft. The five layers (two bronze, two tin, one
    gold) troubled the ancients — if the gold is decorative
    surface, how does it stop a spear at the third layer? —
    and Aristarchus athetized 269-272; some ancient texts
    omitted them entirely. Kept here: the physics of a
    miraculous object need not audit.

[17] (20.273-283) Achilles' cast: through both circles of the
    rim where bronze and hide run thinnest — the Pelian ash
    "shrieked" through and stood planted in the earth "still
    hungry" (ἱεμένη — straining onward). Aeneas crouched
    under his own shield with the spear quivering over his
    back: "grief past measure flooded down over his eyes, in
    terror at how close the point was planted." Zenodotus's
    text had a different pair of lines here; the vulgate's
    sequence — cast, crouch, the standing spear — is the most
    cinematic near-miss in the poem.

[18] (20.285-291) The stone "which no two men could carry,
    such as mortals are now" (the register's fixed diminished-
    present formula, its fourth occurrence) — and the double
    counterfactual: rock against sword, both blows about to
    land, when Poseidon "saw it sharply." The duel is frozen
    at its decisive instant and never resumed: the poem
    refuses to know who was better.

[19] (20.292-308) Poseidon's rescue speech — a Greek god
    saving Troy's prince — is the poem's window onto a future
    beyond itself: Aeneas is "guiltless," pious, and destined:
    "It is destined for him to escape, so that the race of
    Dardanus not perish seedless... and now the might of
    Aeneas shall be king over the Trojans — and his children's
    children." The Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite makes the same
    promise; Strabo knew a variant — "his race shall rule
    over all" (γένος πάντεσσιν ἀνάξει) — which Rome, tracing
    the Julii through Iulus to Aeneas, had every reason to
    prefer; the apparatus records it. Whatever its politics,
    the moment is structurally superb: in the middle of the
    Iliad's terminal battle, the poem certifies that
    something Trojan survives everything.

[20] (20.309-317) Hera's consent is glacial: do as you like
    about Aeneas — "we two have sworn oaths, many of them...
    never to ward the evil day away from the Trojans, not
    even when all Troy is burning in ravening fire." The
    burning-city clause, spoken as calmly as a contract term,
    is the coldest sentence any god utters in the poem.

[21] (20.318-339) The rescue itself: mist over Achilles'
    eyes, the spear laid back at his feet (a courtesy with an
    edge: nothing of yours is lost — except the kill), and
    Aeneas thrown over "many ranks of heroes, many ranks of
    horses" to the Caucones at the battle's rim. Poseidon's
    parting instructions are precise prophecy: avoid Achilles
    while he lives; after his death, "fight among the
    front-fighters: no other of the Achaeans will ever strip
    your armor." Aeneas exits the poem's war carrying its
    only personal guarantee of survival.

[22] (20.340-352) Achilles' soliloquy over the vanished
    opponent gets his tone exactly: wonder for one breath
    ("a great marvel is this I see with my eyes"), then the shrug
    — "Let him go, and good riddance (ἐρρέτω). He will find
    no heart to test me again" — and back to work. The
    aristeia's engine does not idle.

[23] (20.353-363) The order of the day: "man against man."
    His self-estimate is the rarest thing in his mouth —
    a limit: not even Ares or Athena "could work the mouth of
    a battle-line so wide" (the battle imagined as a thing
    with jaws; cf. 19.313 "the mouth of bloody war"). What
    follows is the promise kept literally: "straight through
    the line I will go."

[24] (20.364-372) Hector's rally answers it with the poem's
    most honest command ever given to troops: "I too could
    fight the very immortals — with words; with the spear it
    is harder, since they are far stronger." Then the great
    epanalepsis, the line that turns on its own hinge:
    "I will go against him, though his hands are like fire —
    though his hands are like fire, and his fury like burning
    iron." The repetition is courage audibly talking itself
    past its own information.

[25] (20.373-380) Apollo's counter-order — wait in the mass,
    "or he will hit you with a cast — or close, and cut you
    down with the sword" — names exactly the two deaths
    Hector risks; and Hector, who told the Trojans not to
    fear Achilles, obeys the god and sinks back "shaken." The
    gap between his speech and his obedience is the whole
    tragedy of Hector, one book early.

[26] (20.381-395) The aristeia's first kill: Iphition, a
    nymph's son from under snowy Tmolus, his skull split
    clean in two — and Achilles' epitaph-taunt returns the
    man to his geography: "Here is your death. Your birth was
    by the Gygaean lake" — death here, birth there, the whole
    biography reduced to two map-points (the register's
    "most terrifying of all men" — Iris's address to Achilles
    at 18.170 — flung at a corpse). Then the poem lets the
    chariots shred him: no burial-notice, only wheel-rims.

[27] (20.395-406) Demoleon (temple, through the bronze-cheeked
    helmet, "the brain was all spattered inside it") and
    Hippodamas, whose soul leaves him "bellowing — as a bull
    bellows when he is dragged for the Heliconian lord": the
    sacrifice of bulls to Poseidon Heliconius at the
    Panionian festival, where a bellowing victim was a good
    omen — "and the earth-shaker delights in them." A man's
    death-cry heard by the poem as a pleasing sacrifice: the
    grimmest cult-note in the Iliad, and Poseidon is standing
    right there on the field.

[28] (20.407-418) Polydorus — Priam's youngest, forbidden the
    war "because among his children he was youngest-born and
    dearest," fastest of them all, killed "in his boy's
    folly, showing off the speed of his feet" as he darted
    through the front-fighters: struck where the war-belt's
    golden clasps meet, the point out beside the navel,
    kneeling with a scream, "and he clutched his bowels to
    him with his hands as he sank." The poem will reveal in
    Books 21-22 that he and Lycaon were Laothoe's sons —
    Priam mourns the pair at 22.46-48 — making Polydorus the
    first payment of a double debt this aristeia collects
    from one mother.

[29] (20.419-454) The premature Hector-duel. The sight of his
    brother's bowels brings Hector out against orders — the
    mist that "poured down over his eyes" is grief doing to
    him what gods do to the doomed — and the exchange is a
    dress rehearsal of Book 22: taunt ("Come closer — so you
    come the sooner to the borders of destruction"), Hector's
    reply reusing Aeneas's frame verbatim (431-433 = 200-202)
    but adding what Aeneas never said: "I know that you are
    brave, and that I am far weaker than you. But these
    things lie on the knees of the gods." Then Athena turns
    his spear back "with a breath — the gentlest breathing"
    (the war decided by the smallest quantity of divine
    effort in the poem), Apollo snatches him into mist, and
    Achilles' three charges and fourth "like more than man"
    (δαίμονι ἶσος) end in the shout that repeats 11.362-367
    word for word — Diomedes' very speech to the same
    rescued man: "Dog! Again you have run out from under
    death." Two heroes, nine years apart in the poem's
    afternoon, are given identical words against Hector's
    charmed life — a repetition that makes Apollo's
    protection feel like policy, and its expiry date (Book
    22) feel like doom.

[30] (20.455-483) The spree, each kill a different door out
    of life: Dryops (neck), Demuchus (knee, then sword),
    the sons of Bias tumbled from their chariot, Mulius
    (spear in one ear, point out the other), Echeclus (the
    whole sword-blade "grew hot with the blood" — the
    register's "purple death" takes his eyes), Deucalion
    (arm pinned through the elbow-tendons; he "stood and
    waited, his arm hanging heavy, looking at death before
    him" — the poem's stillest instant of doomed
    consciousness — then head and helmet flying, "and the
    marrow spurted up out of the spine"). At the center,
    Tros son of Alastor, who tries the war's other exit:
    supplication, knees clasped, mercy of an age-mate —
    "the fool: he did not know there would be no persuading.
    This was not a sweet-tempered man, not mild of mind."
    The litotes is the aristeia's moral thermometer: the man
    who once spared and ransomed captives (as Lycaon will
    remind him, 21.74-96) is not there to be reached. The
    liver slides out; the tunic fills with blood; the theme
    is set for Lycaon.

[31] (20.484-503) Rhigmus of Thrace and his driver Areithous
    close the count (a dozen named kills), and the book ends
    in two similes that refuse pathos: the fire-storm in the
    parched glens ("the wind, driving it everywhere, whirls
    the flame around"), and — harder — the threshing-floor:
    oxen treading barley, "and quickly the grain is husked
    under the feet of the bellowing oxen: so under
    great-hearted Achilles the single-hoofed horses trampled
    corpses and shields together." Slaughter as agriculture,
    the dead as processed grain; the axle and chariot-rails
    "spattered" with the spray from hooves and wheel-rims.
    The last line hangs the whole book on its hero's hands:
    "spattering his invincible hands with gore" — the hands
    Thetis called man-slaughtering (18.317), still reaching
    for glory, not yet for Hector. There is no closural
    cadence: Book 21 continues the same hour at the river.
