BOOK 11

The long day begins: Agamemnon's morning of glory, the captains carried
off wounded one by one, and Nestor plants the plan that will kill
Patroclus.

TRANSLATOR'S COMMENTARY

Book 11 opens the longest day in poetry: the sun that rises from
Tithonus's bed in line 1 will not set until 18.239-242, seven and a
half books from now, and Zeus dictates the day's whole shape in
advance in his message to Hector (185-194): let Agamemnon rage; when
he is wounded and mounts his chariot, Hector's hour begins, and runs
until he reaches the ships and the sun goes down. What follows is a
day of subtraction. Agamemnon's aristeia — the poem's first sight of
the commander actually pre-eminent in battle — burns hot and short,
and then the captains are removed one by one: Agamemnon by Coon's
spear, Diomedes and Eurypylus and Machaon by Paris's arrows,
Odysseus by Socus's thrust; by the book's end Ajax alone is left in
the field, backing away like a beast, like a donkey — and the
despised bow (note [29]) has done most of the day's work.

The book's second half changes register entirely, and is the hinge of
the whole epic. Achilles, watching the rout from his stern for
pleasure, sees Nestor carry a wounded man past and sends Patroclus to
ask the name — and the poet stamps the errand at once: "he came out,
the equal of Ares — and that was the beginning of his evil" (604).
In Nestor's hut the plan is invented that Patroclus will repeat at
the opening of Book 16: let Achilles send you out in his own armor
(794-803, largely athetized in antiquity — see notes [42]-[43]).
Nestor's enormous Pylian reminiscence (656-762) earns its length as
paradigm — the young Nestor fought although his father forbade him
and hid his horses; Achilles, able, withholds — and closes on the
saddest line the old man speaks: "Such was I among men — if ever I
was." Patroclus, running back, is detained by the wounded Eurypylus
and stays to cut out the arrow: the book ends with the gentlest man
in the poem healing one wound while his own death is being prepared,
and its last clause repeats, word for word, the drying of Agamemnon's
wound at 267 — the day's first casualty and its last, rhymed.

Our printed Greek genuinely lacks line 543 — the line Aristotle read,
"for Zeus would be indignant with him, whenever he fought a better
man" — and the translation reproduces the gap (numbering jumps 542 to
544), with the line translated in note [35]. Register notes: the
Muse-invocation reuses 2.484; 13-14 reuse 2.453-454 (athetized here
as a wrongful repeat); 5-9 reuse the camp geometry of 8.222-226;
Peleus's "always to be the best" repeats 6.208, our wording kept; and
the cup of Nestor (632-637) meets the oldest joke in Greek epigraphy
(note [38]).

   1  Dawn rose from her bed beside illustrious Tithonus[1]
   2  to bring light to the immortals and to mortal men;
   3  and Zeus sent Strife down to the swift ships of the Achaeans,
   4  the grim goddess, holding a portent of war in her hands.
   5  She stood on Odysseus's black ship, huge of hull,
   6  which lay midmost, so a shout could carry to both ends,
   7  both to the huts of Ajax, Telamon's son,
   8  and to Achilles' — for they had drawn their balanced ships up
   9  at the ends, trusting their courage and the strength of their hands.
  10  Standing there the goddess screamed, great and terrible,
  11  a high shriek; and into each Achaean's heart she threw
  12  great strength, to war and fight without ceasing.
  13  And at once war became sweeter to them than going home[2]
  14  in the hollow ships to their dear fatherland.
  15  And Atreus' son shouted aloud, commanding the Argives
  16  to gird themselves; and he himself put on the flashing bronze.
  17  First he set the greaves along his shins,
  18  fine greaves, fitted with silver ankle-pieces;
  19  second, he put the corslet about his chest,
  20  the one that Cinyras once gave him as a guest-gift:[3]
  21  for the great report had reached him in Cyprus — the Achaeans
  22  were about to sail up in their ships for Troy —
  23  and so he gave it to him, a favor for the king.
  24  On it there were ten bands of dark blue enamel,[4]
  25  and twelve of gold, and twenty of tin;
  26  and serpents of blue enamel reached up toward the neck,
  27  three on either side, like the rainbows the son of Cronos
  28  sets in a cloud, a portent for mortal men.
  29  Around his shoulders he threw his sword: on it the studs
  30  glittered gold, and the scabbard about it
  31  was silver, fitted with golden straps.
  32  And he took up his man-covering, rich-worked, rushing shield,
  33  a fine shield: around it ran ten circles of bronze,
  34  and on it were twenty bosses of tin,
  35  white, and in the center one of dark blue enamel;
  36  and crowning it was the Gorgon, grim of face,
  37  glaring terribly, and around her Terror and Panic.[5]
  38  The shield-strap was of silver, and along it
  39  writhed a serpent of blue enamel, with three heads
  40  twisting every way, grown from a single neck.
  41  On his head he set his helmet, double-ridged, four-bossed,
  42  horsehair-crested; the plume nodded terribly above him.
  43  And he took two stout spears, headed with bronze,
  44  sharp ones; and far off into heaven the bronze
  45  flashed from them; and Athena and Hera thundered over him,
  46  doing honor to the king of Mycenae rich in gold.[6]
  47  Then each man gave his charioteer the order
  48  to hold the horses in good order there at the ditch,
  49  while they themselves, on foot, armed in their gear,
  50  swept forward; and quenchless rose the shouting before dawn.
  51  They formed at the ditch well ahead of the charioteers,
  52  who followed a little behind. And an evil tumult
  53  the son of Cronos stirred among them, and down from on high he sent
  54  dews dripping with blood out of the upper air, because he was about[7]
  55  to hurl down to Hades many strong heads.
  56  The Trojans on the other side, on the swell of the plain,
  57  gathered around great Hector and blameless Polydamas
  58  and Aeneas, honored like a god among Trojans by the people,
  59  and Antenor's three sons — Polybus, and brilliant Agenor,
  60  and young Acamas, the image of the immortals.
  61  Hector among the foremost carried his shield, balanced every way.
  62  As out of clouds a baleful star shows clear,[8]
  63  all shining, and then sinks again into the shadowed clouds,
  64  so Hector would appear now among the foremost,
  65  now in the rearmost ranks, giving commands; and all in bronze
  66  he flashed like the lightning of father Zeus who bears the aegis.
  67  And as reapers, facing one another down the rows,[9]
  68  drive their swathes through a rich man's field
  69  of wheat or barley, and the handfuls fall thick and fast,
  70  so Trojans and Achaeans, springing at one another,
  71  cut men down, and neither side thought of ruinous flight.
  72  The battle held their heads level; and they raged
  73  like wolves. And Strife of the many groans rejoiced to see it,
  74  for she alone of the gods was with them as they fought;
  75  the other gods were not there, but at their ease
  76  they sat in their own halls, where for each of them
  77  a fair house was built along the folds of Olympus.
  78  And all were blaming the son of Cronos, lord of the dark cloud,[10]
  79  because his will was to hand the glory to the Trojans.
  80  The father took no thought of them. Withdrawn apart,
  81  he sat away from the others, exulting in his glory,
  82  looking out at the city of the Trojans and the ships of the Achaeans,
  83  at the flash of bronze, at the killers and the killed.
  84  So long as it was morning, and the sacred day still growing,[11]
  85  so long the missiles of both sides struck home, and the people fell.
  86  But at the hour a woodcutter makes his meal ready
  87  in the mountain glens, when his arms have had their fill
  88  of felling the tall trees, and weariness comes on his heart,
  89  and desire for sweet food takes hold of his mind,
  90  at that hour the Danaans by their courage broke the battle-lines,
  91  calling to their companions down the ranks. And Agamemnon
  92  charged first, and killed a man: Bienor, shepherd of the people —
  93  the man himself, then his companion Oïleus, lasher of horses,
  94  who had leapt down from the chariot and stood to face him;
  95  and as he came straight on, he stabbed him with the sharp spear
  96  in the forehead; the helmet's heavy bronze rim did not stop it —
  97  through rim and bone it went, and the brain
  98  was all spattered inside it. He broke him in mid-charge.
  99  And the lord of men Agamemnon left them there,
 100  their chests gleaming bare, when he had stripped their tunics,
 101  and went on to kill Isus and Antiphus,
 102  two sons of Priam, bastard and true-born both,
 103  riding one chariot: the bastard held the reins,
 104  and famed Antiphus stood beside him to fight. Achilles
 105  once bound these two with willow shoots, in the spurs of Ida,
 106  taking them as they watched their sheep, and freed them for ransom.[12]
 107  But now the son of Atreus, wide-ruling Agamemnon,
 108  struck one with the spear in the chest above the nipple,
 109  and struck Antiphus by the ear with the sword, and threw him from the chariot.
 110  Hurrying, he stripped their fine armor from them —
 111  knowing them: for he had seen them once before by the swift ships,
 112  when swift-footed Achilles brought them down from Ida.
 113  As a lion easily crushes the helpless young
 114  of a swift deer, seizing them in his strong teeth,
 115  having come to their bed, and takes their soft life away,
 116  and the mother, even if she chances to be very near, can do
 117  nothing to help them, for terrible trembling comes on her too,
 118  and swiftly she darts away through the thick brush and the timber,
 119  sweating, at speed, before the assault of the mighty beast —
 120  so no one of the Trojans could ward destruction
 121  from those two; they themselves were fleeing before the Argives.
 122  Next Peisander and Hippolochus, staunch in the fight,
 123  sons of war-minded Antimachus — the man who, above all,
 124  taking Alexandros's gold, his glittering gifts,
 125  opposed giving Helen back to tawny-haired Menelaus —
 126  his two sons the lord Agamemnon caught,
 127  both in one chariot, trying to hold their swift horses,
 128  for the shining reins had slipped out of their hands
 129  and the pair had panicked. Like a lion the son of Atreus
 130  rose to meet them; and from the chariot the two begged him:
 131  "Take us alive, son of Atreus — and take a worthy ransom.
 132  Many treasures lie stored in the house of Antimachus,
 133  bronze, and gold, and iron worked with much labor:
 134  out of these our father would gladden you with ransom past counting
 135  if he learned we were alive by the ships of the Achaeans."
 136  So the two of them, weeping, addressed the king
 137  with honeyed words; but the voice they heard was unhoneyed:[13]
 138  "If you two are truly sons of war-minded Antimachus,
 139  who once in the Trojan assembly urged that Menelaus,
 140  come as an envoy with godlike Odysseus,
 141  be killed on the spot and not released back to the Achaeans —
 142  now you will pay for your father's foul outrage."
 143  He spoke, and thrust Peisander from the chariot to the ground,
 144  striking him with the spear in the chest; he crashed on his back in the dust.
 145  Hippolochus leapt away; and him he killed on the ground,
 146  shearing his hands off with the sword, and striking off his neck,
 147  and sent him rolling like a stone through the crowd.
 148  He left them; and where the thickest battle-lines were driven,
 149  there he charged in, and with him other strong-greaved Achaeans.
 150  Foot-soldiers killed foot-soldiers, forced into flight,
 151  and horsemen horsemen — from under them rose the dust
 152  out of the plain, stirred by the thundering feet of horses —
 153  dealing death with the bronze. And the lord Agamemnon,
 154  killing always, followed, calling on the Argives.
 155  As when annihilating fire falls on unthinned timber,
 156  and the whirling wind carries it everywhere, and the thickets
 157  fall root and all under the fire's assault,
 158  so under Atreus' son Agamemnon fell the heads
 159  of Trojans in flight; and many strong-necked horses
 160  rattled empty chariots down the causeways of the battlefield,
 161  missing their blameless charioteers — who lay on the earth,
 162  far dearer now to the vultures than to their wives.[14]
 163  But Hector — Zeus drew him out of the missiles and the dust,
 164  out of the man-slaughter, out of the blood and the tumult,
 165  while Atreus' son followed on, hot, calling to the Danaans.
 166  Past the tomb of ancient Ilus, Dardanus' descendant,[15]
 167  across the middle of the plain, past the fig tree they streamed,
 168  straining for the city; and always Atreus' son followed
 169  screaming, his invincible hands spattered with gore.
 170  But when they came to the Scaean Gates and the oak tree,
 171  there at last they halted, and stood waiting for one another.
 172  Others were still in full flight across the plain, like cattle
 173  a lion has set running, coming on them in the milking-dark of night —[16]
 174  all of them; but to one of them sheer destruction appears:
 175  her neck he breaks first, seizing it in his strong teeth,
 176  and then he gulps down the blood and all the inward parts;
 177  so Atreus' son, the lord Agamemnon, pressed them,
 178  killing always the hindmost man; and they fled before him.
 179  Many fell from their chariots on their faces, or on their backs,
 180  under Atreus' son's hands, for he raged with the spear before all.[17]
 181  But when he was on the point of coming up under the city
 182  and the steep wall, then the father of men and gods
 183  came down from heaven and took his seat on the peaks
 184  of Ida of the many springs; and he held the lightning in his hands.
 185  And he sent Iris of the golden wings to carry his word:
 186  "Up, go, swift Iris; tell this word to Hector.
 187  So long as he sees Agamemnon, shepherd of the people,
 188  raging among the front-fighters, cutting down the ranks of men,
 189  so long let him give ground, and command the rest of the army
 190  to fight the enemy through the strong encounter.
 191  But when, struck by a spear or hit by an arrow,
 192  Agamemnon leaps to his horses, then I will hand him the power
 193  to kill, until he reaches the well-benched ships
 194  and the sun goes down and the sacred darkness comes on."[18]
 195  So he spoke, and wind-footed swift Iris did not disobey,
 196  and went down from the hills of Ida to holy Ilion.
 197  She found the son of war-minded Priam, brilliant Hector,
 198  standing among his horses and his bolted chariot;
 199  and standing close, swift-footed Iris spoke to him:
 200  "Hector, son of Priam, the equal of Zeus in counsel,
 201  Zeus the father has sent me out to tell you this.
 202  So long as you see Agamemnon, shepherd of the people,
 203  raging among the front-fighters, cutting down the ranks of men,
 204  so long yield from the fighting, and command the rest of the army
 205  to fight the enemy through the strong encounter.
 206  But when, struck by a spear or hit by an arrow,
 207  Agamemnon leaps to his horses, then Zeus will hand you the power
 208  to kill, until you reach the well-benched ships
 209  and the sun goes down and the sacred darkness comes on."
 210  So speaking, swift-footed Iris went her way,
 211  and Hector with his weapons leapt from the chariot to the ground,
 212  and shaking his sharp spears went everywhere through the army,
 213  stirring them to fight; and he woke the dread din of battle.
 214  They wheeled about and stood to face the Achaeans,
 215  and the Argives on their side strengthened their battle-lines.
 216  The battle was set, and they stood face to face; and Agamemnon
 217  charged first, determined to fight far ahead of all.
 218  Tell me now, Muses, who keep your homes on Olympus —[19]
 219  who was the first to come against Agamemnon,
 220  either of the Trojans themselves or of their famed allies?
 221  Iphidamas, Antenor's son, a good man and a big one,
 222  who was reared in Thrace of the deep soil, mother of flocks.
 223  Cisses raised him in his house when he was little,
 224  his mother's father, who begot fair-cheeked Theano;
 225  and when he reached the measure of glorious youth,
 226  Cisses tried to keep him there, and offered him his daughter.
 227  But newly wed, out of the bridal chamber he went chasing the report
 228  of the Achaeans, with twelve beaked ships that followed him.
 229  The balanced ships he left behind at Percote,
 230  and came on foot the rest of the way to Ilion;
 231  and now he came against Atreus' son Agamemnon.
 232  And when they were close, advancing on one another,
 233  Atreus' son missed his cast; the spear was turned aside;
 234  but Iphidamas stabbed at the belt, below the corslet,
 235  and leaned into the thrust, trusting his heavy hand.
 236  He did not pierce the glittering war-belt: long before that,
 237  meeting the silver, the point bent back like lead.
 238  And wide-ruling Agamemnon caught the shaft in his hand
 239  and dragged it toward him, furious as a lion, and out of his grip
 240  wrenched it; and he struck his neck with the sword, and unstrung his limbs.
 241  So he fell there, and slept a sleep of bronze,[20]
 242  pitiable man — far from his wedded bride, helping his townsmen,
 243  far from the wife he had seen no joy of, though he gave much for her:
 244  first he gave a hundred cattle, then promised a thousand
 245  goats and sheep together, out of his numberless flocks.
 246  But now Agamemnon, Atreus' son, stripped him bare
 247  and carried his fine armor away through the crowd of the Achaeans.
 248  But when Coon saw him — a man conspicuous among men,
 249  Antenor's eldest-born — strong grief came down
 250  over his eyes for his fallen brother.
 251  He took his stand aside with his spear, unseen by brilliant Agamemnon,
 252  and stabbed him mid-forearm, below the elbow;
 253  and the point of the shining spear drove clean through.
 254  Then the lord of men Agamemnon shuddered —
 255  but even so he did not leave off from battle and war:
 256  he sprang at Coon, gripping his wind-toughened spear.
 257  Coon was dragging Iphidamas, his brother by one father,
 258  hurrying, holding his foot, calling to all the best men;
 259  but as he dragged him through the crowd, under his bossed shield
 260  Agamemnon thrust with the bronze-shod shaft, and unstrung his limbs,
 261  and stepped up close and cut his head off, over Iphidamas.[21]
 262  There the sons of Antenor, at the hands of the king, Atreus' son,
 263  filled up their measure of doom and went down into the house of Hades.
 264  But he kept ranging down the ranks of the other men
 265  with spear and sword and with great stones,
 266  so long as the blood still welled warm from his wound.
 267  But when the wound began to dry, and the blood stopped,
 268  sharp pains came sinking into the strength of Atreus' son.
 269  As when the sharp shaft of labor seizes a woman in her pangs,[22]
 270  the piercing shaft the Eileithyiai of hard labor send,
 271  Hera's daughters, keepers of the bitter pangs,
 272  so sharp pains sank into the strength of Atreus' son.
 273  He sprang into the chariot and told his charioteer
 274  to drive to the hollow ships; for his heart was heavy.
 275  And he cried a piercing cry, to carry to the Danaans:
 276  "Friends, leaders and lords of the Argives,
 277  yours be the task now: keep from the seafaring ships
 278  the grim din of battle, since Zeus the counselor has not
 279  allowed me to war with the Trojans all day long."
 280  So he spoke; and the charioteer whipped the fair-maned horses
 281  toward the hollow ships, and the two flew, nothing loath;
 282  their chests were white with foam, dusted beneath with dust,
 283  as they carried the worn king out of the fighting.
 284  And when Hector saw Agamemnon going off,
 285  he called to Trojans and Lycians with a great shout:
 286  "Trojans and Lycians and Dardanians who fight at close quarters —
 287  be men, my friends; remember your rushing courage.
 288  Their best man is gone, and Zeus the son of Cronos has given
 289  great glory to me. Drive your whole-hoofed horses straight
 290  at the powerful Danaans, and win the higher glory."
 291  So speaking he stirred the fury and the heart of every man.
 292  As when some hunter sets his white-toothed hounds
 293  on a wild boar or a lion,
 294  so on the Achaeans Hector, Priam's son, the equal
 295  of Ares bane of mortals, set the great-hearted Trojans.
 296  He himself strode among the foremost, thinking great things,
 297  and fell upon the battle like a howling squall
 298  that leaps down and lashes the violet-colored sea.
 299  Whom first, whom last did he strip of his arms,[23]
 300  Hector, Priam's son, when Zeus gave him the glory?
 301  Asaeus first, and Autonous, and Opites,
 302  and Dolops, Clytius' son, and Opheltius, and Agelaus,
 303  and Aesymnus, and Orus, and Hipponous staunch in the fight.
 304  These leaders of the Danaans he took, and after them
 305  the multitude — as when the West Wind batters the clouds
 306  of the white South Wind, striking with deep squall,
 307  and the swollen wave rolls huge, and the spray
 308  is scattered high under the wandering wind's blast,
 309  so thick and fast were the heads of the army beaten down by Hector.
 310  And now there would have been ruin, deeds beyond remedy,[24]
 311  and the Achaeans in flight would have flung themselves on their ships,
 312  had not Odysseus called to Tydeus' son Diomedes:
 313  "Tydeus' son, what has come over us? We have forgotten our rushing courage.
 314  Come here, old friend; stand beside me. It will be disgrace
 315  if Hector of the flashing helmet takes the ships."
 316  Answering him spoke strong Diomedes:
 317  "I will stand, certainly, and I will endure it; but small
 318  will be our profit, since Zeus the cloud-gatherer
 319  would rather hand the power to the Trojans than to us."
 320  He spoke, and thrust Thymbraeus from his chariot to the ground,
 321  striking him with the spear by the left nipple; and Odysseus
 322  killed Molion, the godlike aide of that same lord.
 323  They left them there, done with fighting for good,
 324  and the two went through the crowd wreaking havoc, as when two boars
 325  fall on the hunting hounds, thinking great things;
 326  so, turning back, they cut down Trojans; and the Achaeans
 327  gladly caught their breath in their flight from brilliant Hector.
 328  Then they took a chariot and two men, the best of their district,
 329  two sons of Merops of Percote, who beyond all men[25]
 330  knew the seer's art, and would not let his sons
 331  go to man-destroying war; but the two would not
 332  obey him: the fates of black death were leading them on.
 333  Tydeus' son Diomedes, famed with the spear,
 334  robbed them of life and breath and took their glorious armor;
 335  and Odysseus killed Hippodamus and Hypeirochus.
 336  Then the son of Cronos stretched the battle level between them,
 337  looking down from Ida; and they went on killing one another.
 338  Tydeus' son wounded the hero Agastrophus with his spear,
 339  Paeon's son, at the hip-joint; his horses were not
 340  near for escape — great was the blindness of his heart:
 341  his aide was holding them apart, while he on foot
 342  raged through the front-fighters, until he lost his own life.
 343  But Hector marked them sharply down the ranks, and rushed at them
 344  shouting; and with him followed the Trojan battle-lines.
 345  Diomedes, good at the war-cry, shuddered to see him,
 346  and quickly spoke to Odysseus, who was near:
 347  "Here it comes rolling at the two of us, this plague — massive Hector.
 348  Come, let us stand and beat him off, holding our ground."
 349  He spoke, and balanced his long-shadowed spear and hurled it,
 350  and hit — he did not miss — aiming at the head,
 351  at the helmet's crown; but bronze was turned by bronze
 352  and never reached the fair skin: the helmet stopped it,
 353  three-layered, socket-eyed, which Phoebus Apollo gave him.[26]
 354  And Hector ran back an immense way, and merged with the crowd,
 355  dropped to his knees and stayed there, propping himself
 356  with one thick hand on the earth; and black night covered his eyes.
 357  But while Tydeus' son went after his spear's cast,
 358  far through the front-fighters, where he saw it strike the earth,
 359  Hector caught his breath again, and springing back into his chariot
 360  drove out into the mass of men, and dodged black doom.
 361  Rushing after with his spear, strong Diomedes called:
 362  "Dog! Again you have run out from under death — and yet the evil[27]
 363  came close. Now once more Phoebus Apollo has pulled you out —
 364  to him you must pray whenever you go into the thud of javelins.
 365  I will finish you yet, meeting you later,
 366  if any god anywhere stands helper to me too.
 367  For now I will go after the others, whoever I can catch."
 368  He spoke, and set to stripping Paeon's spear-famed son.
 369  But Alexandros, the husband of lovely-haired Helen,
 370  drew his bow against Tydeus' son, shepherd of the people,
 371  leaning against the pillar on the man-made mound,
 372  the tomb of Ilus, Dardanus' son, elder of the people of old.
 373  Diomedes was lifting the corslet of powerful Agastrophus
 374  from his chest, all glittering, and the shield from his shoulders,
 375  and the heavy helmet — and Paris drew back the grip of his bow
 376  and shot; and the shaft did not leave his hand in vain:
 377  he hit the flat of the right foot, and the arrow, clean through,
 378  fixed in the earth. And laughing very sweetly
 379  Paris leapt up out of his hiding-place, and spoke in triumph:[28]
 380  "You are hit! The arrow did not fly in vain. If only
 381  I had struck deep in the groin and taken your life!
 382  Then the Trojans could have breathed again after their misery —
 383  they who shudder at you as bleating goats at a lion."
 384  Unshaken, strong Diomedes answered him:
 385  "Bowman, foul-mouth, glorious with your curls, ogler of girls —[29]
 386  if you made trial of me face to face, in armor,
 387  your bow would not help you, and your showers of arrows;
 388  as it is you have scratched the flat of my foot, and boast of it.
 389  I care no more than if a woman had hit me, or a witless child;
 390  blunt is the dart of a worthless man, a nobody.
 391  From me it is otherwise: even if it only grazes,
 392  the point is sharp, and makes its man a corpse on the instant.
 393  His wife's cheeks are torn on both sides in mourning,
 394  his children are fatherless; and he, reddening the earth with his blood,
 395  rots, and there are more birds than women about him."
 396  So he spoke; and Odysseus, famed with the spear, came near
 397  and stood in front of him; and sitting behind, Diomedes drew
 398  the swift arrow out of his foot; and hard pain went through his flesh.
 399  He sprang into the chariot and told his charioteer
 400  to drive to the hollow ships; for his heart was heavy.
 401  Now Odysseus, famed with the spear, was left alone: not one
 402  of the Argives stayed by him, for fear had seized them all.
 403  Troubled, he spoke to his own great-hearted spirit:
 404  "Ah me, what happens to me now? Great evil if I run,
 405  fearing their numbers; but grimmer still if I am taken
 406  alone — and the son of Cronos has set the rest of the Danaans running.
 407  But why does my own heart argue these things with me?
 408  For I know that cowards walk away from the war,
 409  but whoever would be best in battle, his duty is
 410  to stand his ground with strength, whether he is hit or hits another."[30]
 411  While he was turning this over in mind and heart,
 412  the ranks of the Trojan shield-men came on
 413  and penned him in their midst — setting their own plague among them.
 414  As when hounds and young men in their vigor rush
 415  a boar on every side, and he comes out of the deep thicket
 416  whetting the white tusk in his curved jaws,
 417  and they charge in around him, and from under comes the noise
 418  of gnashing tusks; but they stand up to him, terrible as he is —
 419  so then around Odysseus, dear to Zeus, the Trojans
 420  rushed. And he first wounded blameless Deïopites
 421  above the shoulder, leaping in with his sharp spear;
 422  and after that he stripped the life from Thoon and Ennomus,
 423  and next Chersidamas, as he sprang down from his chariot,
 424  he stabbed with the spear below the bossed shield
 425  in the navel; and he fell in the dust and clutched the earth with his palm.
 426  These he left, and wounded with the spear Charops, Hippasus' son,
 427  own brother of wealthy Socus;
 428  and Socus came to his rescue, a man like a god,
 429  and came up very close and spoke his word to him:
 430  "Odysseus of the many tales, insatiate of tricks and of labor,[31]
 431  today you will either boast over both of Hippasus' sons —
 432  such a pair of men! — for killing them and stripping their armor,
 433  or struck under my spear you will lose your life."
 434  So speaking, he struck the shield balanced every way:
 435  through the shining shield the heavy spear went,
 436  and through the richly worked corslet it drove,
 437  and tore all the flesh clean from his ribs; but Pallas Athena
 438  did not let it reach the man's inward parts.
 439  Odysseus knew the point had not come home to a mortal spot,
 440  and drawing back he spoke his word to Socus:
 441  "Poor wretch — sheer destruction has surely caught you now.
 442  True: you have stopped my fighting against the Trojans;
 443  but I say that death and black doom will be yours
 444  here, on this day; broken under my spear you will give
 445  the triumph to me, and your soul to Hades of the famed colts."
 446  He spoke; and Socus had turned and was moving into flight,
 447  and square in his back as he turned he fixed the spear
 448  between the shoulders, and drove it through the chest.
 449  He fell with a thud; and brilliant Odysseus exulted over him:
 450  "Socus, son of war-minded Hippasus, breaker of horses:
 451  death's end was too quick for you — you did not slip it.
 452  Poor wretch! Your father and your lady mother will not[32]
 453  close your eyes in death; the flesh-eating birds
 454  will pull at you long before, their wings beating thick about you.
 455  But me, when I die, the brilliant Achaeans will bury with honors."
 456  So speaking, he dragged the massive spear of war-minded Socus
 457  out of his flesh and out of his bossed shield;
 458  and when it was wrenched free the blood sprang up, and his heart sank.
 459  And the great-hearted Trojans, when they saw Odysseus's blood,
 460  called to one another through the crowd, and all made for him.
 461  He gave ground backward, and shouted to his companions.
 462  Three times he shouted, as loud as a man's head can hold,
 463  and three times Menelaus, dear to Ares, heard him crying,

 465  "Ajax, Zeus-sprung son of Telamon, marshal of the people,

 467  and it sounds as though the Trojans had cut him off alone
 468  in the strong encounter and were forcing him under.
 469  Come, let us go through the crowd; better to defend him.
 470  I fear he may suffer something, alone among the Trojans,
 471  brave as he is, and a great longing come to the Danaans."
 472  So speaking he led the way, and the other followed, a man like a god.
 473  They found Odysseus, dear to Zeus; and around him
 474  the Trojans pressed, like tawny jackals in the mountains

 476  with an arrow from the bowstring, and it escaped him, running
 477  on its feet, while the blood ran warm and its knees still lifted;

 480  in a shadowy grove; but then some god leads in a ravening
 481  lion, and the jackals scatter in terror, and he does the tearing —[33]

 483  the Trojans pressed, many and stout, while the hero
 484  lunging with his spear held off the pitiless day.

 488  holding him by the hand, till his aide drove the horses close.

 490  Priam's bastard son, and then wounded Pandocus,

 500  the heads of men were falling, and the quenchless roar had risen,

 503  with spear and horsemanship, wrecking the young men's battle-lines.

 509  that the enemy might take him as the fighting tilted;

 512  up, mount your chariot; let Machaon mount beside you;

 532  with the whistling whip; and feeling the stroke

 537  and from the wheel-rims. And Hector strained to plunge into the crowd
 538  of men and break it, leaping in; and an evil tumult

 540  But he kept ranging down the ranks of the other men
 541  with spear and sword and with great stones —
 542  yet he shunned the fight with Ajax, Telamon's son.

 841  but even so I will not abandon you in your suffering."
 842  He spoke, and gripping him under the chest led the shepherd of the people
 843  to his hut; and his aide, seeing them, spread down oxhides.
 844  There Patroclus laid him at full length, and with a knife cut
 845  the sharp, biting arrow out of his thigh, and washed the black blood
 846  from it with warm water, and cast on a bitter root,
 847  rubbing it between his hands, a pain-killer, which stayed
 848  all his pains; and the wound began to dry, and the blood stopped.[44]

NOTES

[1] (11.1-2) Dawn rises "from her bed beside illustrious Tithonus" —
    the same couplet opens Odyssey 5, a famous cross-epic doublet.
    Tithonus is the Trojan prince (brother of Priam, 20.237) whom
    Dawn carried off as her consort; the story of his withering
    immortality is later. A new dawn-formula for the register,
    distinct from "early-born rosy-fingered Dawn": this is the dawn
    of the poem's longest day, which will not set until 18.239-242.

[2] (11.3-14) Strife (Eris) opens the day as Dream opened Book 2 —
    Zeus's emissary to a sleeping army. Lines 5-9 repeat the camp
    geometry of 8.222-226 in our same words: Odysseus's ship lies at
    the midpoint of the beached fleet, Ajax and Achilles hold the
    exposed ends. And 13-14 repeat 2.453-454 ("war became sweeter to
    them than going home...") — there the effect of Odysseus and
    Athena rallying a mob that had run for the ships; here the work
    of a shrieking goddess. Ancient critics (Zenodotus omitted,
    Aristophanes and Aristarchus athetized) judged the couplet
    wrongly repeated here; the poet's point may be exactly the
    repetition: what rhetoric did in Book 2, mere din does now.

[3] (11.20-23) Cinyras, legendary king of Cyprus — the island's only
    appearance in the Iliad. He hears the "great report" (κλέος) of
    the coming war and buys his distance from it with a corslet: a
    guest-gift as diplomatic exemption. Later tradition made him
    promise fifty ships and send one real ship and forty-nine clay
    models; the poem knows only the elegant bribe.

[4] (11.24-28) κύανος, rendered "blue enamel," is dark-blue glass
    paste or niello inlay — a genuine Bronze Age luxury technique
    (the inlaid daggers of Mycenae). Serpents "like rainbows" that
    Zeus "sets in a cloud, a portent for mortal men": the rainbow as
    omen of war or storm (17.547-549), armor that carries the day's
    weather on the king's chest.

[5] (11.36-37) The Gorgon between Terror and Panic (Deimos and
    Phobos, capitalized personifications since 4.440): the shield's
    face is the face of battle itself — cf. the aegis at 5.739-742,
    which carries the same company.

[6] (11.45-46) Athena and Hera thunder in honor of "the king of
    Mycenae rich in gold" — a unique gesture: thunder is Zeus's
    instrument, but the arming of the paramount king draws a salute
    from the two pro-Achaean goddesses. The aristeia of Agamemnon
    is thus formally announced; it will last some two hundred lines.

[7] (11.52-55) Zeus rains dew dripping with blood — the sky itself
    hemorrhaging before the killing (again at 16.459, for Sarpedon,
    where the drops are explicitly tears). Line 55 reads "many
    strong heads" where the proem (1.3) has "many strong souls";
    ancient copies show the variant ψυχάς here too — the echo of the
    poem's opening is audible either way.

[8] (11.62-66) Hector as the "baleful star" (οὔλιος ἀστήρ) that
    shows and hides among clouds: the star similes track him and
    Achilles across the poem until Achilles becomes the Dog Star
    itself at 22.26-31 — "brightest of all, but an evil sign."

[9] (11.67-73) The reapers: two lines of harvesters facing each
    other, driving swathes toward the middle of a rich man's field
    — armies as farmhands, the plain as harvest. "The battle held
    their heads level" (72): the fighting itself is the subject,
    holding the two lines like a beam of scales (cf. Zeus's actual
    scales, 8.69). Then the wolves; and Strife alone of the gods
    present, rejoicing — the day's one spectator-deity on the field,
    while Zeus watches from Ida "exulting in his glory."

[10] (11.78-83) The gods sit at home blaming Zeus; he ignores them.
    Zenodotus omitted the passage and Aristophanes and Aristarchus
    athetized it, apparently as inconsistent with the gods' oath-
    bound quiescence of Book 8. But the tableau — all heaven sulking
    in its fine houses while one god watches the killing with
    pleasure — is the day's theology in miniature.

[11] (11.84-91) The register morning-line (fixed at 8.66-67) is
    followed by the poem's homeliest time-stamp: the hour when the
    woodcutter, arms weary, makes his midday meal. Homer's clock
    runs on labor, not hours — dawn, the woodcutter's dinner, the
    unyoking of oxen (16.779). At that hour the line breaks, and
    the aristeia proper begins.

[12] (11.101-112) Isus and Antiphus: Achilles once caught these
    same two on Ida, bound them with willow shoots, and released
    them for ransom — pastoral war, an economy of capture and
    return. Agamemnon recognizes them and kills them: the third
    ransom-refusal of the poem (6.45-65 Adrestus; 10.378-457 Dolon)
    and the mark of what the war has become since Achilles left it.

[13] (11.122-147) Peisander and Hippolochus: their father
    Antimachus took Paris's gold to block the return of Helen, and
    proposed murdering the ambassador Menelaus (the embassy of
    3.205-224). Agamemnon's sentence — "now you will pay for your
    father's foul outrage" — is justice as inherited debt, and the
    execution (hands sheared off, head struck off, the trunk sent
    "rolling like a stone") is the poem's most clinical mutilation.
    The frame is a bitter pun kept from the Greek: they beg "with
    honeyed words" (μειλιχίοις) and hear a voice "unhoneyed"
    (ἀμείλικτον). Compare 21.98-135, where Achilles will answer a
    suppliant's plea with the same adjective and worse.

[14] (11.159-162) The empty chariots rattling down the causeways,
    their drivers "far dearer now to the vultures than to their
    wives" — the aristeia's cost stated in one hexameter of domestic
    arithmetic. It prepares the Iphidamas vignette (note [20]).

[15] (11.166-168) The rout streams past the tomb of Ilus and the
    wild fig tree: the plain's fixed survey-marks (the tomb: 10.415,
    and again 11.371; the fig tree: 6.433, 22.145). Landmarks let
    the audience feel the front move — from mid-plain to the Scaean
    Gates and the oak, and back again within the book.

[16] (11.172-178) "in the milking-dark of night" (νυκτὸς ἀμολγῷ) —
    an already-obscure formula (recurring 15.324, 22.28, 22.317)
    connected by the ancients with milking-time; the lion who
    scatters the whole herd but eviscerates one cow is Agamemnon
    exactly: the multitude flees, the hindmost dies.

[17] (11.179-180) Omitted by Zenodotus, athetized by Aristarchus
    (179-180) and Aristophanes (180): the couplet generalizing the
    slaughter ("many fell from their chariots...") judged a weak
    doublet of what precedes.

[18] (11.185-194) Zeus's message is the day's script, delivered in
    advance: Agamemnon's wounding as the trigger-clause, Hector's
    power to run "until he reaches the ships and the sun goes down
    and the sacred darkness comes on." Everything from here to
    18.242 executes this sentence. Iris relays it with the change
    the grammar demands and nothing more (202-209 = 187-194) —
    divine messages are verbatim or they are nothing (cf. 2.23-33).

[19] (11.218-220) The Muse-invocation repeats 2.484 in our fixed
    line — full formality for a single duel: Iphidamas is granted a
    catalogue-style entrance, which is his funeral honor too (the
    other invocations: 2.484, 14.508, 16.112).

[20] (11.221-247) Iphidamas of Thrace: raised by his grandfather
    Cisses, married to his young aunt (Theano's sister — the poet
    does not pause over it), he left the bridal chamber for the war
    and "slept a sleep of bronze" — χάλκεον ὕπνον, the poem's
    hardest metal laid over its softest noun. The bride-price
    arithmetic — a hundred cattle down, a thousand head promised —
    turns the obituary into a ledger: everything paid, nothing
    enjoyed. This is the Iliad's marriage-economics of death at its
    most explicit, and the template for Protesilaus (2.700-702) and
    for Othryoneus later (13.363-382).

[21] (11.248-263) Coon wounds Agamemnon and dies trying to drag his
    brother's body clear; Agamemnon beheads him over the corpse —
    the two sons of Antenor "filled up their measure of doom" in one
    another's arms. Antenor, the Trojan advocate of returning Helen
    (7.347-353), loses two sons in this book to the king Helen was
    taken from; the poet says nothing, and the silence does the work.

[22] (11.269-272) The wounded king's pain is a woman's labor: the
    sharp shaft the Eileithyiai send, "Hera's daughters, keepers of
    the bitter pangs." The inversion is deliberate and unique — the
    most masculine act in the heroic economy, the aristeia, ends in
    the most feminine of agonies; war-wound and birth-pang are the
    poem's two involuntary cries. The Eileithyiai (birth-goddesses)
    enter the register here; one returns at 16.187, 19.103.

[23] (11.299-300) The narrator's roll-call question, fixed at 5.703:
    here it marks the transfer of the aristeia — the same frame that
    measured Hector-and-Ares now measures Hector alone, with "when
    Zeus gave him the glory" as the ominous answer-clause.

[24] (11.310-319) The register despair-line (8.130) sounds again,
    and Diomedes' answer to Odysseus is the day's arithmetic at its
    most honest: I will stand, "but small will be our profit," since
    Zeus wills it otherwise. Piety and futility in one breath — the
    last speech of Diomedes' fighting day.

[25] (11.328-334) The two sons of Merops of Percote fall to
    Diomedes: lines 329-332 repeat their Catalogue entry (2.831-834)
    almost verbatim, our wording kept. The Catalogue planted the
    seer-father who foresaw and forbade and was not obeyed; Book 11
    harvests it. Compare 5.148-151 (the sons of the dream-reader).

[26] (11.349-356) Diomedes' cast rings off the helmet "which
    Phoebus Apollo gave him" — divine equipment as plot-armor — yet
    Hector goes down; 355-356 repeat 5.309-310 (Aeneas's swoon), our
    words kept, and the ancients athetized the repeat here. Hector's
    day of guaranteed glory (192-194) nearly ends at its opening:
    the promise covered the outcome, not the pain.

[27] (11.362-367) "Dog! Again you have run out from under death..."
    — this six-line taunt returns, word for word, in Achilles'
    mouth at 20.449-454. Diomedes has been the poem's stand-in
    Achilles all through the middle books; here he even pre-speaks
    him. Reuse the English verbatim there.

[28] (11.369-379) Paris shoots from the tomb of Ilus, leaning on
    the ancestor's grave-pillar — the city's founder as cover for
    the city's most criticized fighter — and comes up "laughing
    very sweetly." Three captains leave the field by his arrows in
    this book (Diomedes, Machaon, Eurypylus): the day of the bow.

[29] (11.385-395) "Bowman, foul-mouth, glorious with your curls,
    ogler of girls" — the concentrated contempt-vocabulary of the
    spear-ethos. "Glorious with your curls" renders κέρᾳ ἀγλαέ, an
    old crux: "horn" was read by some ancients as the horn bow, by
    others as a horn-shaped lovelock (the reading our translation
    follows; the apparatus records the living dispute). Diomedes'
    disdain — "I care no more than if a woman had hit me, or a
    witless child" — is the poem's fullest statement of the bow's
    dishonor; yet the arrow-graze sends him from the field for the
    rest of the epic's fighting, and an arrow of Paris will one day
    kill Achilles (19.416-417, 22.359-360). The taunt loses the war.

[30] (11.401-410) Odysseus alone: the poem's first full deliberative
    soliloquy ("Troubled, he spoke to his own great-hearted spirit"
    — a frame that will recur for Menelaus, Agenor, Hector). The
    deliberation runs the heroic code to its bare axiom: "cowards
    walk away; whoever would be best must stand, hit or hitting."
    Hector's fatal monologue at 22.99-130 is this scene's dark twin
    — there the same logic will forbid the retreat that would have
    saved him.

[31] (11.428-433) Socus — the only man in the poem to wound
    Odysseus — addresses him with a new-minted title: "Odysseus of
    the many tales, insatiate of tricks and of labor." Even his
    enemies speak his epithets; the compliment is the challenge.

[32] (11.450-455) Odysseus over Socus: your parents will not close
    your eyes; the birds will have you; "but me, when I die, the
    brilliant Achaeans will bury with honors." The grimmest speech
    Odysseus makes in the Iliad — burial as the last competitive
    good, boasted over a corpse (Plutarch quotes the lines in his
    Consolation). It is the exact inverse of the poem's ending,
    where the enemy dead is given back for burial.

[33] (11.473-484) The triple simile: jackals round the arrowed stag
    — until "some god leads in" a lion who scatters them and eats.
    Ajax arrives as the lion; the rescued Odysseus, uncomfortably,
    occupies the stag's place, and the "rescue" is another
    predation. In 480 Zenodotus read "hollow" for "shadowy" grove,
    and some copies inserted a line naming the shield of Tychius
    (= 7.220) after 485 — the apparatus notes both; the printed
    text keeps neither.

[34] (11.514-515) "A healer is a man worth many other men together"
    — proverbial ever after; the specifying line 515 ("at cutting
    out arrows and sprinkling gentle drugs") was omitted by
    Zenodotus and athetized by Aristophanes and Aristarchus as a
    prosaic gloss. Homeric medicine is exactly these two skills —
    extraction and pharmacy — practiced by warriors: Machaon and
    Podaleirius are fighting doctors, and when both are down the
    craft passes to laymen taught by laymen (830-832: Patroclus,
    taught by Achilles, taught by Chiron).

[35] (11.543) Our printed Greek text genuinely lacks line 543 (the
    numbering jumps 542 to 544): the line was absent from the
    medieval manuscripts and is known because Aristotle quotes it,
    discussing righteous indignation (Rhetoric 1387a; cf. Plutarch's
    citations of the passage): "for Zeus would be indignant with
    him, whenever he fought a better man" — Ajax retreating not
    from Hector but from theology. Editors since Wolf print it in
    brackets; our source omits it as the OCT records, and the
    translation reproduces the gap. With 9.458-461, this is the
    second transmission-wound this project keeps visible.

[36] (11.544-565) The double simile of Ajax's retreat: first the
    lion driven all night from the cattle-yard by dogs, javelins,
    and firebrands, going off "sullen at heart" at dawn; then,
    without pause, the donkey — slow, stick-broken, cropping the
    deep grain while boys beat him, leaving only when he is full.
    The lion was athetized by Zenodotus (548-557), and ancient
    taste flinched at the donkey; but the pair is the whole
    portrait: nobility from inside Ajax's chest, comedy from
    outside — the strongest man in the army backing away at his own
    pace, the only donkey in Homer, and one of the great tonal
    risks in epic.

[37] (11.596-604) The hinge of the Iliad. Achilles, "gazing at the
    sheer toil and the tearful rout" from his stern — spectator by
    choice — sees Nestor pass with a wounded man and calls out
    Patroclus, who comes out "the equal of Ares — and that was the
    beginning of his evil" (ἔκμολεν ἶσος Ἄρηϊ, κακοῦ δ' ἄρα οἱ
    πέλεν ἀρχή). The narrator breaks his own reticence to mark the
    moment: a door opens, and the poem names it fate. Achilles'
    stated hope — "now I think the Achaeans will soon stand about
    my knees begging" — shows the wrath still fed by the sight; his
    curiosity about one wounded man is what kills his friend.

[38] (11.632-637) The cup of Nestor: golden studs, four handles,
    doves feeding at each — and the poet's tape-measure detail that
    another man could barely lift it full, "but Nestor, the old
    man, raised it without strain." On a clay cup from Pithekoussai
    (Ischia), c. 730-720 BC, someone scratched three lines: "I am
    the cup of Nestor, good to drink from; whoever drinks from me,
    straightway desire of fair-crowned Aphrodite will seize." It is
    among the oldest surviving Greek writing — and it is a joke
    that only works on an audience that already knew this passage:
    the earliest external witness to the Iliad in circulation.
    Scholars have matched the described cup to Mycenaean finds
    (the gold "dove cup" from Shaft Grave IV); the poem's furniture
    is centuries older than the poem.

[39] (11.624-641) Hecamede — Nestor's prize-woman from Tenedos,
    sacked by Achilles (every comfort in this hut traces back to
    the man refusing to fight) — mixes the κυκεών, "posset":
    Pramnian wine, grated goat's-milk cheese, white barley, with
    honey and onion as relish alongside. Plato quotes the recipe
    (Ion 538c) — and elsewhere (Republic 405e-406a) makes it a
    scandal of Homeric medicine that a wine posset is served to
    wounded men; Machaon, shot through the shoulder, drinks it. The
    same drink, drugged, is Circe's instrument in Odyssey 10 — the
    ordinary hospitality this scene shows straight.

[40] (11.654) "a terrible man. He would quickly blame even the
    blameless" — Patroclus on Achilles, the poem's most intimate
    character-note: fear inside love. Plutarch quotes the line (How
    to Tell a Flatterer). Patroclus will say the harder version to
    Achilles' face at 16.29-35 ("your parent was the grey sea and
    the cliffs").

[41] (11.656-762) Nestor's Pylian epic, his longest memory: the
    cattle-raid against Elis, the debt-division at dawn, Heracles'
    devastation of Pylos (eleven brothers dead), the Moliones saved
    by their father Poseidon, and Nestor's first aristeia — fifty
    chariots taken, "two men around each." Its function is
    paradigm: the young Nestor fought although Neleus forbade him
    and hid his horses; Achilles, with every power to fight,
    withholds. (One line of the tale, 699, is flagged in the
    scholia as suspected spurious.) The close is the old man's
    whole tragedy in seven words: "Such was I among men — if ever
    I was" (εἴ ποτ' ἔον γε) — glory so far off that its owner
    half-doubts it happened.

[42] (11.765-790) The send-off at Peleus's house: the embassy of
    recruitment, the sacrifice in the courtyard, young Achilles
    leaping up to seat the guests — the poem's one glimpse of its
    hero at home, in a scene of pure xenia. Peleus's charge repeats
    6.208 word for word in Greek and in our English — "always to be
    the best, and to hold one's head above all others" — the
    aristocratic imperative issued identically to Glaucus and to
    Achilles; and against it Menoetius sets the other ethic: you
    are lesser-born and weaker, but older — advise him. Patroclus's
    whole life is in that division of labor. Aristophanes and
    Aristarchus athetized 767-785 (the reminiscence's core!) as
    inconsistent in its geography; the vulgate kept it, and so does
    the printed text.

[43] (11.794-803) The fatal plan, invented by Nestor: if a prophecy
    holds Achilles back, "let him send you out at least... and let
    him give you his fine armor to carry into the war, so that the
    Trojans may take you for him." Patroclus will repeat these
    lines to Achilles nearly verbatim at 16.36-45 — reuse this
    English there. Ancient critics chipped at the passage from both
    ends (794-795 athetized by Zenodotus; 802-803 by Aristarchus,
    as lifted from 16.44-45 — where they stand in Patroclus's
    mouth). "You may be a light to the Danaans": the image is of
    the beacon-fire — and what the disguise actually buys is
    Patroclus's death, Hector's brief triumph, and Achilles'
    return. The oldest man in the army, meaning pure good, sets
    the poem's catastrophe in motion in ten lines.

[44] (11.804-848) The delay that structures the middle of the poem:
    Patroclus, running back with Nestor's words in him, is stopped
    at the assembly-ground by wounded Eurypylus, pities him, and
    stays — "even so I will not abandon you in your suffering."
    He cuts out the arrow, washes the wound, crushes the bitter
    root: the healer's craft learned from Achilles, learned from
    Chiron "the most righteous of the Centaurs" — the gentlest
    genealogy in the poem, running through its two great killers.
    Patroclus will still be in this hut at 15.390-404, and what
    finally moves him is watching the wall fail. The book's last
    clause — "and the wound began to dry, and the blood stopped" —
    repeats 267 exactly: Agamemnon's wound opened the day's
    subtraction, Eurypylus's closes the book; between the two
    dryings, the poem has chosen its victim.
