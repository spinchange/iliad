BOOK 19

The wrath is renounced from a seat; the blame is laid on a goddess;
and the gifts nobody looks at are carried past a fasting man — who
arms, mounts, and hears his own horse foretell his death.

TRANSLATOR'S COMMENTARY

Book 19 is called the Renunciation of the Wrath, and its business
is to close the quarrel that opened the poem — which it does in the
strangest scene of public reconciliation in literature. Achilles,
who now wants only to kill, renounces the wrath in nine lines and
wishes Briseis — the girl the quarrel was about — had died before
it started (note [6]). Agamemnon answers seated, "not standing up
in their midst" (the ancient editors were so unsettled that
Zenodotus's text made him stand; note [8]), and delivers not an
apology but a theology: "I am not to blame: Zeus is, and Fate, and
the Erinys who walks in mist" — followed by the great parable of
Ate, Blindness, the soft-footed eldest daughter of Zeus who walks
on the heads of men, and whom Zeus himself, blinded over the birth
of Heracles, hurled from heaven by her shining braids (notes [9],
[10]). Book 9's Blindness was "strong and sound of foot"; this
register keeps the one English name so the two portraits can argue
with each other. And Achilles accepts the theology — "Father Zeus,
great is the blindness you deal to men!" — so the quarrel ends
with both parties agreeing that nobody human did it (note [19]).
The gifts so bitterly itemized in Book 9 are carried into the
middle of the assembly, and the man they were meant to persuade
tells Agamemnon to give them or keep them as he pleases.

The book's real argument is between grief and the belly. Odysseus
— "I was born first and know more things" — insists twice that
armies cannot mourn fasting: his harvest of bronze, "where the
bronze strews the most straw on the ground and the crop is
smallest," and his terrible discipline: bury the dead "keeping a
pitiless heart, weeping for one day" (note [16]). Achilles refuses
— "slaughter, and blood, and the hard groaning of men" is the only
food he wants (note [15]) — and has to be fed like a wick, drop by
drop, with nectar by Athena. Between the two positions stands the
book's finest scene: Briseis, restored at last and given her only
speech in the poem, keening over Patroclus who had promised to make
her Achilles' wedded wife — "You were gentle, always" — while the
narrator, in two lines as cold and true as anything in Homer, notes
that the women wailed with her "for Patroclus in show; but each
for her own sorrows" (note [20]).

Then the arming — snowfall of helmets, the earth laughing at the
lightning of bronze, the shield's light like a lonely mountain fire
seen by sailors storm-driven away from their dear ones (the inverse
of Book 18's rescue-beacon; note [25]) — the Pelian ash only
Achilles can swing (kept verbatim from Book 16, against Aristarchus;
note [26]), and the ending no other book has: the war-horse Xanthus,
given voice by Hera, bows his head till his mane pours to the
ground and tells his master he will die — "by a god, and by a man"
— before the Erinyes shut his mouth. Achilles already knows. He
says so, and drives.

   1  Dawn in her saffron robe rose up from the streams of Ocean[1]
   2  to bring light to the immortals and to mortal men;
   3  and she came to the ships carrying the god's gifts.
   4  She found her dear son lying over Patroclus,
   5  wailing his piercing grief; and around him his many companions
   6  mourned. The brilliant goddess stood among them,
   7  and clung to his hand, and spoke a word, and named him:
   8  "My child — we must let this man lie, for all our grief,[2]
   9  since by the will of the gods, first of all, he was broken.
  10  Now you: take from Hephaestus this famed armor,
  11  beautiful — such as no man has ever worn on his shoulders."
  12  So speaking, the goddess laid the armor down[3]
  13  in front of Achilles; and all the intricate work clashed aloud.
  14  And trembling took all the Myrmidons: not one dared
  15  look at it straight on; they shrank away. But Achilles —
  16  the more he looked, the deeper the anger sank in him, and his eyes
  17  blazed out terribly under their lids like flame-glare;
  18  and he delighted, holding the god's shining gifts in his hands.
  19  And when he had taken his joy of looking at the intricate work,
  20  at once he spoke winged words to his mother:
  21  "Mother, the god has given me arms: such work as is fitting[4]
  22  for immortals — such as no mortal man could have made.
  23  Now I will arm myself in them. But I am terribly
  24  afraid that meanwhile, into the stout son of Menoetius,
  25  flies will enter by the bronze-cut wounds
  26  and breed worms in him, and defile the body —
  27  the life is slain out of it — and all the flesh will rot."
  28  Then answered him the goddess, silver-footed Thetis:
  29  "Child, do not let these things trouble your mind.
  30  I will undertake to beat away those savage tribes,
  31  the flies, that feed on men killed in war.
  32  Even if he lies here till a year runs to its end,
  33  his flesh shall stay sound as it is — or better.
  34  But you: call the Achaean heroes to assembly,
  35  renounce your wrath against Agamemnon, shepherd of the people,
  36  and then arm quickly for the war, and put on your valor."
  37  So speaking, she sent daring strength into him;
  38  and into Patroclus, through the nostrils, she distilled
  39  ambrosia and red nectar, so his flesh might stay sound.
  40  And brilliant Achilles went along the beach of the sea[5]
  41  shouting his terrible shout, and roused the Achaean heroes.
  42  And even those who before had always stayed among the ships —
  43  the helmsmen, the men who kept the steering-oars,
  44  and the stewards who stayed aboard as dealers of bread —
  45  even they went then to the assembly, because Achilles
  46  had appeared, who had long stayed out of the grievous fighting.
  47  And two came limping, the squires of Ares:
  48  war-staunch Tydeus's son and brilliant Odysseus,
  49  leaning on their spears, for they still carried grievous wounds;
  50  and they went and sat down in the front of the assembly.
  51  And last of all came the lord of men Agamemnon,
  52  carrying his wound: for he too, in the strong encounter,
  53  was stabbed by Coon, Antenor's son, with a bronze-fitted spear.
  54  And when all the Achaeans were gathered together,
  55  swift-footed Achilles rose up among them and spoke:
  56  "Son of Atreus — was this, then, in any way the better for us both,[6]
  57  for you and for me, when the two of us, grieved at heart,
  58  raged in heart-devouring strife for the sake of a girl?
  59  I wish Artemis had killed her with an arrow at the ships
  60  on the day I chose her from the sack of Lyrnessus:
  61  then not so many Achaeans would have bitten the boundless earth
  62  under enemy hands, while I raged on in my wrath.
  63  For Hector and the Trojans, the gain. The Achaeans, I think,
  64  will long remember the strife of me and you.
  65  But we will let that be — a thing done and past, for all our grief,[7]
  66  beating down the heart in our chests because we must.
  67  Now I end my anger. There is no need for me
  68  to rage on, relentless, forever. Come then: quickly
  69  drive the long-haired Achaeans into the war,
  70  so that I can go out against the Trojans once again
  71  and test them — whether they choose to spend the night by the ships.
  72  Many a one of them, I think, will be glad to bend the knee,
  73  whoever escapes out of the deadly war from under our spear."
  74  So he spoke; and the strong-greaved Achaeans rejoiced
  75  that the great-hearted son of Peleus renounced the wrath.
  76  And among them spoke also the lord of men Agamemnon —[8]
  77  from the place where he sat, not standing up in their midst:
  78  "Friends, Danaan heroes, squires of Ares:[9]
  79  when a man stands to speak, it is right to listen, and unseemly
  80  to break in on him: that is hard even for a practiced speaker.
  81  In a great uproar of men, how could anyone hear
  82  or speak? Even a clear-voiced speaker is defeated there.
  83  To the son of Peleus I will declare myself. But the rest of you
  84  Argives, mark it, and each man know my word well.
  85  Often the Achaeans have said this thing against me,
  86  and they would revile me for it. But I am not to blame:
  87  Zeus is, and Fate, and the Erinys who walks in mist,
  88  who cast wild blindness into my mind in the assembly
  89  on that day when I myself stripped Achilles of his prize.
  90  But what could I do? A god carries all things to their end:
  91  Blindness, the eldest daughter of Zeus, who blinds[10]
  92  all men — the deadly one. Her feet are soft: not on the ground
  93  does she walk, but over the heads of men
  94  she moves, damaging mankind; and one after another she snares.
  95  For once she blinded even Zeus — and he, they say, is best
  96  of men and gods alike; yet even Zeus
  97  Hera, female as she is, deceived with her scheming
  98  on the day when the might of Heracles was due to be born
  99  of Alcmene, inside fair-crowned Thebes.
 100  For Zeus spoke out in triumph among all the gods:
 101  'Hear me, all you gods and all you goddesses,
 102  while I say what the heart in my chest commands me.
 103  Today Eileithyia of hard labor will bring
 104  into the light a man who shall rule all who dwell around him —
 105  of the race of men who are of my own blood.'
 106  And scheming lady Hera answered him:
 107  'You will prove a liar: you will set no fulfillment on your word.
 108  Come then, Olympian: swear me now a strong oath
 109  that he shall indeed be lord of all who dwell around him
 110  who on this day falls between the feet of a woman,
 111  of those men who are of the race of your blood.'
 112  So she spoke; and Zeus saw none of her scheming,
 113  but swore the great oath — and was blinded to his cost.
 114  And Hera darted down, leaving the peak of Olympus,
 115  and came quickly to Achaean Argos, where she knew
 116  the sturdy wife of Sthenelus, Perseus's son.
 117  She was pregnant with a dear son, and her seventh month was standing;
 118  and Hera brought him out into the light before his month,
 119  and stayed Alcmene's labor, and held back the Eileithyiai.
 120  Then she herself brought the news, and said to Zeus, Cronos's son:
 121  'Father Zeus of the bright bolt — I will put a word in your mind.
 122  Already a fine man is born who shall rule the Argives:
 123  Eurystheus, son of Sthenelus, Perseus's son —
 124  your stock. It is no disgrace for him to rule the Argives.'
 125  So she spoke; and sharp grief struck him deep in his mind.
 126  And at once he seized Blindness by her shining-braided head,
 127  raging in his heart, and swore a strong oath
 128  that never again to Olympus and the starry heaven
 129  would she come back — Blindness, who blinds all.
 130  So speaking, he whirled her in his hand and flung her
 131  out of the starry heaven; and soon she came to the works of men.
 132  Over her he groaned forever, whenever he saw his dear son
 133  doing shameful labor under Eurystheus's tasks.
 134  So I too — when great Hector of the flashing helmet
 135  kept slaughtering Argives at the sterns of the ships,
 136  I could not forget the Blindness that first blinded me.
 137  But since I was blinded, and Zeus took my wits away,[11]
 138  I am willing to make amends, and to give ransom past counting.
 139  Up, then, into the war — and rouse the rest of the army.
 140  The gifts: I am here to furnish all of them, as many
 141  as brilliant Odysseus promised yesterday in your hut.
 142  Or if you wish, hold back a while, pressed though you are for war,
 143  and my attendants will fetch the gifts out of my ship
 144  and bring them here — and you will see I give what contents your heart."
 145  Then in answer spoke to him swift-footed Achilles:
 146  "Most glorious son of Atreus, lord of men Agamemnon:[12]
 147  the gifts — furnish them if you wish, as is proper,
 148  or keep them with you. But now let us remember our war-craft,
 149  at once. There is no need to sit here bandying words,
 150  no need for delay: a great work is still undone —
 151  so that men may see Achilles once again among the front-fighters,
 152  wrecking the Trojan battalions with his bronze spear.
 153  So let each of you remember this, and fight your man."
 154  Then in answer spoke to him resourceful Odysseus:
 155  "Not so — brave though you are, godlike Achilles.[13]
 156  Do not drive the sons of the Achaeans fasting toward Ilion
 157  to fight the Trojans, since the press of battle will not last
 158  a short time only, once the phalanxes of men
 159  close with each other, and the god breathes fury into both.
 160  No — order the Achaeans by the swift ships to take
 161  bread and wine: for that is fury, and that is valor.
 162  No man will be able to fight the whole day through,
 163  to the setting of the sun, fasting from bread:
 164  for even if in his heart he is wild to make war,
 165  his limbs grow heavy on him unawares, and thirst
 166  and hunger overtake him, and his knees fail as he goes.
 167  But the man who has had his fill of wine and food
 168  and fights the enemy men the whole day long —
 169  his heart in his chest is daring, and his limbs
 170  do not tire before all break off from the war.
 171  Come then: dismiss the army, and order the morning meal
 172  made ready. And the gifts — let the lord of men Agamemnon
 173  bring them into the middle of the assembly, so all the Achaeans
 174  may see them with their eyes, and your heart be warmed.
 175  And let him stand up among the Argives and swear you an oath
 176  that he never went up into her bed, he never lay with her,
 177  as is the way, lord, of men and women;
 178  and let the heart in your own chest be gracious.
 179  And then let him appease you with a rich feast
 180  in his hut, so you may lack nothing of your due.
 181  And you, son of Atreus, will be more just toward another man
 182  hereafter. There is no cause for indignation when a king
 183  makes amends to a man, when he was first to turn harsh."
 184  Then answered him the lord of men Agamemnon:[14]
 185  "I am glad, son of Laertes, to hear your speech:
 186  you have gone through it all in due measure, point by point.
 187  The oath — I am willing to swear it; my heart commands me;
 188  and I will not forswear myself before the god. But let Achilles
 189  wait here so long, pressed though he is for the war-god's work;
 190  and wait, all the rest of you, together, until the gifts
 191  come from my hut, and we cut the faithful oaths.
 192  And to you yourself, Odysseus, I give this charge and order:
 193  choose out the noblest young men of all the Achaeans
 194  and carry the gifts from my ship, all that yesterday
 195  we promised to give Achilles — and lead the women too.
 196  And let Talthybius, quickly, through the broad Achaean camp,
 197  make ready a boar, to sacrifice to Zeus and to the Sun."
 198  Then in answer spoke to him swift-footed Achilles:
 199  "Most glorious son of Atreus, lord of men Agamemnon:[15]
 200  some other time were better for busying yourselves with this —
 201  some pause in the fighting, whenever one may come,
 202  some hour when the fury is not so great in my chest.
 203  Now they lie torn — the men whom Hector, Priam's son,
 204  broke, when Zeus was handing him the glory —
 205  and you two urge us to our food! For my part
 206  I would command the sons of the Achaeans to fight now,
 207  fasting, unfed, and at the setting of the sun
 208  make a great supper — once we have paid off the outrage.
 209  Before that, down my own throat, not a thing
 210  shall pass — no drink, no food — my companion being dead,
 211  who lies in my hut torn by the sharp bronze,
 212  turned toward the doorway, and around him our companions
 213  mourn. So those things have no place in my thoughts —
 214  but slaughter, and blood, and the hard groaning of men."
 215  Then in answer spoke to him resourceful Odysseus:
 216  "Achilles, son of Peleus, far greatest of the Achaeans,[16]
 217  you are stronger than I am, and mightier, not by a little,
 218  with the spear; but in thought I would surpass you
 219  by much, since I was born first and know more things.
 220  So let your heart endure to hear my words.
 221  Quickly men have their fill of battle —
 222  that harvest where the bronze strews the most straw on the ground
 223  and the crop is smallest, once Zeus tilts his scales —
 224  Zeus, the dispenser of war to men.
 225  With the belly the Achaeans cannot mourn a corpse:
 226  too many men, one on another, day after day,
 227  are falling: when could anyone breathe from the labor?
 228  No — we must bury the man who dies,
 229  keeping a pitiless heart, weeping for one day;
 230  and all who are left alive from the hateful war
 231  must remember drink and food, so that all the more
 232  we may fight the enemy without pause, forever,
 233  our flesh clothed in unwearying bronze. And let no man
 234  hang back, waiting for a second summons to the army:
 235  this is the summons — and it will go hard with the man who is left
 236  behind at the ships of the Argives. All in one body,
 237  let us wake the sharp war-god against the horse-taming Trojans."
 238  He spoke, and took with him the sons of glorious Nestor,[17]
 239  and Meges, Phyleus's son, and Thoas, and Meriones,
 240  and Lycomedes, Creon's son, and Melanippus;
 241  and they went to the hut of Agamemnon, Atreus's son.
 242  Then no sooner was the word said than the thing was done:
 243  seven tripods they carried out of the hut, as he had promised him,
 244  and twenty gleaming cauldrons, and twelve horses;
 245  and they led out at once the women skilled in blameless work,
 246  seven, and the eighth was fair-cheeked Briseis.
 247  Then Odysseus weighed out ten full talents of gold
 248  and led the way; and the young Achaean men behind him carried the gifts.
 249  And they set them in the middle of the assembly; and Agamemnon[18]
 250  stood up. Talthybius, his voice like a god's,
 251  holding the boar in his hands, stood beside the shepherd of the people.
 252  And the son of Atreus drew out with his hands the knife
 253  that always hung beside the great scabbard of his sword,
 254  cut the first hairs from the boar, and lifting his hands
 255  to Zeus he prayed. And all the Argives sat by in silence,
 256  in due order, listening to the king.
 257  And praying, he spoke, looking up into the wide heaven:
 258  "Let Zeus be witness first — highest of the gods, and best —
 259  and Earth, and the Sun, and the Erinyes, who under the earth
 260  exact payment from men, whoever swears a false oath:
 261  I never laid a hand on the girl Briseis,
 262  neither desiring her for my bed nor for anything else;
 263  untouched she has remained inside my huts.
 264  If any of this is falsely sworn, may the gods give me pains
 265  in plenty — all they give a man who sins against them in his oath."
 266  He spoke, and cut the boar's throat with the pitiless bronze.
 267  And Talthybius whirled it and threw it into the great gulf
 268  of the grey sea, food for fish. Then Achilles
 269  rose up and spoke among the war-loving Argives:
 270  "Father Zeus — great is the blindness you deal to men![19]
 271  Never otherwise would the son of Atreus have stirred
 272  the heart in my chest so thoroughly, nor led the girl off
 273  against my will, unbending. No: it was Zeus, somehow,
 274  who willed that death should come to many Achaeans.
 275  Now go to your meal, so we may join the war-god together."
 276  So he spoke, and dissolved the assembly quickly.
 277  They scattered then, each man to his own ship;
 278  but the great-hearted Myrmidons busied themselves with the gifts
 279  and went carrying them to the ship of godlike Achilles.
 280  They laid them in the huts, and seated the women there,
 281  and proud attendants drove the horses to the herd.
 282  Then Briseis, like golden Aphrodite,[20]
 283  when she saw Patroclus torn by the sharp bronze,
 284  flung herself on him with a piercing wail, and with her hands tore
 285  her breasts and her soft throat and her beautiful face.
 286  And weeping, the woman like the goddesses said:
 287  "Patroclus — dearest of all to my unhappy heart —
 288  alive I left you when I went out of this hut;
 289  now I come back, leader of the people, and find you
 290  dead. So evil follows evil for me, always.
 291  The husband to whom my father and lady mother gave me
 292  I saw torn by the sharp bronze before our city,
 293  and my three brothers, whom one mother bore with me,
 294  beloved — all of them met their day of destruction.
 295  And you would not let me — not even when swift Achilles
 296  killed my husband, and sacked the city of godlike Mynes —
 297  you would not let me weep: you kept saying you would make me
 298  the wedded wife of godlike Achilles, and take me in the ships
 299  to Phthia, and hold my marriage-feast among the Myrmidons.
 300  So I weep your death without ceasing. You were gentle, always."
 301  So she spoke, weeping; and the women wailed in answer —
 302  for Patroclus in show; but each for her own sorrows.
 303  And around Achilles the elders of the Achaeans gathered,[21]
 304  begging him to eat; but he refused them, groaning:
 305  "I beg you — if any of my dear companions will hear me —
 306  do not bid me, before it is time, with food and drink
 307  to sate my own heart, since terrible grief has come to me.
 308  Until the sun goes down I will hold out, and endure regardless."
 309  So speaking, he sent the other kings away,
 310  but the two sons of Atreus stayed, and brilliant Odysseus,
 311  and Nestor, and Idomeneus, and the old horseman Phoenix,
 312  trying to comfort his thick grief. But his heart would not
 313  be comforted, before he entered the mouth of bloody war.
 314  Remembering, he heaved a deep sigh, and spoke aloud:
 315  "There was a time — unhappy man, dearest of my companions —[22]
 316  when you yourself would set the savory meal before me in this hut,
 317  quickly and deftly, whenever the Achaeans were rushing
 318  to carry the war of many tears against the horse-taming Trojans.
 319  Now you lie torn; and my heart goes
 320  without taste of the drink and food that are here in plenty,
 321  for want of you. Nothing worse could I suffer —
 322  not even if I learned my father had died,
 323  who now in Phthia, I suppose, is letting soft tears fall
 324  for lack of a son like me — while I, in a strange country,
 325  fight the Trojans for blood-chilling Helen's sake —
 326  or the death of my dear son, who is being reared for me on Scyros:
 327  if he still lives somewhere — godlike Neoptolemus.
 328  For until now the heart in my chest had hoped
 329  that I alone would die far from horse-pasturing Argos,
 330  here in Troy, and that you would come home to Phthia,
 331  so you might take my child in the swift black ship
 332  out of Scyros, and show him everything of mine —
 333  my possessions, my slaves, my great high-roofed house.
 334  For Peleus by now, I think, is either altogether
 335  dead, or — barely living still — he grieves on
 336  in hateful old age, forever waiting the bitter
 337  news of me: when he shall hear that I am dead."
 338  So he spoke, weeping; and the elders groaned in answer,[23]
 339  each remembering what he had left behind in his own halls.
 340  And seeing them mourning, the son of Cronos pitied them,
 341  and quickly he spoke winged words to Athena:
 342  "My child, you have deserted your man completely.
 343  Is Achilles, then, no longer any care to your mind?
 344  There he sits, in front of his ships with their upright horns,
 345  mourning his dear companion. The others
 346  have gone to their meal; he is fasting, and will not eat.
 347  Go — distill nectar and lovely ambrosia
 348  into his chest, so hunger does not reach him."
 349  So speaking, he urged on Athena, eager already;
 350  and like a falcon, wide-winged and shrill-voiced,
 351  she dropped from heaven through the upper air. And the Achaeans
 352  were arming there and then throughout the camp. To Achilles
 353  she distilled nectar in his chest, and lovely
 354  ambrosia, so joyless hunger would not reach his knees;
 355  and she was gone, back to the close-built house of her mighty
 356  father. And they poured out away from the swift ships.
 357  As when thick snowflakes flutter down from Zeus,[24]
 358  cold under the blast of Boreas born in the clear sky,
 359  so thick then the helmets, glittering bright,
 360  streamed out of the ships, and the bossed shields,
 361  the strong-plated corslets and the ash-wood spears.
 362  The gleam went up to heaven; and all the earth around laughed
 363  at the lightning-flash of bronze; and a thunder rose from under
 364  the feet of men. And in their midst brilliant Achilles armed.
 365  His teeth ground loud against each other; and his two eyes[25]
 366  blazed as if they were flame-glare; and into his heart
 367  sank grief past bearing. Raging against the Trojans,
 368  he put on the gifts of the god that Hephaestus had labored to forge.
 369  First he set the greaves along his shins,
 370  fine greaves, fitted with silver ankle-pieces;
 371  second, he put the corslet about his chest;
 372  and around his shoulders he threw the sword, silver-studded,
 373  bronze; and after that the shield, great and heavy,
 374  he took up, and its light went out far, like the moon's.
 375  As when out at sea a light shows to sailors,
 376  the glare of a burning fire, that burns high in the mountains
 377  at a lonely steading — while the storm-winds carry them,
 378  unwilling, over the fish-filled sea away from their dear ones:
 379  so from Achilles' shield the light went up to the upper air,
 380  the beautiful, intricate shield. And lifting the heavy helmet
 381  he set it on his head; and it shone out like a star,
 382  the horse-haired helmet; and around it shook the plumes
 383  of gold that Hephaestus had set thick along the crest.
 384  And brilliant Achilles tried himself in the armor,[26]
 385  whether it fitted him, and his shining limbs ran free in it;
 386  and it became like wings to him, and lifted the shepherd of the people.
 387  And out of its case he drew his father's spear —
 388  heavy, huge, compact — no other of the Achaeans
 389  could swing it; Achilles alone knew how to swing it —
 390  the Pelian ash that Chiron gave his dear father
 391  from the peak of Pelion, to be death for heroes.
 392  And Automedon and Alcimus tended the horses[27]
 393  and yoked them: they put the fine breast-straps on, cast the bits
 394  into their jaws, and drew the reins back tight
 395  to the jointed chariot. And taking up the shining whip,
 396  fitted to his hand, Automedon sprang up
 397  behind the horses; and behind him, armed, mounted Achilles,
 398  blazing in his gear like Hyperion the shining sun,
 399  and terribly he called to the horses of his father:
 400  "Xanthus and Balius, far-famed children of Podarge:
 401  take thought, this time, to bring your charioteer back safe
 402  into the crowd of the Danaans, when we have had our fill of war —
 403  do not leave him there, like Patroclus, dead."
 404  Then from under the yoke the glancing-footed horse answered him —
 405  Xanthus. He dropped his head, suddenly, and all his mane,
 406  spilling from the yoke-pad by the yoke, came down and reached the ground;
 407  and the goddess white-armed Hera gave him voice:
 408  "This time, yes — we will bring you back safe, mighty Achilles.
 409  But the day of your destruction is near. And it is not we
 410  who are to blame, but a great god, and strong Fate.
 411  It was not by our slowness, not by any sloth of ours,
 412  that the Trojans stripped the armor from Patroclus's shoulders:
 413  the best of the gods, whom lovely-haired Leto bore,
 414  killed him among the front-fighters, and gave the glory to Hector.
 415  We two could run with the very breath of the West Wind,
 416  which men say is the lightest thing that flies; but for you yourself
 417  it is fated to be broken by force — by a god, and by a man."
 418  When he had spoken so, the Erinyes stopped his voice.
 419  Then, deeply troubled, swift-footed Achilles answered him:
 420  "Xanthus, why prophesy my death? You need not.
 421  I know it well myself: it is my portion to die here,
 422  far from my dear father and my mother. Even so,
 423  I will not stop before I have driven the Trojans to their fill of war."
 424  He spoke; and shouting he held his single-hoofed horses in the front rank.

NOTES

[1] (19.1-7) Dawn "in her saffron robe" (κροκόπεπλος, the
    register's fixed rendering) rises from Ocean to light a day
    that will end with Hector dead; line 2 repeats 11.2 exactly
    (our English kept), binding this dawn to the one that opened
    the poem's longest day. Thetis arrives to find her son not
    waiting for the armor but "lying over Patroclus" — the
    delivery of the most beautiful object in literature
    interrupts an embrace of a corpse.

[2] (19.8-11) Thetis's consolation is the poem's hardest kind:
    "we must let this man lie, for all our grief, since by the
    will of the gods, first of all, he was broken" — the same
    verb-frame Achilles used of the quarrel at 18.112. Grief is
    to be set down the way a settled thing is set down. Then the
    gift: armor "such as no man has ever worn on his shoulders"
    — literally true; it was forged for one wearing.

[3] (19.12-18) The armor "clashed aloud" (ἀνέβραχε) — its first
    act is a war-sound — and the Myrmidons cannot look at it:
    Hephaestus promised that "any man of the many who see it
    hereafter will marvel" (18.466-467), and the marvel arrives
    as terror. Only Achilles can face it, and the poem's
    psychology is exact: "the more he looked, the deeper the
    anger sank in him." Beauty does not soothe him; it fuels
    him. His eyes blaze "like flame-glare" — the fire Athena
    lit over his head at the ditch (18.206-227) has moved
    inside.

[4] (19.21-39) Achilles' first thought after accepting the arms
    is the flies. The realism is unblinking — "flies will enter
    by the bronze-cut wounds and breed worms... and all the
    flesh will rot" — and the divine answer matches it:
    ambrosia and red nectar distilled through the nostrils, the
    body kept "a year entire... sound as it is, or better."
    Embalming, in the poem's idiom: the gods' food, which makes
    the immortals deathless, here holds one mortal body just
    short of decay. (Line 39's placement was doubted by some
    ancient editors; a few copies added an extra line after 38
    on Thetis's departure — the apparatus records it.)

[5] (19.40-53) The muster of everyone: even the helmsmen and
    the ships' bread-stewards — the poem's only census of the
    noncombatants — come to see Achilles reappear. The wounded
    kings arrive as casualties of Book 11: Diomedes and
    Odysseus "leaning on their spears," Agamemnon last, still
    carrying Coön's spear-wound (11.251-253). The assembly that
    ends the quarrel physically displays what the quarrel cost.

[6] (19.56-64) The renunciation begins with a wish that undoes
    the poem: better Artemis had shot Briseis "on the day I
    chose her from the sack of Lyrnessus" — the prize whose
    taking launched the wrath now wished dead by the man who
    fought for her honor... or rather for his. The accounting
    is pitiless: "For Hector and the Trojans, the gain." Briseis
    herself will be standing in the assembly within two hundred
    lines; the poem lets the wish and the woman meet.

[7] (19.65-75) The formal close of the wrath: 65-66 repeat
    18.112-113 verbatim (our English kept) — the renunciation
    rehearsed to his mother now performed in public. The
    narrator seals it with the poem's own thematic word:
    "the great-hearted son of Peleus renounced the wrath"
    (μῆνιν ἀπειπόντος) — the μῆνις of line 1.1, formally
    discharged. What replaces it is not peace: it is a purer
    anger with a different object.

[8] (19.76-77) The strangest stage-direction in the poem:
    Agamemnon speaks "from the place where he sat, not standing
    up in their midst." Ancient editors were so unsettled that
    Zenodotus's text (with the Massaliot and Chian editions)
    substituted a standing formula — "rising up among them
    spoke the wide-ruling Agamemnon" — and the Massaliot text
    added a line about him "groaning over his wound." The
    vulgate's seated king is better: whether he sits as an
    invalid, or to avoid the exposed stance of a formal orator
    (his next lines fret about hecklers), or because he cannot
    bring himself to perform deference standing, the poem's
    great authority-figure ends the quarrel without ever
    getting to his feet.

[9] (19.78-94) The apology that is not one. After a nervous
    exordium on heckling ("even a clear-voiced speaker is
    defeated"), the thesis: "I am not to blame: Zeus is, and
    Fate, and the Erinys who walks in mist." The word rendered
    "blindness" is ἄτη — the divinely-sent infatuation that
    makes disaster feel reasonable. This register keeps
    "Blindness" from Phoenix's allegory (9.502-512), where she
    is "strong and sound of foot" and outruns the limping
    Prayers; here her feet are "soft: not on the ground does
    she walk, but over the heads of men." Two portraits, one
    goddess: fast as rumor, soft as a thought. Aristarchus
    athetized line 94 as an over-explaining tag. Line 77's
    opening ("when a man stands to speak...") was read by some
    ancients as a rebuke of Achilles' brevity; more likely it
    is anxiety dressed as etiquette.

[10] (19.95-133) The parable of Ate: even Zeus was blinded —
    on the day of Heracles' birth, when Hera extracted the
    unguarded oath, held back the Eileithyiai to delay Alcmene,
    and rushed Eurystheus into the light at seven months: the
    poem's most consequential act of obstetric sabotage,
    making the strongest man ever born the servant of the
    weakest king. Zeus's response founds the theology
    Agamemnon needs: he threw Blindness out of heaven by her
    "shining-braided head" — so she now lives here, "among
    the works of men." Delusion is a permanent resident of
    earth by divine eviction: whatever we wreck, we wreck in
    her company. The self-serving use is patent — if Zeus
    could be blinded, the king's surrender of judgment is
    company for gods — and the poem neither endorses nor
    mocks it; it lets Achilles accept the same doctrine at
    270-274.

[11] (19.134-144) The amends: 138 repeats 9.120 exactly ("I am
    willing to make amends, and to give ransom past counting")
    — Agamemnon's position has not moved one line in ten
    books; what has moved is everything around it. One ancient
    text (quoted by Athenaeus from Dioscurides) added a line
    after 137: "either drunk with wine, or the gods themselves
    hurt me" — a plus-line that makes the king blame the cup
    as well as the goddess; the vulgate rightly refuses it.

[12] (19.145-153) Achilles' answer prices the gifts at nothing:
    "furnish them if you wish, as is proper, or keep them with
    you." The catalogue that Book 9 built across forty lines
    and Book 19 will parade through the assembly is waved off
    in a line and a half — the entire heroic economy of
    compensation dismissed by the man it was designed to
    persuade. What he wants now cannot be paid in tripods.

[13] (19.154-183) Odysseus's first meal-speech, and a small
    masterpiece of practical authority: "bread and wine — for
    that is fury, and that is valor." The physiology is exact
    (the limbs "grow heavy unawares"; the knees fail), and the
    procedure he lays out — gifts displayed in mid-assembly,
    the oath about Briseis' bed sworn standing (176-177 reuse
    the wording of 9.133-134), the feast of appeasement, so
    Achilles "may lack nothing of your due" (δίκης — of
    justice) — is a complete protocol for ending a feud, dikē
    made procedural. His closing sentence dares what no one
    else has: "you, son of Atreus, will be more just toward
    another man hereafter" — the king corrected to his face,
    inside a compliment.

[14] (19.184-197) Agamemnon accepts everything, gladly — the
    management of the reconciliation has passed entirely to
    Odysseus ("to you yourself I give this charge"). The king's
    one initiative is liturgical: Talthybius and the boar for
    Zeus and the Sun.

[15] (19.198-214) Achilles' refusal of food is the book's
    center of gravity. His counter-proposal — fight fasting,
    feast at sundown "once we have paid off the outrage" —
    makes eating itself a thing that must be earned by
    vengeance; and the personal vow beneath it is absolute:
    "down my own throat, not a thing shall pass." The speech
    ends on the book's most famous line, the fast's true diet:
    "but slaughter, and blood, and the hard groaning of men."
    Grief has reorganized appetite itself.

[16] (19.215-237) Odysseus's second speech is the Iliad's
    ethics of endurance. The opening claim of eldership
    (216 = 16.21, our English kept — the vocative Patroclus
    once used to beg) licenses the great agricultural figure:
    battle as the harvest "where the bronze strews the most
    straw on the ground and the crop is smallest, once Zeus
    tilts his scales" — maximum cutting, minimum grain; and
    224 = 4.84, "Zeus, the dispenser of war to men" (our
    English kept). Then the doctrine: "With the belly the
    Achaeans cannot mourn a corpse... we must bury the man who
    dies, keeping a pitiless heart, weeping for one day."
    An army that loses men daily must ration grief like food —
    the survivor's discipline stated once for the whole
    tradition. Achilles is not persuaded; he is overruled by
    everyone else's hunger.

[17] (19.238-248) The gifts pass in review, matching the Book 9
    pledge item for item (9.122-130, renderings kept): seven
    tripods, twenty cauldrons, twelve horses, seven women of
    Lesbos — "and the eighth was fair-cheeked Briseis." The
    woman the poem began over enters as the last item in an
    inventory, between the women and the gold. Lycomedes and
    Melanippus and the young nobles carry the treasure; nobody
    in the text so much as looks at it.

[18] (19.249-268) The oath-rite, in exact ritual order: the
    herald holding the boar; the knife kept beside the sword's
    scabbard (a sacrificial implement carried into battle); the
    first-cut hairs; the invocation of Zeus, Earth, Sun, and
    the Erinyes "who under the earth exact payment from men" —
    the oath-police of the cosmos; the oath itself ("I never
    laid a hand on the girl Briseis" — confirming 9.133 was
    true when sworn); and the disposal: the boar whirled into
    the sea, "food for fish." An oath-victim is not eaten —
    it has absorbed the conditional curse — so the carcass
    goes to the element that swallows everything.

[19] (19.270-275) Achilles' response accepts Agamemnon's
    theology wholesale: "Father Zeus — great is the blindness
    you deal to men!" — the wronged man agreeing that Zeus,
    not the king, "willed that death should come to many
    Achaeans." The quarrel thus closes with both parties
    blaming heaven: a settlement, not a reckoning — and the
    poem quietly files it against Zeus's own protest in the
    Odyssey (1.32-34) that mortals blame the gods for what
    their own recklessness earns. The last line is pure
    Achilles: dismissal, meal, war — "so we may join the
    war-god together."

[20] (19.282-302) Briseis's only speech in the poem — the
    prize speaks. Her lament gives her, at last, a biography:
    husband cut down before the city, three full brothers dead
    in one day, and the man who did it — Achilles — the man
    whose wife Patroclus promised she would become, "with the
    marriage-feast among the Myrmidons." Her grief for
    Patroclus is grief for the one person who converted her
    catastrophe into a future; "You were gentle, always"
    (μείλιχον αἰεί) is the poem's whole portrait of the dead
    man in three words. Then the narrator's terrible widening:
    the captive women wail with her "for Patroclus in show;
    but each for her own sorrows" — every slave in the camp
    mourning her own dead under cover of the master's. Two
    lines that contain the entire suppressed grief of the
    war's losers.

[21] (19.303-313) The comforters: both Atreidae, Odysseus,
    Nestor, Idomeneus, Phoenix — the embassy of Book 9
    enlarged — around a man who again refuses what they offer;
    but where Book 9's refusal was rage, this is grief with a
    deadline: "Until the sun goes down I will hold out." He
    cannot be comforted "before he entered the mouth of bloody
    war" — battle as the only door out of mourning.

[22] (19.314-337) The lament for a lost future. It begins at
    the table — Patroclus remembered not in arms but serving
    dinner "quickly and deftly": intimacy measured in
    housework — and opens into the poem's geography of
    bereavement: Peleus in Phthia weeping for a son who will
    not return, "while I, in a strange country, fight the
    Trojans for blood-chilling Helen's sake"; and on Scyros
    the son, Neoptolemus, named only here in the Iliad —
    "if he still lives" — whom Patroclus was to fetch home
    and show "my possessions, my slaves, my great high-roofed
    house." Aristophanes and Aristarchus athetized line 327
    (the Neoptolemus line); it should stand: the dead man's
    assignment was to be the bridge between Achilles' father
    and Achilles' son, and with him dies the family's last
    connected future. Achilles imagined one exemption — "that
    I alone would die" — and Troy has refused even that.

[23] (19.338-356) Zeus's pity and the strangest feeding in the
    poem: Athena, falcon-swift, distills nectar and ambrosia
    into the chest of a man who must not know he is being fed
    — the fast kept and the body sustained, both at once. The
    gods honor his vow by cheating it. The elders' tears
    ("each remembering what he had left behind in his own
    halls") make Achilles' singular grief, for one moment, the
    army's common one.

[24] (19.357-364) The arming of the host as weather: helmets
    thick as snowflakes "under the blast of Boreas born in the
    clear sky" — cold light, innumerable, wind-driven — "and
    all the earth around laughed at the lightning-flash of
    bronze." The laughing earth (γέλασσε... χθών) is the
    poem's boldest pathetic figure: the world itself amused,
    or gleaming — the Greek means both — at what is coming.

[25] (19.365-383) The fourth and last full arming scene of the
    poem (Paris 3.330-338; Agamemnon 11.17-45; Patroclus
    16.131-139; now this), each item in the canonical order —
    greaves, corslet, sword, shield, helmet — and everything
    scaled up: teeth grinding, eyes as flame-glare, the
    shield's light "like the moon's." The great simile turns
    Book 18 inside out: there, beacon-fires promised rescue to
    a besieged island; here the mountain fire is seen by
    sailors "storm-driven, unwilling, over the fish-filled sea
    away from their dear ones" — light as pure distance, home
    visible and unreachable. It is the truest emblem in the
    poem of what Achilles' return actually is: he shines, and
    he is not coming home.

[26] (19.384-391) The test: the armor fits so perfectly it
    "became like wings to him, and lifted the shepherd of the
    people" — Hephaestus's craft answering the body it was
    made for. Then the one weapon the god did not make: the
    Pelian ash of Chiron, which "no other of the Achaeans
    could swing" — 388-391 repeat 16.141-144 verbatim (our
    English kept), where Patroclus, taking everything else,
    left precisely this spear behind. Aristarchus athetized
    the lines here as an importation ("they were not even in
    the other editions"); kept, they complete the Book 16
    figure: the borrowed armor is gone with the borrower, and
    what remains, inimitable, is the father's spear — the one
    piece of Achilles no one else could carry.

[27] (19.392-424) The ending no other book has. Achilles
    charges his horses to bring their driver back — "do not
    leave him there, like Patroclus, dead" — and Xanthus
    answers: Hera gives him voice (line 407, athetized by
    Aristarchus), and the horse first bows till his mane pours
    from the yoke-pad to the ground: the animal's grief-gesture
    the poem showed at 17.426-440, the weeping horses, now
    given language. His speech is exact prophecy: not our
    slowness but "the best of the gods" killed Patroclus; "we
    two could run with the very breath of the West Wind" —
    their sire (16.150) — "but for you yourself it is fated to
    be broken by force — by a god, and by a man" (Apollo and
    Paris, 22.359-360, though Aristarchus athetized 416-417).
    Then "the Erinyes stopped his voice" — the same powers
    invoked at 259 as oath-police: guardians of the order of
    things, for whom a speaking horse is a breach to be
    closed. Achilles' reply is the book in miniature: "I know
    it well myself." Foreknowledge changes nothing; that is
    what makes him Achilles. The book ends with the shout and
    the horses held "in the front rank" — the wrath's second
    life, pointed at Troy at last.
