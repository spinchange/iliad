BOOK 10

The Doloneia: both armies send spies into the same night — Dolon dies
talking, and Odysseus and Diomedes come home laughing, with the horses
of Rhesus.

TRANSLATOR'S COMMENTARY

Book 10 is the Iliad's night. Nothing in it happens by daylight rules:
the arming is in animal skins, the omens are heard rather than seen,
the fighting is throat-cutting among sleeping men, and the one killing
that follows a supplication does not even wait for the suppliant to
finish his sentence. It is also the only book of the Iliad whose right
to be in the poem has been disputed since antiquity (curated dispute
#8): a scholion reports that "this rhapsody was arranged separately by
Homer and not counted part of the Iliad, but was placed into the poem
by Pisistratus." Aristarchus kept it; Analysts have excised it; its
defenders answer that the ambush (the lochos) is an ancient heroic
theme with its own conventions — leaders' fear, borrowed gear, beast-
skins, two-man teamwork — which this book executes with complete
control, and that the poem's long day of Books 11-18 needs precisely
this restorative night between the Embassy's failure and disaster. The
internal evidence cuts both ways: the diction has an Odyssean cast
(the bathing-tubs of 576 and the whistle of 502 occur nowhere else in
the Iliad; Athena tells Diomedes to "think of your return"), and
nothing in the book — Rhesus, his marvelous horses, Dolon's
intelligence — is ever mentioned again in the poem. Our policy is
unchanged: the printed text is translated entire, and the argument
lives in the notes ([1], [39], [43]).

What the book is, read on its own terms, is a study in symmetry. Two
sleepless kings; two councils held in the dark, each seated in the
other's killing-ground (note [13]); two spies sent out in the same
hour with twinned volunteer-speeches (note [25]) — but one side sends
its best two men for a black ewe apiece, and the other sends its worst
man alone, for a promise of Achilles' horses that was never Hector's
to give. The interrogation runs on the courteous formulas of guest-
conversation with a corpse at the end of it (note [29]); the thirteenth
Thracian killed matches the "thirteenth" scout in Odysseus's tally
(note [42]); and the book ends with the poem's only laughter after
killing and its only hot bath — the night washed off before rosy-
fingered dawn restores the day-world's rules.

Register notes: the boar's-tusk helmet pedigree returns the loot of
Phoenix's father's house (note [19]); "when two go together" enters
the proverb-stock (note [15]); ἀλαοσκοπιή "no blind man's watch" is
fixed for Books 13-14; ἄτη "blindness" shrinks to fit Dolon (note
[30]); and 10.199 reuses our 8.491 verbatim, as the Greek does.

   1  The other captains of all the Achaeans by the ships[1]
   2  slept the whole night through, mastered by soft sleep;[2]
   3  but not Atreus' son Agamemnon, shepherd of the people —
   4  sweet sleep did not hold him, his mind churning many things.
   5  As when the husband of lovely-haired Hera flashes lightning,
   6  working a huge rain beyond all telling, or hail,
   7  or a snowstorm, when snow powders over the plowlands,
   8  or somewhere the great mouth of bitter war,[3]
   9  so thick and fast came the groans of Agamemnon in his chest,
  10  from the bottom of his heart; and his mind shook within him.
  11  Whenever he looked out toward the Trojan plain,
  12  he marveled at the many fires burning before Ilion,
  13  at the voice of flutes and pipes, and the din of men;[4]
  14  but whenever he looked at the ships and the Achaean host,
  15  he tore the hair from his head out by the roots
  16  before Zeus on high, and his glorious heart groaned greatly.
  17  And this to his mind seemed the best counsel:
  18  to go first of all men to Nestor, Neleus' son,
  19  and see whether with him he might build some blameless plan
  20  that would prove a warder-off of evil for all the Danaans.
  21  He rose upright, and put his tunic on about his chest,
  22  and beneath his shining feet he bound fair sandals,
  23  and after that wrapped round him the blood-red hide of a lion,
  24  fiery, huge, reaching his feet; and he took up a spear.
  25  In the same way trembling held Menelaus; on his eyelids
  26  either, sleep would not settle: for fear something befall
  27  the Argives, who for his sake across so much water
  28  had come to Troy, nursing their audacious war.
  29  First he covered his broad back with the skin of a leopard,[5]
  30  dappled; and then he lifted a rimmed helmet of bronze
  31  and set it on his head, and took a spear in his thick hand.
  32  Then he went to rouse his brother, who ruled with might
  33  over all the Argives, honored by the people like a god.
  34  He found him putting his fine armor about his shoulders
  35  by the stern of his ship; and his coming was welcome to him.
  36  Menelaus, good at the war-cry, spoke to him first:
  37  "Elder brother, why arm yourself so? Is it to rouse
  38  some companion to spy upon the Trojans? I am afraid,
  39  badly afraid, that no one will undertake that work for you —
  40  to go out alone against the enemy men, and scout them,
  41  through the ambrosial night. It will need a very bold heart."
  42  Answering him spoke the lord Agamemnon:
  43  "There is need of counsel, Zeus-nurtured Menelaus, for me
  44  and for you — cunning counsel, that will protect and save
  45  the Argives and the ships; for the mind of Zeus has turned:
  46  his heart is set now rather on Hector's sacrifices.
  47  For never have I seen, nor heard a man tell of it,
  48  one man contriving so much havoc in one day
  49  as Hector, dear to Zeus, has done against the sons of the Achaeans,
  50  unaided — no dear son of a goddess or a god.
  51  He has done deeds that I tell you will be the Argives' concern
  52  long and lastingly — such evils he has devised against the Achaeans.[6]
  53  But go now, run beside the ships, and call
  54  Ajax and Idomeneus; and I will go to brilliant Nestor
  55  and urge him to rise — to see if he is willing
  56  to go to the sacred company of the guards, and give the orders.
  57  They would obey him soonest, for his own son
  58  is a commander of the guards, with Idomeneus' aide
  59  Meriones; to them above all others we gave the charge."
  60  Then answered him Menelaus, good at the war-cry:
  61  "How do you mean this word of command you give me?
  62  Do I stay there with them, waiting until you come,
  63  or run after you again, once I have delivered your orders?"
  64  Then the lord of men Agamemnon answered him:
  65  "Stay there, so that we do not miss one another
  66  as we come and go; there are many paths through the camp.
  67  Call out, wherever you go, and bid them stay awake,
  68  naming each man from his father, by his lineage,[7]
  69  giving every man his honor. Do not be lofty in spirit;
  70  no, let us do the labor ourselves, we too. This must be
  71  the heavy misery Zeus laid on us at our very birth."
  72  So speaking, he sent his brother off with clear instructions,
  73  and set out himself for Nestor, shepherd of the people.
  74  He found him beside his hut and his black ship,
  75  in a soft bed; beside him lay his intricate gear —
  76  a shield, and two spears, and a shining helmet;
  77  beside him lay the glittering war-belt the old man girded on
  78  whenever he armed himself for man-destroying war
  79  and led out his people; he made no concession to grim old age.
  80  He raised himself up on his elbow, lifted his head,
  81  and spoke to Atreus' son, questioning him with a word:
  82  "Who are you, walking alone through the camp among the ships
  83  in the murky night, when other mortals are sleeping?
  84  Seeking one of your mules, or one of your companions?[8]
  85  Speak — do not come at me silent. What is your need?"
  86  Then answered him the lord of men Agamemnon:
  87  "Nestor, Neleus' son, great glory of the Achaeans,
  88  you will know Atreus' son Agamemnon — the man whom Zeus
  89  beyond all men has plunged into toils without end, while breath
  90  remains in my chest and my own knees keep their spring.
  91  I wander so because sweet sleep will not settle on my eyes;
  92  the war is my care, and the sufferings of the Achaeans.
  93  I fear terribly for the Danaans. My heart is not
  94  steady; I am storm-tossed; the heart leaps out of my chest,
  95  and my shining limbs tremble beneath me.
  96  But if you will bestir yourself — since sleep has not come to you either —
  97  come, let us go down to the guards, and look:
  98  for fear that, sodden with weariness and with sleep,
  99  they have dropped off, and forgotten the watch entirely.
 100  Enemy men are camped close by, and we know nothing:
 101  they might even be minded to fight us in the night."
 102  Then answered him the Gerenian horseman Nestor:
 103  "Most glorious son of Atreus, lord of men Agamemnon,
 104  surely not all of Hector's designs will Zeus the counselor
 105  accomplish for him — all he now hopes. I think instead
 106  he will sweat under troubles even more than ours, if once Achilles
 107  turns his own heart back from its galling anger.
 108  And I will follow you, gladly. But let us wake others as well:
 109  Tydeus' son, famed with the spear, and Odysseus,
 110  and swift Ajax, and the stout son of Phyleus.[9]
 111  And if someone would also go and summon the rest —
 112  godlike Ajax, and the lord Idomeneus,
 113  whose ships are the farthest off of all, nowhere near us.
 114  But Menelaus — dear though he is to me, and respected —
 115  I will fault him, though you may resent me; I will not hide it:[10]
 116  he sleeps, and has left the labor to you alone.
 117  Now is when he should be laboring among all the captains,
 118  pleading with them; the need that has come will not be borne."
 119  Then the lord of men Agamemnon answered him:
 120  "Old man, another day I would even bid you blame him;
 121  he does often hang back, and is unwilling to labor —
 122  not that he yields to sloth or thoughtlessness of mind,
 123  but he looks to me, and waits for my lead.
 124  This time he woke well before me, and came and stood over me,
 125  and I sent him ahead to summon the very men you ask for.
 126  Let us go: we shall overtake them before the gates,
 127  among the guards; for that is where I told them to gather."
 128  Then answered him the Gerenian horseman Nestor:
 129  "Then no man of the Argives will resent him
 130  or disobey, when he stirs a man on and gives commands."
 131  So speaking, he put his tunic on about his chest,
 132  and beneath his shining feet he bound fair sandals,
 133  and pinned about himself a purple-red cloak,
 134  double-folded, full-falling, thick with a nap of wool.
 135  And he took up a stout spear, edged with sharp bronze,
 136  and went along the ships of the bronze-shirted Achaeans.
 137  First it was Odysseus, the equal of Zeus in counsel,
 138  that the Gerenian horseman Nestor woke from sleep,
 139  calling out; and the voice came quickly about his senses,
 140  and he came out of the hut, and spoke to them a word:
 141  "Why do you wander alone like this by the ships, through the camp,
 142  in the ambrosial night — what need is so great upon you?"
 143  Then answered him the Gerenian horseman Nestor:
 144  "Zeus-sprung son of Laertes, Odysseus of many devices,
 145  do not be angry: such grief has overmastered the Achaeans.
 146  Come with us, so we may wake the next man too, one fit
 147  to take counsel together, whether to run or fight."
 148  So he spoke; and resourceful Odysseus, going into the hut,
 149  put his figured shield about his shoulders, and went with them.
 150  They went on to Tydeus' son Diomedes, and came upon him
 151  outside his hut with his weapons; and around him his companions
 152  slept with their shields beneath their heads; their spears
 153  stood driven upright on their butt-spikes, and far off the bronze
 154  flashed like the lightning of father Zeus. The hero himself
 155  slept, the hide of a field-ox spread underneath him
 156  and a bright carpet stretched beneath his head.
 157  The Gerenian horseman Nestor stood by him and woke him,
 158  stirring him with a kick of his heel, and scolded him to his face:
 159  "Wake, son of Tydeus! Why doze away the whole night's sleep?[11]
 160  Do you not hear how the Trojans, on the swell of the plain,
 161  sit close to the ships, and only a little ground still holds them off?"
 162  So he spoke; and Diomedes started sharply out of sleep,
 163  and speaking winged words he addressed him:
 164  "You are a hard one, old man. You never cease from labor.
 165  Are there not other, younger sons of the Achaeans,
 166  who might go round and wake each of the kings in turn,
 167  walking everywhere? You are impossible, old man."
 168  Then answered him the Gerenian horseman Nestor:
 169  "Yes, friend — all this you have said rightly and in due measure.
 170  I have blameless sons of my own, and I have people,
 171  plenty of them; one of these might go around and call.
 172  But a very great need has overmastered the Achaeans:
 173  now for all of us it stands upon the razor's edge —[12]
 174  either grim destruction for the Achaeans, or life.
 175  Go then and rouse swift Ajax and the son of Phyleus —
 176  you are the younger — if you have pity on me."
 177  So he spoke; and round his shoulders he wrapped the hide of a lion,
 178  fiery, huge, reaching his feet; and he took up a spear.
 179  And the hero went, woke those men, and brought them from their places.
 180  And when they came among the assembled guards,
 181  they did not find the leaders of the watch asleep:
 182  all of them sat wide awake with their weapons.
 183  As dogs around sheep in a fold keep painful watch,
 184  hearing a hard-hearted beast that through the timber
 185  comes down among the mountains, and much clamor rises
 186  about it from men and dogs, and their sleep is destroyed,
 187  so sweet sleep was destroyed from their eyelids
 188  as they kept watch through the evil night; toward the plain
 189  they were turned always, listening for the Trojans coming.
 190  The old man rejoiced to see them, and heartened them with a word,
 191  and speaking winged words he addressed them:
 192  "Keep your watch like that, dear children. Let no one
 193  be taken by sleep — or we become a joy to our enemies."
 194  So speaking he swept on across the ditch; and with him followed
 195  the kings of the Argives, all who had been summoned to council.
 196  With them went Meriones and Nestor's shining son,
 197  for the kings themselves had called them in to share the counsels.
 198  They crossed the deep-dug ditch and seated themselves
 199  in a clear space, where ground showed empty of the dead[13]
 200  who had fallen — there where massive Hector had turned back
 201  from destroying Argives, when night closed in around them.
 202  There they sat down and declared their words to one another,
 203  and the Gerenian horseman Nestor began to speak among them:
 204  "Friends, will no man put trust enough in his own
 205  daring heart to go out among the great-hearted
 206  Trojans? He might catch some straggler at the enemy's edge,
 207  or he might even gather some rumor among the Trojans —
 208  what they are counseling among themselves — whether they mean
 209  to stay where they are by the ships, far away, or will withdraw
 210  back to the city, since they have beaten down the Achaeans.
 211  All that he might learn, and come back to us again
 212  unharmed; and great would his fame be under heaven
 213  among all men, and a noble gift will be his:
 214  for as many of the best men as command the ships,
 215  of all of those, each single one will give him a black ewe,
 216  a female with lamb at teat — no possession like it —[14]
 217  and he will always be included at feasts and banquets."
 218  So he spoke, and they all went still in silence.
 219  And among them spoke Diomedes, good at the war-cry:
 220  "Nestor, my own heart and my proud spirit urge me
 221  to enter the camp of the enemy men who lie
 222  close by — the Trojans. But if some other man went with me,
 223  there would be more comfort in it, and more boldness too.
 224  When two go together, one marks before the other[15]
 225  where the advantage lies. A man alone may see it,
 226  but his wit is slower, and his cunning thin."
 227  So he spoke; and many were willing to follow Diomedes.
 228  The two Ajaxes were willing, those squires of Ares;
 229  Meriones was willing; most willing, Nestor's son;
 230  willing was Atreus' son Menelaus, famed with the spear;
 231  and willing was hardy Odysseus to slip into the throng
 232  of the Trojans — the heart in his chest was always daring.
 233  And among them spoke the lord of men Agamemnon:
 234  "Diomedes, Tydeus' son, delight of my heart,
 235  the companion — you shall choose him yourself, whichever you want,
 236  the best of those who offer themselves; many are eager.
 237  Do not, from deference in your heart, leave the better man
 238  behind, and take the worse to go with you,
 239  yielding to respect, eyeing birth — not even if he is the more kingly."
 240  So he spoke, and was afraid for tawny-haired Menelaus.[16]
 241  And among them spoke again Diomedes, good at the war-cry:
 242  "If you truly bid me choose my companion myself,
 243  how then could I forget godlike Odysseus,
 244  whose heart is forward and whose spirit proud
 245  in every kind of labor — and Pallas Athena loves him.
 246  With him following me, even out of blazing fire
 247  the two of us would come home; he knows beyond all how to think."
 248  Then answered him much-enduring brilliant Odysseus:
 249  "Tydeus' son, neither praise me overmuch nor blame me;
 250  these are things known to the Argives you say them among.
 251  Come, let us go. The night is far spent, and dawn is near;
 252  the stars have moved far on; and more of the night has passed
 253  than two of its parts, and only the third part is left."[17]
 254  So the two spoke, and got themselves into their fearsome gear.
 255  Thrasymedes, steadfast in battle, gave Tydeus' son
 256  a two-edged sword — for his own was left by his ship —
 257  and a shield; and about his head he set a helmet
 258  of bull's hide, ridgeless, crestless — the kind men call
 259  a skull-cap, and it guards the heads of strong young men.[18]
 260  And Meriones gave Odysseus a bow and a quiver
 261  and a sword; and about his head he set a helmet
 262  made of leather; and inside it was strung taut
 263  with many thongs; and outside the white teeth
 264  of a shining-tusked boar were set close along it, this way and that,
 265  well and with skill; and in the middle a cap of felt was fitted.
 266  This helmet Autolycus once stole out of Eleon[19]
 267  from Amyntor, Ormenus' son, breaking into his close-built house;
 268  he gave it at Scandeia to Amphidamas of Cythera;
 269  Amphidamas gave it to Molus, to be his guest-gift,
 270  and Molus gave it to his son Meriones to wear;
 271  and now it was set round the head of Odysseus, and covered it.
 272  And when the two had got themselves into their fearsome gear,
 273  they set out, and left all the best men there in their places.
 274  And close by their road Pallas Athena sent them
 275  a heron on the right; their eyes could not see it[20]
 276  through the murky night, but they heard it cry aloud.
 277  Odysseus rejoiced at the bird-sign, and prayed to Athena:
 278  "Hear me, child of aegis-bearing Zeus — you who always
 279  stand beside me in every labor, and I do not
 280  escape your eye when I move: now again love me best, Athena,
 281  and grant that we come back with glory to the ships,
 282  having done a great deed, one that will be the Trojans' concern."
 283  And second in turn prayed Diomedes, good at the war-cry:
 284  "Hear me now also, child of Zeus, Unwearied one.
 285  Go with me as once you went with my father, brilliant Tydeus,[21]
 286  into Thebes, when he went before the Achaeans as their envoy.
 287  He left the bronze-shirted Achaeans there at the Asopus,
 288  and himself carried a honeyed word to the Cadmeans
 289  there; but on the way back he devised deeds of sheer havoc
 290  with you, brilliant goddess, when you stood by him in your kindness.
 291  So now again be willing to stand by me, and guard me.
 292  And I in return will sacrifice to you a yearling heifer,
 293  broad-browed, unbroken, that no man has yet led under the yoke:
 294  that I will sacrifice to you, and sheathe her horns in gold."[22]
 295  So they spoke in prayer, and Pallas Athena heard them.
 296  And when they had prayed to the daughter of great Zeus,
 297  they went on their way like two lions through the black night,
 298  through the slaughter, through the corpses, through the gear and the black blood.[23]
 299  Nor did Hector allow the proud Trojans to sleep
 300  either; he called together all their best men,
 301  all who were leaders and lords of the Trojans;
 302  and calling them together he framed his shrewd plan:
 303  "Who will undertake a work for me and bring it through
 304  to the end, for a great gift? The wage will be enough:
 305  I will give a chariot and two strong-necked horses,
 306  the finest that there are beside the swift ships of the Achaeans,
 307  to the man who dares — and he will win glory for himself —
 308  to go close to the swift-faring ships, and find out
 309  whether the swift ships are guarded as before,
 310  or whether by now, beaten down under our hands,
 311  they are planning flight among themselves, and have no wish
 312  to keep watch through the night, sodden with brutal weariness."
 313  So he spoke, and they all went still in silence.
 314  Now there was among the Trojans one Dolon, the son of Eumedes[24]
 315  the sacred herald — rich in gold and rich in bronze —
 316  who was ugly to look at, but swift-footed;
 317  and he was an only son among five sisters.
 318  He then spoke his word to the Trojans and Hector:
 319  "Hector, my own heart and my proud spirit urge me[25]
 320  to go close to the swift-faring ships, and find out.
 321  Come then, lift up your scepter to me, and swear
 322  that you will give the horses, and the chariot intricate with bronze,
 323  that carry the blameless son of Peleus;
 324  and I will not be a vain scout for you, nor fall short of your thought.
 325  For I will go straight on through the camp until I come
 326  to Agamemnon's ship, where no doubt their best men
 327  are taking counsel together, whether to run or fight."
 328  So he spoke; and Hector took the scepter in his hand, and swore:
 329  "Let Zeus himself now witness it, the loud-thunderer, Hera's lord:
 330  no other man of the Trojans shall mount behind those horses;
 331  you and none other, I declare, shall glory in them forever."
 332  So he spoke, and swore it falsely — and set the man on fire.
 333  At once he slung his curved bow over his shoulders,
 334  and over it put on the hide of a grey wolf,
 335  and on his head a ferret-skin cap; and he took a sharp javelin,[26]
 336  and set out from the camp toward the ships — nor was he ever
 337  to come back from the ships and carry his word to Hector.
 338  But when he had left the crowds of horses and men behind,
 339  he went up the road, all eagerness; and Zeus-sprung Odysseus
 340  marked him coming on, and said to Diomedes:
 341  "Diomedes, here is some man coming out of the camp;
 342  I cannot tell if he means to spy upon our ships
 343  or to strip one of the corpses of the dead.
 344  Let us let him pass us by on the plain
 345  a little first; then rushing after we can take him
 346  quickly. And if he outruns the two of us on foot,
 347  keep crowding him always toward the ships, away from the camp,
 348  lunging with your spear, so he cannot slip off to the town."
 349  So the two spoke, and they lay down off the road
 350  among the corpses; and in his folly he ran quickly past.
 351  But when he was as far away as mules plow in one stretch —[27]
 352  for mules are better beasts than oxen
 353  at dragging the jointed plow through deep fallow land —
 354  the two ran after him; and he stopped still at the sound of feet,
 355  for he hoped in his heart they were companions coming
 356  from the Trojans to turn him back, Hector recalling him.
 357  But when they were a spear-throw off, or even less,
 358  he knew them for enemy men, and moved his nimble knees
 359  in flight; and they leapt quickly into the chase.
 360  As when two jag-toothed hounds that know the hunt
 361  press hard on a fawn or a hare, unrelenting, always,
 362  through wooded country, and it runs crying before them,
 363  so Tydeus' son and Odysseus, sacker of cities,
 364  cut him off from his people and chased him, unrelenting, always.
 365  But when he was on the point of mixing with the guards
 366  in his flight toward the ships, then Athena put fury
 367  into Tydeus' son, so that no bronze-shirted Achaean
 368  might boast that he struck first, with Diomedes coming second.
 369  Rushing at him with the spear, strong Diomedes spoke:
 370  "Stand — or I reach you with the spear; and I tell you,
 371  you will not long escape sheer destruction from my hand."
 372  He spoke, and let the spear fly — and missed the man on purpose:[28]
 373  over his right shoulder the point of the polished spear
 374  stuck fast in the earth. Dolon stood still, terrified,
 375  stammering — through his mouth came the chatter of his teeth —
 376  green with fear. Panting, the two caught up with him
 377  and seized his hands; and he burst into tears and said:
 378  "Take me alive, and I will ransom myself: at home there lies
 379  bronze, and gold, and iron worked with much labor;
 380  out of these my father would gladden you with ransom past counting
 381  if he learned I was alive by the ships of the Achaeans."
 382  Answering him spoke resourceful Odysseus:
 383  "Take heart; and let death be no concern of your mind.[29]
 384  But come, tell me this, and recount it truly:
 385  where are you going alone to the ships, away from the camp,
 386  in the murky night, when other mortals are sleeping?
 387  To strip one of the corpses of the dead?
 388  Or did Hector send you out to spy on everything
 389  at the hollow ships? Or did your own heart drive you?"
 390  Then Dolon answered him, his limbs trembling under him:
 391  "With many blindnesses Hector led my mind astray:[30]
 392  the whole-hoofed horses of the illustrious son of Peleus
 393  he promised on his nod to give, and the chariot intricate with bronze,
 394  and told me to go on through the swift black night,
 395  and get close to the enemy men, and find out
 396  whether the swift ships are guarded as before,
 397  or whether by now, beaten down under our hands,
 398  they are planning flight among themselves, and have no wish
 399  to keep watch through the night, sodden with brutal weariness."[31]
 400  And resourceful Odysseus answered him, smiling:
 401  "Great gifts indeed those were your heart was set on —
 402  the horses of the war-minded son of Aeacus. They are hard
 403  for mortal men to break or to drive,
 404  for any man but Achilles, whom an immortal mother bore.
 405  But come, tell me this, and recount it truly:
 406  where, when you came here, did you leave Hector, shepherd of the people?
 407  Where does his warlike gear lie? Where are his horses?
 408  And the other Trojans — how are their watches and sleeping-places?
 409  What are they counseling among themselves — whether they mean
 410  to stay where they are by the ships, far away, or will withdraw
 411  back to the city, since they have beaten down the Achaeans?"
 412  Then answered him Dolon, Eumedes' son:
 413  "Well then, I will recount all this to you very truly.
 414  Hector, with all those men who are counselors,
 415  is taking counsel by the tomb of godlike Ilus,[32]
 416  apart from the din. But the watches you ask about, hero —
 417  no picked guard protects the camp, and none keeps watch.
 418  As for the Trojans' watch-fires: the men under necessity,
 419  they stay awake beside them, and call to one another
 420  to keep the watch. But the allies, called from many lands,
 421  are sleeping; they leave the watching to the Trojans,
 422  for their own children and wives do not lie near them."
 423  Answering him spoke resourceful Odysseus:
 424  "Now how is it — do they sleep mingled with the horse-taming
 425  Trojans, or apart? Tell me plainly, so I may know."
 426  Then answered him Dolon, Eumedes' son:
 427  "Well then, I will recount this too to you very truly.
 428  Toward the sea lie the Carians and the Paeonians of the curved bows,[33]
 429  the Leleges and the Caucones and the brilliant Pelasgians;
 430  toward Thymbre fell the lot of the Lycians and the lordly Mysians
 431  and horse-fighting Phrygians, and Maeonians, men who fight from chariots.
 432  But why do you question me through all this, point by point?
 433  If you two are bent on slipping into the Trojans' throng,
 434  here, apart, are the Thracians, new-come, outermost of all,
 435  and among them their king Rhesus, son of Eïoneus.
 436  His horses are the finest I have ever seen, and the biggest:
 437  whiter than snow, and like the winds in running.
 438  His chariot is finely worked with gold and silver,
 439  and the armor he came in is golden, gigantic, a wonder[34]
 440  to look at — armor that suits, not mortal men
 441  to carry, but the deathless gods.
 442  But now, take me with you to the swift-faring ships,
 443  or bind me with a pitiless bond and leave me here,
 444  until you two have gone and made your trial of me,
 445  whether I have spoken to you justly, or not."
 446  Glaring at him darkly, strong Diomedes spoke:
 447  "Do not put escape into your heart, Dolon,
 448  good though your news is, now you have come into our hands.
 449  For if we free you now for ransom, or let you go,
 450  surely later you will come again to the swift ships of the Achaeans,
 451  either spying once more, or fighting us face to face;
 452  but if, beaten down under my hands, you lose your life,
 453  never again will you be a plague upon the Argives."
 454  He spoke; and the man was reaching for his chin with his broad hand,
 455  meaning to plead — but the other drove at the middle of his neck,
 456  springing with the sword, and sheared through both the tendons;
 457  and the head of the still-speaking man was mingled with the dust.[35]
 458  From him they took the cap of ferret skin,
 459  the grey wolf's hide, the back-bent bow, and the long spear;
 460  and these to Athena of the Spoils brilliant Odysseus[36]
 461  held up high in his hand, and praying spoke a word:
 462  "Rejoice in these, goddess. To you first of all the immortals
 463  on Olympus we will give your share. But once again
 464  send us on, to the Thracian men's horses and sleeping-places."
 465  So he spoke, and lifted the spoils high away from himself
 466  and set them on a tamarisk; and he set a mark plain to see,
 467  pulling together reeds and the tamarisk's flourishing boughs,
 468  so they would not miss it, coming back through the swift black night.
 469  The two went forward, through the gear and the black blood,
 470  and quickly, pressing on, they reached the company of Thracian men.
 471  They were sleeping, sodden with weariness, and their fine gear
 472  lay by them on the ground, all in good order,
 473  in three rows; and beside each man his yoke-pair of horses.
 474  Rhesus slept in the middle, and beside him his swift horses
 475  were tethered by the reins to the chariot's outer rail.
 476  Odysseus saw him first, and pointed him out to Diomedes:
 477  "That is the man, Diomedes; and those are the horses
 478  that Dolon marked out for us — the man we killed.
 479  Come then, show your great strength. You have no business
 480  standing idle in your armor. Untie the horses —
 481  or else you kill the men, and the horses will be my care."
 482  So he spoke; and grey-eyed Athena breathed fury into him.
 483  He killed, wheeling on every side, and a hideous groaning rose
 484  from men struck by the sword; and the earth reddened with blood.
 485  As a lion coming upon flocks without their herdsman,
 486  on goats or on sheep, springs in with evil purpose,
 487  so Tydeus' son moved up and down among the Thracian men
 488  until he had killed twelve. And resourceful Odysseus —
 489  whomever Tydeus' son struck with the sword, standing above him,
 490  him Odysseus would seize by the foot from behind and drag aside,
 491  with this thought in his mind: that the fair-maned horses
 492  might pass through easily, and not shiver in their hearts
 493  at stepping on dead men — being still unused to them.
 494  But when Tydeus' son came upon the king,
 495  he was the thirteenth he robbed of his honey-sweet life,
 496  gasping hard: for an evil dream stood at his head
 497  that night — the son of Oeneus' son — by the cunning of Athena.[37]
 498  All that time hardy Odysseus was untying the whole-hoofed horses;
 499  he bound them together with reins and drove them from the crowd,
 500  beating them with his bow, because he had not thought[38]
 501  to take up in his hands the shining whip from the intricate chariot;
 502  then he gave a whistle, his signal to brilliant Diomedes.
 503  But Diomedes waited, weighing what boldest thing he might do:
 504  whether to seize the chariot where the intricate gear lay
 505  and drag it out by the pole, or heave it high and carry it,
 506  or take away the life of still more Thracians.
 507  While he was turning this over in his mind, Athena
 508  came near and stood, and spoke to brilliant Diomedes:
 509  "Think of your return now, son of great-hearted Tydeus,[39]
 510  back to the hollow ships — or you may go back to them running,
 511  and some other god may waken the Trojans too."
 512  So she spoke; and he knew the voice of the goddess speaking,
 513  and swiftly mounted a horse; and Odysseus struck the pair
 514  with his bow, and they flew toward the swift ships of the Achaeans.
 515  But Apollo of the silver bow kept no blind man's watch[40]
 516  when he saw Athena attending on Tydeus' son:
 517  angry with her, he sank into the great throng of Trojans
 518  and woke a counselor of the Thracians, Hippocoon,
 519  Rhesus' noble kinsman. And he leapt up out of sleep,
 520  and when he saw the place empty where the swift horses had stood,
 521  and the men gasping amid the grim slaughter,
 522  he groaned aloud, and called out the name of his dear companion.
 523  And a clamor rose from the Trojans, and measureless uproar,
 524  as they rushed together; they gazed at the deeds of havoc —
 525  all that the men had done before going to the hollow ships.
 526  And when the two came where they had killed Hector's scout,
 527  there Odysseus, dear to Zeus, held in the swift horses,
 528  and Tydeus' son leapt down, and put the bloody spoils
 529  into Odysseus' hands, and mounted up again;
 530  and he whipped the horses on, and the two flew, nothing loath,
 531  toward the hollow ships; for that way was dear to their hearts.
 532  Nestor was the first to hear the drumming of hooves, and spoke:
 533  "Friends, leaders and lords of the Argives,
 534  shall I be wrong, or right, in what I say? My heart commands it.[41]
 535  The drumming of swift-footed horses strikes about my ears.
 536  If only it were Odysseus and strong Diomedes
 537  driving whole-hoofed horses here, so quickly, out of the Trojans!
 538  But terribly I fear in my heart that they have come to harm —
 539  the best of the Argives — in the uproar of the Trojans."
 540  The whole word was not yet spoken when they came themselves,
 541  and stepped down to the ground; and the others, rejoicing,
 542  welcomed them with clasped right hands and honeyed words.
 543  And first to question them was the Gerenian horseman Nestor:
 544  "Come, tell me, Odysseus of the many tales, great glory of the Achaeans:
 545  how did you two take these horses? By slipping into the throng
 546  of the Trojans? Or did some god meet you, and give them?
 547  They are terribly like the rays of the sun.
 548  Always I mix with the Trojans; I say I am no lingerer
 549  beside the ships, old warrior though I am;
 550  but never yet have I seen such horses, or dreamed of them.
 551  No — I think some god must have met you and given them,
 552  for Zeus the cloud-gatherer loves the two of you,
 553  and the daughter of aegis-bearing Zeus, grey-eyed Athena."
 554  Answering him spoke resourceful Odysseus:
 555  "Nestor, Neleus' son, great glory of the Achaeans,
 556  easily could a god, if he wished, give better horses
 557  even than these, since the gods are far mightier.
 558  But these horses you ask about, old man, are newly come —
 559  Thracians; their lord — brave Diomedes killed him,
 560  and beside him twelve companions, all their best men.
 561  The thirteenth was a scout we took near the ships,[42]
 562  a man sent out to be a spy upon our army
 563  by Hector and the rest of the illustrious Trojans."
 564  So speaking, he drove the whole-hoofed horses across the ditch,
 565  laughing aloud; and the other Achaeans followed, rejoicing.
 566  And when they reached the well-built hut of Tydeus' son,
 567  they tied up the horses with well-cut thongs
 568  at the horse-manger, where the horses of Diomedes
 569  stood, swift-footed, eating their honey-sweet grain.
 570  And on his ship's stern Odysseus laid the bloody spoils
 571  of Dolon, till they could make the offering ready for Athena.
 572  And the men themselves waded into the sea, and washed away
 573  the heavy sweat from their shins and necks and thighs.
 574  And when the waves of the sea had washed the heavy sweat
 575  from their skin, and the dear heart in them was cooled,
 576  they stepped into polished bathing-tubs and bathed.[43]
 577  And when the two had bathed and rubbed themselves sleek with oil,
 578  they sat down to their meal, and from a brimming mixing-bowl
 579  they drew honey-sweet wine and poured it out to Athena.

NOTES

[1] (10.1) The Doloneia is the only book of the Iliad whose place in
    the poem was disputed in antiquity as a whole (curated dispute
    #8). A scholion preserved in the T manuscript reports: "they say
    that this rhapsody was arranged separately by Homer and was not
    counted part of the Iliad, but was placed into the poem by
    Pisistratus." Aristarchus treated the book as genuine; modern
    critics have gone both ways, Analysts excising it, defenders
    reading it as an old ambush-theme (the lochos) integral to the
    tradition. The internal evidence is genuinely double-edged — see
    the commentary above and notes [39] and [43]. The apparatus for
    this book records only line-level variants; the whole-book
    question lives in the testimonia.

[2] (10.1-2) The opening repeats the shape of Book 2's opening, in
    our same words ("slept the whole night through"; 2.1-2). The
    reversal is exact: there all slept but Zeus, who lay awake
    devising the Achaeans' ruin through a false dream; here all sleep
    but Agamemnon, who lies awake suffering that ruin — and the night
    will end with a true nightmare standing over a sleeper's head
    (note [37]).

[3] (10.5-8) Zeus's lightning as the portent of prodigious rain,
    hail, snow, "or somewhere the great mouth of bitter war":
    πευκεδανοῖο (8), a rare word cognate with pine-pitch bitterness,
    occurs only here in Homer. Agamemnon's groans come as thick as
    the flashes.

[4] (10.11-13) The many fires are the ones Hector lit at the end of
    Book 8 (8.553-565): Book 10, like Book 9 (9.77), opens inside the
    tableau Book 8 left burning. The "flutes and pipes" are the only
    battlefield music in the Iliad — the sound of an army sure of
    tomorrow — heard otherwise only in the city at peace on the
    Shield (18.495).

[5] (10.29) Night gear. This book replaces the formal bronze arming
    scene with skins: Agamemnon's lion (23-24), Menelaus's leopard
    and plain bronze cap, Diomedes' lion (177-178), Dolon's wolf-hide
    and ferret cap (334-335), and the two spies' leather helmets
    (257-265). Animal dress belongs to the night-world of the ambush,
    where the day-world's flashing crests would kill their wearer;
    the poet has an exact costume-vocabulary for it, most of it
    appearing nowhere else.

[6] (10.51-52) Athetized by Aristophanes of Byzantium and Aristarchus
    — the hyperbole (one day's work to be the Argives' concern "long
    and lastingly") judged un-Homeric.

[7] (10.68) "naming each man from his father, by his lineage" —
    courtesy as night-discipline: a voice in the dark must honor to
    be trusted. With it goes Agamemnon's "let us do the labor
    ourselves... Zeus laid this heavy misery on us at our very birth"
    (70-71): the night-book consistently shows the commanders' fear
    and drudgery that the day-books dress in ceremony.

[8] (10.84) Athetized by Aristarchus, presumably for the mules:
    Nestor's guess that a man groping through the camp at midnight is
    after a strayed mule struck ancient taste as beneath the epic. It
    is also the poem's one quartermaster's-eye view of its own camp.

[9] (10.110) "swift Ajax" is the Locrian, Oïleus' son (2.527); "the
    stout son of Phyleus" is Meges (2.627). The greater Ajax is the
    "godlike Ajax" of 112, paired as usual with Idomeneus.

[10] (10.114-125) Nestor's rebuke of the sleeping Menelaus, and
    Agamemnon's careful defense — he often hangs back, "not that he
    yields to sloth... but he looks to me, and waits for my lead" —
    reverse the Book 1 Agamemnon, who could not take one word of
    blame. The night steadies him; his fairest self appears at his
    least kingly hour.

[11] (10.158-159) Nestor wakes Diomedes "with a kick of his heel" —
    the poem's only comic reveille, answered by the sleeper's growl
    that the old man is "impossible." After 159 some ancient texts
    carried an extra line preserved only by Diogenes Laertius (6.53),
    who has Diogenes the Cynic quote it while prodding a sleeping
    man: "lest someone plant a spear in your back while you sleep."
    It is not in the printed text.

[12] (10.173-174) "it stands upon the razor's edge" — ἐπὶ ξυροῦ
    ἵσταται ἀκμῆς: the earliest surviving occurrence of the idiom
    English still uses, taken up by Theognis, Sophocles, and
    Herodotus (6.11).

[13] (10.199-201) The Achaean council convenes beyond the ditch "in a
    clear space, where ground showed empty of the dead" — line 199
    repeats 8.491 (our wording kept), where Hector held his night
    assembly by the river on the same terms. Each army deliberates
    sitting in the other's killing-ground; the mirrored councils
    organize the whole book (note [25]).

[14] (10.212-217) The scout's reward: fame "under heaven," plus a
    black ewe with her lamb from every captain, and a standing place
    at the feasts. Livestock and a seat at table — the modest,
    concrete economy of the night-book, set against Book 9's seven
    tripods, twenty cauldrons, and seven cities.

[15] (10.224-226) "When two go together, one marks before the other /
    where the advantage lies" — proverbial already in antiquity:
    Plato has Socrates quote it on the way to the banquet (Symposium
    174d) and again at Protagoras 348d. The couplet is the book's
    thesis; the Iliad's only two-man aristeia follows from it.

[16] (10.240) Omitted by Zenodotus, athetized by Aristarchus: the
    narrator's aside that Agamemnon "was afraid for tawny-haired
    Menelaus." The aside decodes 237-239 — "do not leave the better
    man behind... not even if he is the more kingly" is a king's
    coded plea that his brother not be chosen; the critics apparently
    preferred the code left unbroken.

[17] (10.252-253) Odysseus tells time by the stars — the working
    star-clock of a night operation. Line 253 was athetized by
    Aristophanes and Aristarchus over the arithmetic: if "more of the
    night has passed than two of its parts," then less than a third
    should remain — yet Odysseus allows "the third part." The Greek
    is loose the way spoken time-reckoning is loose ("it's past two
    in the morning; we have till first light"), and the ancient
    fussing over it is a small monument of pedantry.

[18] (10.257-259) The καταῖτυξ, "skull-cap": a plain bull's-hide
    helmet, "ridgeless, crestless," glossed in-line by the poet as if
    his audience needed the word explained — it occurs nowhere else
    in Greek literature. Night war strips off whatever catches light.

[19] (10.261-271) The boar's-tusk helmet is a genuine Bronze Age
    object: rows of sliced boar-tusk plates over felt and leather,
    known from Mycenaean excavation and art, and centuries obsolete
    by any plausible date of the poem's composition — the pedigree
    the poet gives it (stolen by Autolycus, passed via Amphidamas of
    Cythera to Molus to Meriones) is epic's own memory of the
    object's antiquity. Two ironies ride with it. Autolycus is
    Odysseus's maternal grandfather (Od. 19.394-466): the master-
    thief's loot ends the night on his own grandson's head. And its
    original owner, "Amyntor, Ormenus' son" of Eleon, is Phoenix's
    father (9.448) — the house Phoenix fled in Book 9 is burgled
    again, as it were, in Book 10's props.

[20] (10.274-276) A heron on the right hand is a favorable sign; in
    the dark the omen is heard, not seen. The entire daylight
    apparatus of the poem — arming, augury, even prayer-answer — is
    systematically converted to the ear in this book (cf. the hooves
    at 532-535, the whistle at 502).

[21] (10.285-290) Tydeus at Thebes for the fourth time in the poem
    (4.372-398; 5.800-808; cf. 23.679-680): the friendly embassy, the
    "honeyed word," and on the road home the ambush of fifty men that
    he destroyed — "deeds of sheer havoc." Diomedes has been measured
    against his father since Book 4; only here, on a night ambush,
    does the paternal precedent exactly fit the son's occasion.

[22] (10.292-294) The vow of the unbroken heifer with gilded horns
    recurs nearly verbatim in the Odyssey (3.382-384) — vowed there
    too by a Nestor, and fulfilled at 3.430-438, where a goldsmith
    actually sheathes the horns in gold leaf: the fullest "receipt"
    for any Homeric vow, and a famous cross-epic pair with this
    passage.

[23] (10.297-298) The epigraph lines. "Through the slaughter, through
    the corpses" (ἂμ φόνον, ἂν νέκυας) is the literal ground of the
    whole night-walk — repeated when they go forward again at 469.
    The two lions become two hounds on a hare (360-362) and a lion
    among unshepherded flocks (485-486): the book's men are beasts of
    prey throughout, and the skins they wear (note [5]) say so.

[24] (10.314-317) Dolon: the name is transparently "Sneak" (from
    δόλος, "trick"). A rich herald's son, "ugly to look at, but
    swift-footed" — the poet lends him Achilles' own epithet — and an
    only son among five sisters: a portrait, unique in the poem, of
    the spoiled volunteer. He will demand Achilles' horses as his
    wage (321-323) and be run down on foot.

[25] (10.319-332) Dolon's speech opens word for word as Diomedes' did
    (220): "my own heart and my proud spirit urge me..." — twinned
    volunteer-scenes, one issuing in the choice of Odysseus, the
    other in an empty oath. Hector swears by the scepter that "no
    other man of the Trojans shall mount behind those horses," and
    the narrator files his verdict flat: "he swore it falsely" — not
    because Hector meant to cheat, but because the oath was never his
    to keep. Coveting the immortal horses of Achilles is this book's
    measure of delusion (cf. note [30]; at 17.75-78 Hector himself
    is warned off them).

[26] (10.334-335) The grey wolf's hide and the ferret-skin cap
    (κτιδέη, marten or weasel): the spy dressed as a small night
    predator — the bottom rung of the book's ladder of skins (note
    [5]). Attic vase-painters loved the costume; several show Dolon
    on all fours in the wolf-suit.

[27] (10.351-353) Distance measured by plowing: "as far as mules plow
    in one stretch" is the οὖρον, the length of furrow a team draws
    before turning. The homely aside — mules out-pull oxen in deep
    fallow — arrives at the moment of highest suspense; the epic
    measures its night-chase by a farmer's daylight (cf. the
    fence-post interval of 23.431-432).

[28] (10.372-376) The spear "missed the man on purpose" — thrown to
    stop, not kill: they need him talking. Dolon's terror is
    clinically itemized: rooted feet, stammer, teeth chattering
    audibly, "green with fear" — our fixed color of fear (7.479),
    here soaked into the man himself.

[29] (10.383) "Take heart; and let death be no concern of your mind"
    — literally true (his death is already decided, so no longer
    worth minding) and utterly false as the comfort it pretends to
    be. The interrogation that follows is the poem's only one: two
    rounds, each opened with the courteous fixed line "But come, tell
    me this, and recount it truly" (384 = 405), each answered "Well
    then, I will recount... very truly" (413, 427) — the formulas of
    guest-conversation conducted over a man already dead.

[30] (10.391) "With many blindnesses Hector led my mind astray" —
    ἄτῃσι, the register's "blindness" (1.412): the god-sent delusion
    by which Agamemnon will explain the whole war in Book 19, here
    claimed by a small man about a promise of horses. Everyone in
    this poem accounts for ruin the same way; the poet lets the
    excuse shrink to the size of its owner.

[31] (10.397-399, 409-411) Both passages were athetized by
    Aristophanes and Aristarchus. Dolon reports Hector's briefing in
    Hector's very words (396-399 = 309-312 — kept verbatim in our
    English, "our hands" and all), and Odysseus's question repeats
    Nestor's proposal (409-411 = 208-210, flagged in the scholia as
    a wrongful repeat). The critics disliked the mechanical reuse;
    but verbatim relay is exactly how a chain of command speaks, and
    the poem's economy of repeated blocks is nowhere more functional
    than in a spy's debriefing.

[32] (10.415) The tomb of Ilus, eponym and founder of Ilion, is the
    plain's standing landmark (11.166, 371-372; 24.349): Hector
    holds his war-council at the ancestor's grave, as the Achaeans
    hold theirs among the fresh dead (note [13]).

[33] (10.428-431) A miniature order of battle, complement to the
    Trojan Catalogue (2.816-877): the same allied peoples, now laid
    out by billet rather than muster — Carians, Paeonians, Leleges,
    Caucones, Pelasgians toward the sea; Lycians, Mysians, Phrygians,
    Maeonians toward Thymbre. The Thracians of Rhesus stand in no
    catalogue: "new-come, outermost of all" — arrived, and doomed,
    within the one night.

[34] (10.435-441) Rhesus: horses "whiter than snow, and like the
    winds in running," a gold-and-silver chariot, and golden armor
    fit for "the deathless gods." Later tradition — the scholia,
    Virgil (Aeneid 1.469-473), and the Rhesus transmitted under
    Euripides' name — knew an oracle that Troy could never fall once
    Rhesus and his horses had grazed Trojan pasture and drunk of
    Scamander, making the raid a preemption of fate. Our text knows
    nothing of it: here the splendor simply dwarfs its owner's
    one-night part, and the armor "for the deathless gods" lies on
    the ground at 504 while the gods' own agent decides against
    taking it.

[35] (10.454-457) Dolon's hand is out for the suppliant's touch of
    the chin (the gesture of 1.501) when the sword falls: supplication
    not refused, as Agamemnon refused Adrestus (6.55-65), but
    forestalled — a beat crueler. "And the head of the still-speaking
    man was mingled with the dust" (457): Aristotle quotes the line
    in earnest (Parts of Animals 673a), discussing whether severed
    heads can speak. The grammar performs the horror: the man is
    still a speaking subject in mid-construction as his head meets
    the ground.

[36] (10.460-466) "Athena of the Spoils" (ληῖτις) — an epithet found
    only here. The trophies are hung on a tamarisk and flagged with
    reeds and boughs for the return leg: fieldcraft doubling as
    votive religion, an altar-less offering to match the unseen omen
    of note [20]. The dedication is completed at 570-571 and the
    libation of 578-579.

[37] (10.496-497) Rhesus dies "gasping hard: for an evil dream stood
    at his head / that night — the son of Oeneus' son — by the
    cunning of Athena." The nightmare stands at the sleeper's head in
    the regular posture of Homeric dreams (2.20, 23.68) — and the
    dream is Diomedes himself, Oeneus' grandson, sword in hand: dream
    and killer are one figure, the boldest compression in the book,
    closing the frame the false dream of Book 2 opened (note [2]).
    Zenodotus and Aristophanes omitted 497 and Aristarchus athetized
    it — flattening the vision back into ordinary death; the printed
    text keeps it.

[38] (10.498-502) Odysseus improvises: the whip is still lying in the
    chariot, so he beats the horses with his bow, and signals with a
    whistle — the only whistle in Homer. Someone did not think of
    everything: the small unheroic realism the day-books never allow.

[39] (10.509-513) "Think of your return" — νόστου δὴ μνῆσαι: the
    Odyssey's theme-word sounded by Athena in the Iliad, to the other
    poem's kind of hero on the other poem's kind of errand; a
    centerpiece of the book's Odyssean coloring (see commentary). And
    they escape riding bareback — nearly the only riding of horses in
    the Iliad (elsewhere a simile, 15.679-684) — because the chariot
    stayed with the dead.

[40] (10.515) "kept no blind man's watch" — ἀλαοσκοπιή, fixed here at
    its first occurrence; it recurs of Poseidon at 13.10 and 14.135.
    Zenodotus read the phrase as two words with a different sense;
    the printed text follows Aristarchus.

[41] (10.534) "shall I be wrong, or right, in what I say? My heart
    commands it" — literally "shall I lie, or speak true?": Nestor's
    strange scruple, omitted by Zenodotus here, recurs in Helen's
    mouth at Odyssey 4.140. It is the night-book's epistemology in
    one line: hooves heard in the dark must be guessed at aloud
    before they can be greeted.

[42] (10.561-565) Odysseus's tally makes Dolon "the thirteenth" —
    answering Rhesus, the thirteenth Thracian (495): each side's
    night closes its account in the same figure, and Odysseus books
    his interrogation-kill as a line-item. Then he drives the ditch
    "laughing aloud" (565) — no other killing in the Iliad ends in
    laughter; the book's moral register, and part of what has always
    made readers uneasy in it, is in that word.

[43] (10.572-579) The close: a wade in the sea, then "polished
    bathing-tubs" — ἀσάμινθοι, only here in the Iliad against nine
    occurrences in the Odyssey — oil, a meal, and honey-sweet wine
    poured to Athena. An Odyssean hospitality-sequence grafted onto
    the war-camp, and a chief exhibit in the authenticity dossier
    (note [1]); it is also precisely what the poem's one successful
    night requires — the blood of 483-484 washed off before
    rosy-fingered dawn, eight lines into Book 11, restores the rules
    of the day. In 579 Aristarchus read "drew" (ἀφυσσόμενοι); the
    vulgate carried a variant of the same sense.
