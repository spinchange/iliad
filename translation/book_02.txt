BOOK 2

The lying Dream, the testing of the army, Thersites beaten — and the
Catalogue of Ships.

TRANSLATOR'S COMMENTARY

Book 2 falls into two very different halves — the night-intrigue, failed
test, and Thersites scene (1-493), and then the Catalogue of Ships with its
Trojan counterpart (494-877) — and the main book-wide decision here was how
to handle the second half. The Catalogue has been suspected since antiquity's
critics and modern Analysts alike of being an originally independent
composition, and several manuscripts omit the span entirely (see note [20]).
I have translated it as fully integral, and deliberately kept its litany
register rather than flattening it into a gazetteer: one English line per
Greek line as elsewhere, every place-epithet translated ("Thisbe of the many
doves," "rocky Aulis"), leader-genealogies given their full ceremonial
weight. The incantatory repetition of the ship-count refrains ("With him
followed forty black ships") is preserved exactly where the Greek repeats
and varied only where the Greek varies. The little narrative windows that
open out of the list — Thamyris maimed by the Muses, Protesilaus' half-built
house, Philoctetes on Lemnos, Nireus beautiful and negligible — are the
Catalogue's own pulse, and the notes flag them rather than the geography.

The other live decision was tonal: Odysseus' two-tier crowd control
(kings get "gentle words," commoners get the scepter across the back).
Aristarchus athetized 193-197, and the passage is independently missing
from Xenophon's quotation of the scene (note [11]); I translate as printed
and let the class asymmetry stand at full strength, since Thersites'
beating immediately afterward depends on it. Thersites himself I have kept
rough — "endless babble," "word-slinging insulter" — because the poem's own
verdict on him is rough; this is the one place in the Iliad where the army's
laughter is turned on a named common soldier, and softening it misrepresents
the scene.

Zenodotus is unusually active in this book: he condensed the Dream's
triple-repeated message (notes [4]), athetized Agamemnon's atē-speech
(11-118, note [7]), the Thersites portrait-lines, the Nireus anaphora
(note [26]), and the Achilles-passage inside the Catalogue (note [27]).
As in Book 1, the translation follows the printed vulgate text throughout
and records atheteses in the notes.

Two carried-over threads from Book 1 to watch: Agamemnon here blames his
quarrel with Achilles on Zeus-sent atē ("blindness," 111, and openly "it
was I who began the anger," 378) — the same vocabulary Achilles used of him
at 1.412; and the Catalogue's Achilles-paragraph (686-694) holds the plot's
tension in place mid-list. New recurring epithets are footnoted at first
occurrence and logged in translation/CONVENTIONS.md; the famous crux at
318 (did the god make the serpent-portent "conspicuous" or "invisible"?)
is note [14], and the Salamis couplet with its ancient forgery accusation
is note [23].

   1  The other gods, and men who fight from chariots,
   2  slept the whole night through; but sweet sleep did not hold Zeus.
   3  He was turning over in his mind how he might honor
   4  Achilles, and destroy many beside the Achaean ships.
   5  And this plan seemed to his heart the best:
   6  to send baneful Dream to Atreus' son Agamemnon.[1]
   7  And speaking winged words he addressed him:
   8  "Up, go, baneful Dream, to the Achaeans' swift ships.
   9  Come to the hut of Agamemnon, Atreus' son,
  10  and tell him everything exactly as I charge you.
  11  Bid him arm the long-haired Achaeans[2]
  12  in full force: for now he might take the wide-wayed city
  13  of the Trojans. The immortals who keep their homes on Olympus
  14  are no longer divided; Hera has bent them all
  15  by her begging, and sorrows are fastened on the Trojans."
  16  So he spoke, and Dream went when he had heard the word.
  17  Swiftly he came to the Achaeans' swift ships
  18  and went to Atreus' son Agamemnon. He found him
  19  sleeping in his hut, ambrosial slumber poured around him.
  20  He stood above his head in the likeness of Neleus' son
  21  Nestor, whom Agamemnon honored most of the elders;
  22  in his likeness the divine Dream spoke:
  23  "You sleep, son of war-minded Atreus, breaker of horses.[3]
  24  A man of counsel should not sleep the whole night through,
  25  a man to whom peoples are entrusted, who has so much to care for.
  26  Now hear me quickly: I am a messenger of Zeus,
  27  who far away cares greatly for you, and pities you.
  28  He bids you arm the long-haired Achaeans
  29  in full force: for now you might take the wide-wayed city
  30  of the Trojans. The immortals who keep their homes on Olympus
  31  are no longer divided; Hera has bent them all
  32  by her begging, and sorrows are fastened on the Trojans
  33  from Zeus. Keep this in your mind, and let no forgetting
  34  take you, when honey-hearted sleep lets you go."
  35  So he spoke and went away, and left him there
  36  believing in his heart things that were not to be:
  37  he thought he would take Priam's city on that very day —
  38  the fool: he did not know what work Zeus was planning,
  39  who meant to lay yet more griefs and groans
  40  on Trojans and Danaans alike through the strong encounters of war.
  41  He woke from sleep, the divine voice drifting around him,
  42  and sat upright, and pulled on his soft tunic,
  43  fine and new-made, and threw his great cloak around him,
  44  and beneath his shining feet he bound fair sandals,
  45  and slung about his shoulders the silver-studded sword,
  46  and took up the ancestral scepter, imperishable forever,
  47  and with it went down along the ships of the bronze-shirted Achaeans.
  48  Now the goddess Dawn went up to high Olympus,
  49  announcing light to Zeus and the immortals;
  50  and he ordered the clear-voiced heralds
  51  to summon the long-haired Achaeans to assembly.
  52  They made the summons, and the men gathered quickly.
  53  But first he seated a council of great-hearted elders
  54  beside the ship of Nestor, the Pylos-born king,
  55  and calling them together he framed his shrewd plan:
  56  "Hear me, friends. A divine Dream came to me in sleep
  57  through the ambrosial night, and in face, in build, in bearing
  58  it was most like brilliant Nestor.
  59  It stood above my head, and it said to me:
  60  'You sleep, son of war-minded Atreus, breaker of horses.[4]
  61  A man of counsel should not sleep the whole night through,
  62  a man to whom peoples are entrusted, who has so much to care for.
  63  Now hear me quickly: I am a messenger of Zeus,
  64  who far away cares greatly for you, and pities you.
  65  He bids you arm the long-haired Achaeans
  66  in full force: for now you might take the wide-wayed city
  67  of the Trojans. The immortals who keep their homes on Olympus
  68  are no longer divided; Hera has bent them all
  69  by her begging, and sorrows are fastened on the Trojans
  70  from Zeus. Keep this in your mind.' So the Dream spoke
  71  and flew away, and sweet sleep released me.
  72  Come then — let us see if we can arm the sons of the Achaeans.
  73  But first I will test them with words, as is the custom,[5]
  74  and order them to flee with the many-benched ships;
  75  and you, from your places, one here, one there, hold them back with words."
  76  So he spoke and sat down; and among them rose
  77  Nestor, who was king of sandy Pylos.
  78  With good will toward them he spoke and addressed them:
  79  "Friends, leaders and lords of the Argives,
  80  if any other Achaean had told us this dream,
  81  we would call it a lie and turn our backs on it;
  82  but the man who saw it claims to be far the best of the Achaeans.
  83  Come then — let us see if we can arm the sons of the Achaeans."
  84  So he spoke, and led the way out of the council,
  85  and the scepter-holding kings rose and obeyed
  86  the shepherd of the people; and the troops came streaming.
  87  As when the tribes of thronging bees come out
  88  of some hollow rock, always arriving anew,
  89  and fly in clusters over the flowers of spring,
  90  and some swarm thick this way, and some that way —
  91  so the many tribes of men from the ships and huts
  92  marched in companies along the deep beach
  93  to the assembly; and among them Rumor blazed like fire,
  94  Zeus's messenger, driving them on; and they kept gathering.
  95  The assembly was in turmoil, and the earth groaned beneath
  96  as the troops sat down, and there was uproar. Nine heralds,
  97  shouting, tried to hold them back — to stop the clamor
  98  and make them listen to the Zeus-nurtured kings.
  99  With effort the people were seated, held in their places,
 100  and left off shouting. Then the lord Agamemnon stood up,
 101  holding the scepter Hephaestus had labored to make.[6]
 102  Hephaestus gave it to lord Zeus, son of Cronos;
 103  then Zeus gave it to the guide, the slayer of Argus;
 104  lord Hermes gave it to Pelops, driver of horses;
 105  Pelops in turn gave it to Atreus, shepherd of the people;
 106  Atreus, dying, left it to Thyestes rich in flocks;
 107  and Thyestes in turn left it to Agamemnon to carry,
 108  to be lord over many islands and all of Argos.
 109  Leaning on it, he spoke his words to the Argives:
 110  "Friends, Danaan heroes, squires of Ares,
 111  Zeus, Cronos' great son, has bound me fast in heavy blindness —[7]
 112  the hard god: he promised me once, he bowed his head,
 113  that I should sack well-walled Ilion before returning;
 114  now he has planned a cruel deceit, and orders me
 115  back to Argos in disgrace, with half my people lost.
 116  Such, it seems, must be the pleasure of overmastering Zeus,
 117  who has undone the crowns of many cities
 118  and will undo more yet: his power is the greatest.
 119  This is a shameful thing even for men to come to hear of,
 120  that so fine an Achaean host, so great,
 121  fought a fruitless war and battled
 122  fewer men, with no end yet in sight.
 123  For if we wished — Achaeans and Trojans both —
 124  to swear a solemn truce and count ourselves,
 125  the Trojans mustering all who have their hearths in the city,
 126  and we Achaeans forming up in tens,
 127  and each ten chose one Trojan man to pour its wine,
 128  many tens would go without a wine-pourer.
 129  By so much, I tell you, the sons of the Achaeans outnumber
 130  the Trojans who live in the city. But there are allies,
 131  spear-wielding men from many cities,
 132  who beat me far off my course and will not let me
 133  sack Ilion's well-settled citadel, for all my wanting it.
 134  Nine years of great Zeus have gone already,
 135  and now the ships' timbers are rotten, the rigging loosed,
 136  and our wives, no doubt, and our little children
 137  sit in our halls waiting; while the work
 138  for which we came here stands as undone as ever.
 139  Come then — as I say it, let us all obey:
 140  let us flee with the ships to our dear fatherland.
 141  We shall not take wide-wayed Troy now."
 142  So he spoke, and stirred the heart in the breast
 143  of everyone in the crowd who had not heard the council.
 144  The assembly moved like the long waves of the sea,
 145  the Icarian deep, when East Wind and South Wind together
 146  rouse them, rushing down from the clouds of father Zeus;
 147  or as when the West Wind comes and moves deep grain,
 148  driving hard, and the field bows its ears —
 149  so all that assembly was moved. With a roar
 150  they rushed for the ships, and under their feet the dust
 151  rose and hung; and they shouted to one another
 152  to lay hold of the ships and drag them to the bright sea,
 153  and they cleared out the launching-channels; the cry went up to the sky
 154  as they strained for home; and they pulled the props from under the hulls.
 155  Then a homecoming beyond fate would have come to the Argives,
 156  if Hera had not spoken a word to Athena:
 157  "Shame! Unwearied one, child of aegis-bearing Zeus —[8]
 158  is it like this, homeward, to their dear fatherland,
 159  that the Argives are to flee over the broad back of the sea,
 160  and leave to Priam and the Trojans their boast —
 161  Argive Helen, for whose sake so many Achaeans
 162  have perished at Troy, far from their dear fatherland?
 163  Go now through the host of the bronze-shirted Achaeans;
 164  with your gentle words hold back every man;
 165  do not let them drag the balanced ships down to the sea."
 166  So she spoke, and the goddess grey-eyed Athena did not disobey.
 167  Down from the peaks of Olympus she went darting
 168  and swiftly came to the Achaeans' swift ships.
 169  There she found Odysseus, the equal of Zeus in counsel,[9]
 170  standing still: he had not laid a hand on his black
 171  well-benched ship, for grief had reached his heart and spirit.
 172  Standing close, grey-eyed Athena spoke to him:
 173  "Zeus-sprung son of Laertes, Odysseus of many devices,[10]
 174  is it like this, homeward, to your dear fatherland,
 175  that you will all flee, tumbling into the many-benched ships,
 176  and leave to Priam and the Trojans their boast —
 177  Argive Helen, for whose sake so many Achaeans
 178  have perished at Troy, far from their dear fatherland?
 179  Go now through the host of the Achaeans; hold back no longer;
 180  with your gentle words hold back every man;
 181  do not let them drag the balanced ships down to the sea."
 182  So she spoke, and he knew the voice of the goddess speaking.
 183  He set off at a run, and threw off his cloak — the herald
 184  Eurybates of Ithaca, who attended him, picked it up —
 185  and coming face to face with Agamemnon, Atreus' son,
 186  he took from him the ancestral scepter, imperishable forever,
 187  and with it went along the ships of the bronze-shirted Achaeans.
 188  Whenever he met a king, or a man of note,
 189  he would stand beside him and hold him back with gentle words:
 190  "Friend, it is not right to threaten you like a coward;
 191  but sit down yourself, and seat the rest of your people.
 192  You do not yet clearly know the mind of Atreus' son:
 193  now he is testing, and soon he will bear down on the sons of the Achaeans.[11]
 194  Did we not all hear in council what he said?
 195  May he not in anger do some harm to the sons of the Achaeans.
 196  Great is the anger of Zeus-nurtured kings;
 197  their honor is from Zeus, and Zeus the counselor loves them."
 198  But whatever man of the people he saw, and found him shouting,
 199  him he would drive with the scepter and berate aloud:
 200  "Fool — sit still and listen to what others tell you,
 201  your betters. You are unwarlike and without strength,
 202  never counted, whether in battle or in council.
 203  We cannot all be kings here, we Achaeans.
 204  The rule of many is no good thing: let there be one ruler,[12]
 205  one king, to whom the son of crooked-counseled Cronos gave
 206  the scepter and the ordinances, to do the ruling for his people."
 207  So, masterfully, he went through the army setting it in order; and they
 208  came streaming back to the assembly from the ships and huts
 209  with a roar, as when a wave of the loud-roaring sea
 210  thunders on a great beach, and the deep crashes in answer.
 211  Then the others sat down and were held in their places —
 212  all but Thersites, alone, still brawling on, unmeasured in speech,[13]
 213  whose mind was full of words, disorderly and many,
 214  to quarrel with kings — recklessly, in no kind of order,
 215  but with whatever looked likely to raise a laugh
 216  among the Argives. And he was the ugliest man who came under Ilion:
 217  bandy-legged, lame in one foot, his two shoulders
 218  hunched and drawn in over his chest; and above them
 219  a head that came to a point, with sparse stubble growing on it.
 220  He was hatefullest of all to Achilles and Odysseus,
 221  for those two he was forever reviling. But now it was brilliant Agamemnon
 222  he went at, shrieking shrill abuse; the Achaeans
 223  were furious at him, and indignation rose in every heart.
 224  Bawling loudly, he berated Agamemnon:
 225  "Son of Atreus, what are you complaining of now? What more do you want?
 226  Your huts are full of bronze; there are many women
 227  in your huts, choice ones, whom we Achaeans
 228  give you first of all whenever we take a town.
 229  Or do you still lack gold as well, that some son
 230  of the horse-taming Trojans will bring out of Ilion as ransom
 231  for a son that I bound and led away — I, or another Achaean?
 232  Or a young woman, to lie with in love,
 233  one you keep apart for yourself? It is not right
 234  for the commander to march the sons of the Achaeans into misery.
 235  Soft things! Vile disgraces! Achaean women — Achaean men no longer!
 236  Let us go home, home with the ships, and leave this man
 237  here in Troy to digest his prizes, so he may learn
 238  whether we others are any help to him or not —
 239  he who even now dishonored Achilles, a man
 240  far better than himself: he has taken and keeps his prize; he seized it himself.
 241  But there is no anger in Achilles' heart; he lets it go.
 242  Otherwise, son of Atreus, this outrage would be your last."
 243  So he spoke, reviling Agamemnon, shepherd of the people —
 244  Thersites. But quickly brilliant Odysseus was at his side,
 245  and glaring at him darkly he lashed him with hard words:
 246  "Thersites — endless babble, clear-voiced speaker though you are:
 247  stop; do not stand up alone to quarrel with kings.
 248  I say there is no viler mortal than you
 249  of all who came under Ilion with the sons of Atreus.
 250  So you had better not take kings into your mouth when you hold forth,
 251  hurling insults at them, with one eye on the voyage home.
 252  We do not even know clearly yet how these things will end —
 253  whether we sons of the Achaeans come home well or badly.
 254  So now you sit here reviling Agamemnon, Atreus' son,
 255  shepherd of the people, because the Danaan heroes
 256  give him so much. You speak in mockery.
 257  But I tell you this outright, and it will be accomplished:
 258  if I find you playing the fool again as you are now,
 259  then may Odysseus' head no longer sit on his shoulders,
 260  may I no longer be called the father of Telemachus,
 261  if I do not seize you and strip you of your own clothes,
 262  the cloak and the tunic that cover your shame,
 263  and send you off to the swift ships in tears,
 264  whipped out of the assembly with strokes of disgrace."
 265  So he spoke, and with the scepter he struck his back
 266  and shoulders. Thersites doubled up, and a fat tear fell from him,
 267  and a bloody welt rose up out of his back
 268  under the golden scepter. He sat down, terrified,
 269  and hurting, with a helpless look, he wiped away the tear.
 270  And the men, vexed at heart though they were, laughed at him sweetly,
 271  and thus one would say, with a glance at his neighbor:
 272  "Well now! Odysseus has done ten thousand good things,
 273  leading in good counsel, marshaling war;
 274  but this is the best thing he has done among the Argives:
 275  he has shut this word-slinging insulter out of our debates.
 276  That proud heart of his will not send him back again
 277  to rail at kings with words of abuse."
 278  So spoke the multitude. Then the sacker of cities, Odysseus,
 279  stood up with the scepter, and beside him grey-eyed Athena,
 280  in the likeness of a herald, called the host to silence,
 281  so that near and far the sons of the Achaeans
 282  might hear his word alike, and take in his counsel.
 283  With good will toward them he spoke and addressed them:
 284  "Son of Atreus — now, lord, the Achaeans mean
 285  to make you the most despised of mortal men,
 286  and they will not fulfill the promise they undertook
 287  while still on the way here from horse-pasturing Argos:
 288  that you should sack well-walled Ilion before returning.
 289  Like little children or widowed women
 290  they wail to one another about going home.
 291  And truly, there is toil here to send a man home disheartened:
 292  a man kept even one month from his wife
 293  frets by his many-benched ship when the winter gales
 294  and the rising sea shut him in;
 295  and for us this is the ninth year, circling round,
 296  that we have stayed here. So I cannot blame the Achaeans
 297  for fretting beside the beaked ships. But even so —
 298  it is shameful to wait so long and then come home empty.
 299  Bear up, friends, and stay a while longer, till we learn
 300  whether Calchas prophesies truly or not.
 301  For this we know well in our hearts, and you are all
 302  witnesses — all whom the fates of death have not carried off:
 303  only yesterday, it seems, when the Achaean ships were gathering
 304  at Aulis, bearing evils for Priam and the Trojans,
 305  and we, around a spring, at the holy altars,
 306  were offering the immortals perfect hecatombs
 307  under a beautiful plane tree, where the bright water ran:
 308  there a great sign appeared. A serpent, its back blood-dark,
 309  terrible, which the Olympian himself had sent into the light,
 310  darted from under the altar and made for the plane tree.
 311  There were nestling sparrows there, baby chicks,
 312  on the topmost branch, cowering under the leaves —
 313  eight of them, and the mother who hatched them made nine.
 314  There he devoured them, cheeping piteously,
 315  while the mother fluttered around, crying for her dear chicks;
 316  and coiling up he caught her by the wing as she shrieked about him.
 317  But when he had eaten the sparrow's children and the bird herself,
 318  the god who had shown him made him a sign conspicuous:[14]
 319  the son of crooked-counseled Cronos turned him to stone,
 320  and we stood there marveling at what had happened.
 321  So then: since this dread portent entered the gods' hecatombs,
 322  Calchas straightway spoke in prophecy:
 323  'Why have you fallen silent, long-haired Achaeans?
 324  For us Zeus the counselor has shown this great sign,
 325  late, late of fulfillment, whose fame will never perish.
 326  As this serpent ate the sparrow's children and the bird herself —
 327  eight, and the mother who hatched them made nine —
 328  so shall we make war here for as many years,
 329  and in the tenth we shall take the wide-wayed city.'
 330  That is how he spoke; and now it is all being fulfilled.
 331  Come then, stay, all of you, strong-greaved Achaeans,
 332  right here, until we take the great city of Priam."
 333  So he spoke, and the Argives roared aloud — and around them the ships
 334  echoed terribly under the shouting of the Achaeans —
 335  in praise of the word of godlike Odysseus.
 336  And among them spoke the Gerenian horseman Nestor:[15]
 337  "Shame! You hold assembly like little boys,
 338  infants with no interest in deeds of war.
 339  Where then will our compacts go, and our oaths?
 340  Into the fire, then, with every counsel and plan of men,
 341  the unmixed libations, the right hands we trusted!
 342  We are battling with words, and nothing else; a remedy
 343  we cannot find, for all our long time here.
 344  Son of Atreus — hold your counsel unshaken, as before,
 345  and lead the Argives through the strong encounters;
 346  and let them rot, the one or two of the Achaeans
 347  who take counsel apart — they will accomplish nothing —
 348  all for going back to Argos before we know
 349  whether the promise of aegis-bearing Zeus is a lie or not.
 350  For I say the overmastering son of Cronos bowed his head
 351  on that day when the Argives went aboard their swift-faring ships
 352  bearing slaughter and doom for the Trojans:
 353  lightening on the right, showing signs of good omen.
 354  Therefore let no man press to be gone homeward
 355  before he has lain beside the wife of some Trojan,
 356  and avenged the struggles and groans of Helen.[16]
 357  But if any man is wildly set on going home,
 358  let him just lay a hand on his black well-benched ship,
 359  and before all others he will meet his death and doom.
 360  And you, lord — take good counsel yourself, and listen to another;
 361  not to be tossed aside is the word I speak:
 362  divide the men by tribes and by clans, Agamemnon,
 363  so clan may stand by clan, and tribe by tribe.
 364  If you do this, and the Achaeans obey,
 365  you will know then which of your leaders is a coward, and which of your men,
 366  and which is brave: for they will be fighting among their own.
 367  And you will know whether it is by the gods' will you fail to take the city,
 368  or by men's cowardice and folly in war."
 369  Answering him spoke the lord Agamemnon:
 370  "Once again, old man, you outdo the sons of the Achaeans in speech.
 371  O father Zeus, Athena, Apollo —
 372  if I had ten such counselors among the Achaeans,
 373  then the city of lord Priam would soon bow its head,
 374  taken and sacked beneath our hands.
 375  But instead aegis-bearing Zeus, Cronos' son, gives me griefs,
 376  throwing me into fruitless quarrels and feuds.
 377  I and Achilles fought over a girl
 378  with head-on words — and it was I who began the anger.
 379  If ever we take counsel as one, then no longer
 380  will there be any putting off of evil for the Trojans, not for a moment.
 381  Now go to your meal, so we may join battle.
 382  Let every man sharpen his spear well, and set his shield right,
 383  let him give his swift-footed horses their feed,
 384  let him look over his chariot on every side, and put his mind on war,
 385  so that all day long we may measure ourselves in hateful battle.
 386  For there will be no pause, not the least,
 387  until night comes on and parts the fury of men.
 388  The strap of the body-covering shield will be sweated through
 389  on many a chest, and the hand will ache around the spear,
 390  and many a horse will sweat, straining at the polished chariot.
 391  And whomever I see minded to hang back from the fighting,
 392  lingering by the beaked ships — for him there will be
 393  no hope after that of escaping the dogs and the birds."
 394  So he spoke, and the Argives roared aloud, like a wave
 395  against a high headland when the South Wind comes and drives it
 396  on a jutting cliff — the waves never leave it,
 397  whatever wind is blowing, whether they rise this side or that.
 398  They stood up and scattered, hurrying to the ships,
 399  and made their fires smoke among the huts, and took their meal.
 400  And each man sacrificed to one of the gods who are forever,
 401  praying to escape death and the grind of Ares.
 402  But Agamemnon, lord of men, slaughtered an ox,
 403  fat, five years old, to the overmastering son of Cronos,
 404  and called the elders, the chief men of all the Achaeans:
 405  Nestor first of all, and lord Idomeneus,
 406  then the two Ajaxes and Tydeus' son,
 407  and sixth Odysseus, the equal of Zeus in counsel.
 408  And Menelaus, good at the war-cry, came unbidden,[17]
 409  for he knew in his heart how his brother labored.
 410  They stood around the ox and took up the barley-grains,
 411  and praying among them the lord Agamemnon spoke:
 412  "Zeus, most glorious, greatest, lord of the dark cloud, dwelling in the sky:
 413  let not the sun go down, nor the darkness come,
 414  until I have thrown down Priam's hall headlong,
 415  blackened with smoke, and burned its doors with hostile fire,
 416  and slashed the tunic on Hector's chest to rags
 417  with the bronze; and around him may his companions, many of them,
 418  fall face-down in the dust and bite the earth."
 419  So he spoke; but the son of Cronos would not yet grant it.
 420  He accepted the offering — and piled up joyless toil.
 421  Then, when they had prayed and thrown the barley-grains,
 422  they first drew back the victims' heads, cut their throats, and flayed them,
 423  carved out the thigh-pieces and wrapped them in fat,
 424  making a double fold, and laid raw flesh upon them;
 425  these they burned on dry leafless billets,
 426  and spitted the inner parts and held them over the flame of Hephaestus.
 427  When the thighs were burned and they had tasted the inner parts,
 428  they cut up the rest and threaded it on spits,
 429  roasted it with care, and drew it all off.
 430  Then when they had finished the work and made the meal ready,
 431  they feasted, and no heart lacked a fair share of the feast.
 432  And when they had put away desire for eating and drinking,
 433  the Gerenian horseman Nestor began to speak among them:
 434  "Most glorious son of Atreus, lord of men Agamemnon,
 435  let us sit talking here no longer, nor delay
 436  any further the work the god is putting in our hands.
 437  Come: let the heralds of the bronze-shirted Achaeans
 438  make their cry and gather the host along the ships,
 439  and let us go down together, as we are, through the wide Achaean camp,
 440  to waken sharp Ares the sooner."
 441  So he spoke, and the lord of men Agamemnon did not disobey.
 442  At once he ordered the clear-voiced heralds
 443  to summon the long-haired Achaeans to battle;
 444  they made the summons, and the men gathered quickly.
 445  The Zeus-nurtured kings around Atreus' son
 446  hurried about marshaling them, and among them grey-eyed Athena,
 447  holding the aegis — precious, unaging, immortal,[18]
 448  from which a hundred tassels of pure gold float free,
 449  all finely woven, each worth a hundred oxen.
 450  With it she flashed through the Achaean host,
 451  driving them on; and in each man's heart she roused strength
 452  to war and fight without ceasing.
 453  And at once war became sweeter to them than going home
 454  in the hollow ships to their dear fatherland.
 455  As an annihilating fire blazes up through a boundless forest
 456  on the peaks of a mountain, and the glare shows from far away,
 457  so, as they marched, from the tremendous bronze
 458  a gleam went flashing through the upper air to the sky.
 459  And as the many tribes of winged birds —
 460  geese, or cranes, or long-necked swans —
 461  on the Asian meadow around the streams of Caystrius
 462  fly this way and that, glorying in their wings,
 463  settling with cries, always advancing, and the meadow rings —
 464  so their many tribes from the ships and huts
 465  poured out onto the Scamandrian plain, and beneath them the ground
 466  thundered terribly under the feet of men and horses.
 467  They stood in the flowering Scamandrian meadow,
 468  tens of thousands, as many as the leaves and flowers in their season.
 469  As the many tribes of thronging flies
 470  that swarm through a herdsman's steading
 471  in the season of spring, when milk wets the pails,
 472  so many were the long-haired Achaeans who stood in the plain
 473  facing the Trojans, mad to break them.
 474  And as goatherds easily sort out
 475  their wide-wandering flocks of goats when they mingle at pasture,
 476  so the leaders sorted the men this way and that
 477  to go into battle, and among them the lord Agamemnon:
 478  in eyes and head like Zeus who delights in thunder,
 479  in girdle like Ares, in chest like Poseidon.
 480  As a bull stands out far the finest of all in the herd,
 481  conspicuous among the gathering cattle,
 482  such did Zeus make Atreus' son on that day,
 483  conspicuous among many, preeminent among heroes.

 484  Tell me now, Muses, who keep your homes on Olympus —[19]
 485  for you are goddesses, you are present, you know all things,
 486  while we hear only the report and know nothing —
 487  who were the leaders and lords of the Danaans.
 488  The multitude I could not tell nor name,
 489  not if I had ten tongues and ten mouths,
 490  a voice unbreakable, and a heart of bronze within me,
 491  unless the Olympian Muses, daughters of aegis-bearing
 492  Zeus, remembered for me all who came under Ilion.
 493  But the captains of the ships I will tell, and all the ships.

 494  Of the Boeotians, Peneleos and Leïtus were leaders,[20]
 495  with Arcesilaus and Prothoenor and Clonius:
 496  they who dwelt in Hyria and rocky Aulis,
 497  in Schoenus and Scolus and Eteonus of the many ridges,
 498  Thespeia, Graea, and spacious Mycalessus;
 499  who dwelt around Harma and Eilesium and Erythrae;
 500  who held Eleon and Hyle and Peteon,
 501  Ocalea and Medeon, that well-built citadel,
 502  Copae, Eutresis, and Thisbe of the many doves;
 503  who held Coroneia and grassy Haliartus;
 504  who held Plataea; who dwelt in Glisas;
 505  who held Hypothebae, that well-built citadel,
 506  and holy Onchestus, Poseidon's shining grove;
 507  who held Arne rich in grapes, and Mideia,
 508  sacred Nisa, and Anthedon at the land's edge:
 509  of these came fifty ships, and in each one
 510  a hundred and twenty young men of the Boeotians.
 511  And those who lived in Aspledon and Minyan Orchomenus
 512  were led by Ascalaphus and Ialmenus, sons of Ares,
 513  whom Astyoche bore in the house of Actor, Azeus' son —
 514  a modest maiden: she went up to her upper chamber,
 515  and mighty Ares lay with her in secret.
 516  With them thirty hollow ships sailed in order.
 517  And the Phocians were led by Schedius and Epistrophus,
 518  sons of great-hearted Iphitus, Naubolus' son:
 519  they who held Cyparissus and rocky Pytho,
 520  holy Crisa and Daulis and Panopeus;
 521  who dwelt around Anemoreia and Hyampolis;
 522  who lived along the brilliant river Cephisus;
 523  who held Lilaea at the springs of Cephisus.
 524  With them followed forty black ships.
 525  Their leaders were busy ranging the Phocian ranks,
 526  and they armed close beside the Boeotians, on the left.
 527  Of the Locrians, swift Ajax led, Oïleus' son —
 528  the lesser Ajax, in no way so big as Telamonian Ajax
 529  but far smaller: a little man, in a linen corslet,[21]
 530  but with the spear he surpassed all Hellenes and Achaeans:
 531  they who dwelt in Cynus and Opoeis and Calliarus,
 532  Bessa and Scarphe and lovely Augeae,
 533  Tarphe and Thronium, around the streams of Boagrius.
 534  With him followed forty black ships
 535  of the Locrians who live across from holy Euboea.
 536  And the Abantes, breathing fury, who held Euboea —
 537  Chalcis and Eretria and Histiaea rich in grapes,
 538  Cerinthus by the sea and the steep citadel of Dios;
 539  who held Carystus; who dwelt in Styra —
 540  these were led by Elephenor, scion of Ares,
 541  Chalcodon's son, chief of the great-hearted Abantes.
 542  With him followed the swift Abantes, hair grown long behind,
 543  spearmen eager with outstretched ash-wood spears
 544  to tear the cuirasses on the chests of their enemies.
 545  With him followed forty black ships.
 546  And those who held Athens, that well-built citadel,
 547  the district of great-hearted Erechtheus, whom once Athena[22]
 548  raised, Zeus's daughter — the grain-giving earth had borne him —
 549  and settled in Athens, in her own rich temple,
 550  where the young men of the Athenians seek his favor
 551  with bulls and rams as the years come round —
 552  these were led by Menestheus, son of Peteos.
 553  No man on the face of the earth was his equal
 554  at marshaling horses and shield-bearing men;
 555  Nestor alone rivaled him, for he was older.
 556  With him followed fifty black ships.
 557  And Ajax led twelve ships from Salamis,[23]
 558  and brought them and set them where the Athenian ranks stood.
 559  And those who held Argos and walled Tiryns,
 560  Hermione and Asine that lie on the deep gulf,
 561  Troezen, Eïones, and vine-clad Epidaurus,
 562  and the young Achaean men who held Aegina and Mases —
 563  these were led by Diomedes, good at the war-cry,
 564  and Sthenelus, dear son of famed Capaneus;
 565  and with them went a third, Euryalus, a godlike man,
 566  son of lord Mecisteus, Talaus' son;
 567  but the leader of them all was Diomedes, good at the war-cry.
 568  With them followed eighty black ships.
 569  And those who held Mycenae, that well-built citadel,
 570  and wealthy Corinth and well-built Cleonae;
 571  who dwelt in Orneiae and lovely Araethyrea
 572  and Sicyon, where Adrastus first was king;
 573  who held Hyperesia and steep Gonoessa
 574  and Pellene; who dwelt around Aegium
 575  and through all Aegialus and around broad Helice —
 576  of their hundred ships the leader was the lord Agamemnon,
 577  Atreus' son. With him followed by far the most
 578  and the best troops; and he among them, clad in gleaming bronze,
 579  glorying, stood out preeminent among all the heroes,
 580  because he was the best, and led by far the most men.
 581  And those who held hollow Lacedaemon full of ravines,
 582  Pharis and Sparta and Messe of the many doves;
 583  who dwelt in Bryseiae and lovely Augeae;
 584  who held Amyclae and Helos, the citadel by the sea;
 585  who held Laas and dwelt around Oetylus —
 586  these his brother led, Menelaus, good at the war-cry,
 587  with sixty ships; and they armed apart.
 588  He himself moved among them, trusting his own eagerness,
 589  urging them to war; and beyond all others his heart was set
 590  on avenging the struggles and groans of Helen.
 591  And those who dwelt in Pylos and lovely Arene,
 592  Thryon, the ford of Alpheius, and well-built Aepy;
 593  who lived in Cyparisseeis and Amphigeneia,
 594  Pteleos and Helos and Dorium — where the Muses[24]
 595  met Thamyris the Thracian and ended his singing,
 596  as he came from Oechalia, from Eurytus the Oechalian:
 597  for he had vowed in his boasting that he would win, even if
 598  the Muses themselves should sing, the daughters of aegis-bearing Zeus;
 599  and in their anger they maimed him: they took away
 600  his wondrous song and made him forget his harping —
 601  these were led by the Gerenian horseman Nestor.
 602  With him ninety hollow ships sailed in order.
 603  And those who held Arcadia under the steep mountain of Cyllene,
 604  beside the tomb of Aepytus, where men fight hand to hand;
 605  who dwelt in Pheneos and Orchomenus rich in flocks,
 606  Rhipe and Stratie and windswept Enispe;
 607  who held Tegea and lovely Mantinea;
 608  who held Stymphelus and dwelt in Parrhasia —
 609  these were led by Ancaeus's son, the lord Agapenor,
 610  with sixty ships, and in each ship many
 611  Arcadian men went aboard, skilled fighters.
 612  For the lord of men Agamemnon himself had given them
 613  the well-benched ships to cross the wine-dark sea —
 614  Atreus' son — since the works of the sea were no concern of theirs.
 615  And those who dwelt in Buprasium and brilliant Elis,
 616  all that Hyrmine and Myrsinus at the land's edge
 617  and the Olenian rock and Alesium enclose between them —
 618  of these there were four leaders, and ten swift ships
 619  followed each man, with many Epeians aboard.
 620  Two of them, Amphimachus and Thalpius, led —
 621  sons, one of Cteatus, one of Eurytus, of Actor's line;
 622  of the third company strong Diores was leader, Amarynceus' son;
 623  and of the fourth godlike Polyxeinus was leader,
 624  son of lord Agasthenes, Augeias' son.
 625  And those from Dulichium and the holy Echinae,
 626  the islands that lie across the sea facing Elis —
 627  these were led by Meges, the equal of Ares,
 628  Phyleus' son, whom the horseman Phyleus dear to Zeus begot —
 629  who once, angered at his father, had moved away to Dulichium.
 630  With him followed forty black ships.
 631  And Odysseus led the great-hearted Cephallenians,
 632  who held Ithaca and Neriton with quivering leaves;
 633  who dwelt in Crocyleia and rugged Aegilips;
 634  who held Zacynthus; who dwelt around Samos;
 635  who held the mainland and dwelt on the facing shores —
 636  these Odysseus led, the equal of Zeus in counsel,
 637  and with him followed twelve ships with cheeks of vermilion.[25]
 638  And the Aetolians were led by Thoas, Andraemon's son:
 639  they who dwelt in Pleuron and Olenus and Pylene,
 640  Chalcis by the shore and rocky Calydon.
 641  For the sons of great-hearted Oeneus were no more,
 642  and he himself was no more, and fair-haired Meleager was dead;
 643  so on Thoas the whole rule of the Aetolians had been laid.
 644  With him followed forty black ships.
 645  Of the Cretans, Idomeneus, famed with the spear, was leader:
 646  they who held Cnossos and walled Gortyn,
 647  Lyctus and Miletus and chalk-white Lycastus,
 648  Phaestus and Rhytium, fine cities to live in,
 649  and the others who dwelt through hundred-citied Crete.
 650  Of these Idomeneus, famed with the spear, was leader,
 651  with Meriones, the equal of Enyalius, slayer of men.
 652  With them followed eighty black ships.
 653  And Tlepolemus, Heracles' son, tall and strong,
 654  led nine ships from Rhodes, of the lordly Rhodians,
 655  who dwelt through Rhodes in three divisions:
 656  Lindus, Ialysus, and chalk-white Cameirus.
 657  Their leader was Tlepolemus, famed with the spear,
 658  whom Astyocheia bore to the might of Heracles —
 659  he brought her out of Ephyra, from the river Selleïs,
 660  after sacking many towns of Zeus-nurtured fighting men.
 661  Now when Tlepolemus was grown in the well-built hall,
 662  he killed, on the instant, his own father's dear uncle,
 663  Licymnius, scion of Ares, already growing old;
 664  and quickly he built ships, gathered a great following,
 665  and fled away over the sea, for the others threatened him —
 666  the sons and the grandsons of the might of Heracles.
 667  He came at last to Rhodes, a wanderer, a man of sorrows;
 668  and there they settled in three parts, tribe by tribe, and were loved
 669  by Zeus, who is king over gods and men,
 670  and the son of Cronos poured wondrous wealth upon them.
 671  And Nireus led three balanced ships from Syme —[26]
 672  Nireus, son of Aglaïa and lord Charopus;
 673  Nireus, the most beautiful man who came under Ilion
 674  of all the Danaans, after the blameless son of Peleus.
 675  But he was a slight man, and few people followed him.
 676  And those who held Nisyrus and Crapathus and Casus,
 677  and Cos, city of Eurypylus, and the Calydnae islands —
 678  these were led by Pheidippus and Antiphus,
 679  two sons of lord Thessalus, Heracles' son.
 680  With them thirty hollow ships sailed in order.
 681  Now all those who dwelt in Pelasgian Argos,
 682  who lived in Alos and Alope and Trachis;
 683  who held Phthia and Hellas of the beautiful women —
 684  and they were called Myrmidons, and Hellenes, and Achaeans —
 685  of their fifty ships Achilles was captain.
 686  But they had no thought of grim-voiced war;[27]
 687  there was no one to lead them into the ranks.
 688  For swift-footed brilliant Achilles lay among the ships,
 689  in wrath over the girl, Briseis of the lovely hair,
 690  whom he had taken out of Lyrnessus with great toil,
 691  when he sacked Lyrnessus and the walls of Thebe
 692  and cut down Mynes and Epistrophus, the spear-mad
 693  sons of lord Euenus, Selepius' son.
 694  Grieving for her he lay there; but he was soon to rise.
 695  And those who held Phylace and flowering Pyrasus,
 696  Demeter's precinct, and Iton, mother of flocks,
 697  Antron by the sea, and Pteleos deep in meadow-grass —
 698  these warlike Protesilaus had led
 699  while he lived; but by then the black earth held him under.
 700  His wife, cheeks torn with mourning, was left behind in Phylace,
 701  his house but half-built: a Dardanian man had killed him[28]
 702  as he leapt from his ship, far the first of the Achaeans.
 703  Yet the men were not leaderless, though they longed for their leader:
 704  Podarces, scion of Ares, set them in order,
 705  son of Iphiclus rich in flocks, Phylacus' son,
 706  own brother of great-hearted Protesilaus,
 707  the younger-born; the elder and the better man
 708  was the hero Protesilaus the warlike. So the people
 709  lacked no leader, though they longed for the brave man gone.
 710  With him followed forty black ships.
 711  And those who dwelt in Pherae beside Lake Boebeïs,
 712  in Boebe and Glaphyrae and well-built Iolcus —
 713  these Admetus' dear son led with eleven ships:
 714  Eumelus, whom Alcestis bore to Admetus —
 715  brilliant among women, loveliest of the daughters of Pelias.
 716  And those who dwelt in Methone and Thaumacia;
 717  who held Meliboea and rugged Olizon —
 718  these Philoctetes led, the master bowman,[29]
 719  with seven ships; and in each one fifty rowers
 720  had come aboard, all skilled bowmen, strong fighters.
 721  But he lay on an island, suffering hard pains,
 722  on sacred Lemnos, where the sons of the Achaeans had left him
 723  tormented by the evil wound of the murderous water-snake.
 724  There he lay grieving. But soon the Argives by their ships
 725  were to remember lord Philoctetes.
 726  Yet the men were not leaderless, though they longed for their leader:
 727  Medon set them in order, Oïleus' bastard son,
 728  whom Rhene bore to Oïleus, sacker of cities.
 729  And those who held Tricca and terraced Ithome;
 730  who held Oechalia, city of Eurytus the Oechalian —
 731  these were led by the two sons of Asclepius,
 732  the good healers Podaleirius and Machaon.
 733  With them thirty hollow ships sailed in order.
 734  And those who held Ormenion and the spring Hypereia;
 735  who held Asterium and the white peaks of Titanus —
 736  these Eurypylus led, Euaemon's shining son.
 737  With him followed forty black ships.
 738  And those who held Argissa; who dwelt in Gyrtone,
 739  Orthe and Elone and the white city Oloosson —
 740  these were led by Polypoetes, steadfast in battle,
 741  son of Peirithous, whom immortal Zeus begot;
 742  famed Hippodameia bore him to Peirithous
 743  on the day he took vengeance on the shaggy beast-men
 744  and drove them out of Pelion, and pushed them to the Aethices.
 745  Not alone: with him was Leonteus, scion of Ares,
 746  son of high-hearted Coronus, Caeneus' son.
 747  With them followed forty black ships.
 748  And Gouneus led two and twenty ships from Cyphus,
 749  and with him followed the Enienes and the battle-steadfast Peraebians,
 750  who set their homes around wintry Dodona;
 751  who worked the fields around lovely Titaressus,
 752  the river that pours his fair-flowing water into Peneius —
 753  yet he does not mingle with the silver eddies of Peneius,
 754  but flows along on top of him like oil:
 755  for he is a branch broken off the water of Styx, the dread oath.
 756  And the Magnetes were led by Prothoos, Tenthredon's son:
 757  they who dwelt around Peneius and Pelion
 758  of the quivering leaves. Swift Prothoos was their leader,
 759  and with him followed forty black ships.

 760  These, then, were the leaders and lords of the Danaans.
 761  Now which was far the best of them — tell me, Muse —
 762  of the men, and of the horses, that followed the sons of Atreus?
 763  Best by far of the horses were the mares of Pheres' line,
 764  which Eumelus drove: swift-footed as birds,
 765  matched in coat and matched in years, their backs as level as a plumb-line.
 766  Apollo of the silver bow had raised them in Pereia,
 767  both of them mares, carrying with them the panic of war.
 768  And of the men, far the best was Telamonian Ajax —
 769  while Achilles kept his wrath: for he was mightiest by far,
 770  he, and the horses that carried the blameless son of Peleus.
 771  But he lay among his beaked seagoing ships,
 772  nursing his rage at Agamemnon, shepherd of the people,
 773  Atreus' son; and his men along the surf-line of the sea
 774  amused themselves throwing the discus and the hunting-spear,
 775  and shooting with bows; and the horses, each beside its chariot,
 776  stood cropping lotus and marsh-grown parsley,
 777  while the chariots lay well covered in the huts
 778  of their lords; and the men, longing for their leader dear to Ares,
 779  drifted here and there through the camp, and did not fight.

 780  On the others came, as if all the land were swept with fire;
 781  and the earth groaned beneath them, as under Zeus who delights in thunder
 782  in his anger, when he lashes the earth around Typhoeus[30]
 783  among the Arimi, where they say Typhoeus has his bed:
 784  so the earth groaned greatly under their feet
 785  as they came on; and very swiftly they crossed the plain.

 786  And to the Trojans went wind-footed swift Iris, a messenger[31]
 787  from aegis-bearing Zeus, with a grievous message.
 788  They were holding assembly at Priam's doors,
 789  all gathered together, the young men and the old.
 790  Standing close, swift-footed Iris spoke to them,
 791  likening her voice to Priam's son Polites,
 792  who sat as lookout of the Trojans, trusting the speed of his feet,
 793  on the very top of the tomb of old Aesyetes,
 794  watching for when the Achaeans should rush out from their ships;
 795  in his likeness swift-footed Iris spoke:
 796  "Old man, endless talk is always dear to you,
 797  as once it was in peacetime; but relentless war is upon us.
 798  Truly I have gone into very many battles of men,
 799  but never yet have I seen such a host, so great:
 800  for like the leaves, like the sands, in their multitude
 801  they are coming over the plain to fight for the city.
 802  Hector, you above all I charge to do this:
 803  there are many allies through Priam's great city,
 804  and tongue differs from tongue among men of scattered lands.
 805  Let each man give the word to those he rules;
 806  let him marshal his own townsmen, and lead them out."
 807  So she spoke, and Hector did not fail to know the goddess's voice.
 808  At once he dissolved the assembly, and they rushed to arms.
 809  All the gates were flung open, and the host streamed out,
 810  foot-soldiers and horsemen; and a great din arose.

 811  There is a steep mound out before the city,
 812  apart in the plain, with open ground on every side;
 813  men call it Batieia,
 814  but the immortals call it the tomb of Myrine, light of step.[32]
 815  There the Trojans and their allies formed their divisions.

 816  The Trojans were led by great Hector of the flashing helmet,[33]
 817  Priam's son; with him by far the most and the best
 818  troops armed themselves, eager with their spears.
 819  The Dardanians were led by the good son of Anchises,
 820  Aeneas, whom brilliant Aphrodite bore to Anchises
 821  on the spurs of Ida, a goddess bedded with a mortal.
 822  Not alone: with him were Antenor's two sons,
 823  Archelochus and Acamas, skilled in all fighting.
 824  And those who dwelt in Zeleia under the lowest foot of Ida,
 825  men of wealth, who drink the dark water of Aesepus,
 826  Trojans — these were led by Lycaon's shining son,
 827  Pandarus, to whom Apollo himself gave the bow.
 828  And those who held Adresteia and the district of Apaesus;
 829  who held Pityeia and the steep mount of Tereia —
 830  these were led by Adrestus and Amphius of the linen corslet,
 831  two sons of Merops of Percote, who beyond all men
 832  knew the seer's art, and would not let his sons
 833  go to man-destroying war; but the two would not
 834  obey him: the fates of black death were leading them on.
 835  And those who dwelt around Percote and Practius;
 836  who held Sestus and Abydos and brilliant Arisbe —
 837  these were led by Hyrtacus' son Asius, marshal of men —
 838  Asius, Hyrtacus' son, whom horses carried from Arisbe,
 839  tall and fire-colored, from the river Selleïs.
 840  And Hippothous led the tribes of the Pelasgian spearmen,
 841  those who dwelt in deep-soiled Larisa;
 842  these were led by Hippothous and Pylaeus, scion of Ares,
 843  two sons of Pelasgian Lethus, Teutamus' son.
 844  And the Thracians were led by Acamas and the hero Peiroos —
 845  all those the strong-flowing Hellespont encloses.
 846  And Euphemus was captain of the Ciconian spearmen,
 847  son of Zeus-nurtured Troezenus, Ceas' son.
 848  And Pyraechmes led the Paeonians of the curved bows,
 849  from far away, from Amydon, from the wide-flowing Axius —
 850  Axius, whose water spreads fairest over the earth.
 851  And the Paphlagonians were led by Pylaemenes of the shaggy heart,
 852  from the Eneti, where the breed of wild mules is born:
 853  they who held Cytorus and dwelt around Sesamus,
 854  whose famed homes stood around the river Parthenius,
 855  Cromna and Aegialus and the heights of Erythini.
 856  And the Halizones were led by Odius and Epistrophus,
 857  from far away, from Alybe, where silver has its birthplace.
 858  And the Mysians were led by Chromis, and Ennomus the bird-reader —
 859  but his birds did not save him from black doom:
 860  he was broken under the hands of the swift-footed son of Aeacus[34]
 861  in the river, where Achilles cut down Trojans and others with them.
 862  And Phorcys led the Phrygians, with godlike Ascanius,
 863  from far Ascania; and they were eager to fight in the press of battle.
 864  And the Maeonians were led by Mesthles and Antiphus,
 865  the two sons of Talaemenes, whom the Gygaean lake bore;
 866  they led the Maeonians, born under Tmolus.
 867  And Nastes was captain of the Carians of barbarous speech,[35]
 868  who held Miletus and the mountain of Phthires, dense with leaves,
 869  the streams of Maeander and the steep peaks of Mycale;
 870  these were led by Amphimachus and Nastes —
 871  Nastes and Amphimachus, the shining children of Nomion.
 872  And one of them went to war decked in gold, like a girl —
 873  the fool: it did not ward off wretched destruction from him;
 874  he was broken under the hands of the swift-footed son of Aeacus
 875  in the river, and war-minded Achilles carried off the gold.
 876  And Sarpedon led the Lycians, with blameless Glaucus,
 877  from far Lycia, from the eddying Xanthus.

Notes

[1] (2.6) "baneful Dream" (οὖλον ὄνειρον) — Dream is a personified agent
    here, capitalized accordingly. That Zeus sends a deliberately lying
    dream scandalized ancient readers (Plato cites this passage
    disapprovingly, Republic 383a); the poem itself is unembarrassed —
    the deception is the mechanism of Zeus's promise to Thetis.

[2] (2.11) "long-haired Achaeans" (κάρη κομόωντες Ἀχαιοί) — stock epithet
    of the army, literally "letting the hair grow long on the head";
    always so rendered.

[3] (2.23) "breaker of horses" (ἱπποδάμοιο) — here of Atreus; later in
    the poem it is the standing epithet of the Trojans and of Hector
    ("horse-taming Trojans," 230). Rendered "breaker(s) of horses" for
    individuals, "horse-taming" for the people.

[4] (2.60-70) The Dream repeats Zeus's instructions verbatim, and
    Agamemnon repeats them verbatim again to the council — standard epic
    messenger technique. Zenodotus' text drastically condensed such
    repetitions (apparatus, Line 60-70), and he omitted 157-168 below
    (Hera's speech restated by Athena) entirely, replacing it with a
    single line (apparatus, Line 156, 157-168). The vulgate keeps the
    full repetitions; so does this translation, in identical English
    each time.

[5] (2.73) "I will test them with words, as is the custom" (ἣ θέμις
    ἐστί) — the "testing" (peira) is one of the poem's strangest plot
    devices: no custom of testing armies is otherwise known, and the
    plan nearly destroys the expedition. The phrase may mean only "as
    is my right." Translated neutrally; the narrative logic is left as
    the poem leaves it — precarious.

[6] (2.101-108) The scepter's pedigree — Hephaestus, Zeus, Hermes,
    Pelops, Atreus, Thyestes, Agamemnon — is a title-deed in miniature:
    Agamemnon's authority is literally handed down from Zeus. "The
    slayer of Argus" (ἀργεϊφόντης) is Hermes' fixed epithet, of disputed
    meaning ("Argus-slayer" is the traditional reading and is kept);
    "the guide" (διάκτορος) is his companion title.

[7] (2.111-118) Zenodotus athetized this opening of the speech
    (apparatus). Lines 111-118 recur nearly verbatim at 9.18-25, where
    Agamemnon speaks them in earnest; here they are play-acted despair.
    Note the vocabulary: Zeus "bound me in heavy blindness" (ἄτη) — the
    same word Achilles used of him at 1.412, and Agamemnon concedes at
    378 that "it was I who began the anger."

[8] (2.157) "Unwearied one" (Ἀτρυτώνη) — obscure cult title of Athena,
    traditionally derived from "not to be worn down"; rendered by that
    traditional sense.

[9] (2.169) "the equal of Zeus in counsel" (Διὶ μῆτιν ἀτάλαντον) —
    Odysseus' loftiest epithet, crediting him with mētis, the cunning
    practical intelligence that is also Zeus's; always so rendered.

[10] (2.173) "Zeus-sprung son of Laertes, Odysseus of many devices"
     (διογενὲς Λαερτιάδη πολυμήχανʼ Ὀδυσσεῦ) — the full-line address
     formula used for Odysseus throughout both epics; πολυμήχανος
     ("of many devices") is kept distinct from πολύμητις ("resourceful").

[11] (2.193-197) Aristarchus athetized these five lines, and they are
     independently absent from Xenophon's quotation of the passage
     (Memorabilia 1.2.56) — two converging hints they may not be
     original (apparatus, Line 193-197; disputed_lines.md #3). Without
     them, Odysseus' speech to the kings loses its "he is only testing"
     explanation. Translated as printed; the two-tier treatment of kings
     and commoners on either side of this line is in any case secure.
     One crux inside the passage: 194 is printed here as a rhetorical
     question ("Did we not all hear in council what he said?" — so
     Lattimore), but Kirk reads it as a statement — "in council we did
     NOT all hear what he said" — which better explains why the man
     addressed needs Odysseus's telling. The question-rendering stands;
     the alternative is recorded.

[12] (2.204) "The rule of many is no good thing: let there be one ruler"
     (οὐκ ἀγαθὸν πολυκοιρανίη· εἷς κοίρανος ἔστω) — the most quoted
     political sentence in Homer (Aristotle cites it in the Metaphysics
     in defense of monarchy). In context it is crowd-control rhetoric,
     not constitutional theory.

[13] (2.212-219) Thersites is the only common soldier in the Iliad who
     is named, described, and given a speech — and the description is a
     catalogue of deformities, the epic's physiognomic shorthand for
     baseness (contrast every hero's beauty). His name suggests θέρσος,
     "audacity." Zenodotus athetized 220-223 (apparatus). Notice that
     his complaints against Agamemnon are substantively Achilles'
     complaints from Book 1, voiced one rung down the social ladder —
     and answered with a beating the assembly applauds.

[14] (2.318-319) "made him a sign conspicuous" — the vulgate reads
     ἀρίζηλον ("very plain to see"): the petrified serpent becomes a
     lasting marker of the portent. An ancient variant read ἀΐζηλον
     ("unseen"): the god made the serpent vanish. Aristarchus athetized
     319 (the petrification line) outright (apparatus, Line 318, 319).
     Translated from the printed text: conspicuous, then turned to stone.

[15] (2.336) "the Gerenian horseman Nestor" (Γερήνιος ἱππότα Νέστωρ) —
     his fixed title henceforth; "Gerenian" was obscure already in
     antiquity (usually connected with Gerenia in Messenia, where Nestor
     was said to have been raised). Always so rendered.

[16] (2.356) "the struggles and groans of Helen" (Ἑλένης ὁρμήματά τε
     στοναχάς τε) — ambiguous since antiquity: either Helen's own
     strivings and griefs (a Helen carried off unwilling), or the
     struggles and groans fought and suffered over her. The English
     keeps the genitive's ambiguity. The line recurs at 590 of Menelaus.

[17] (2.408) "good at the war-cry" (βοὴν ἀγαθός) — fixed epithet of
     Menelaus and Diomedes: the carrying battlefield voice as a command
     skill. Always so rendered. Menelaus coming "unbidden" to his
     brother's feast was proverbial in antiquity.

[18] (2.447-449) The aegis (see Book 1, note [21]) here gets its fullest
     description: a hundred all-gold tassels, "each worth a hundred
     oxen" (ἑκατόμβοιος) — value is reckoned in cattle, the Homeric
     world's standard of price; there is no money in the poem.

[19] (2.484-493) The poet's second and most elaborate invocation of the
     Muses, marking the Catalogue as a feat of memory beyond unaided
     human power ("ten tongues, ten mouths"). The Muses know because
     they are present and see; the poet otherwise has only kleos —
     "the report" — at second hand.

[20] (2.494-759) The Catalogue of Ships: 29 Achaean contingents, some
     190 place names, 1,186 ships by the traditional count. It has been
     argued since antiquity to reflect an originally separate
     composition (its geography fits mainland Greece of an earlier era
     better than the poem's own dramatic situation — Boeotia leads,
     plausibly from the muster at Aulis), and several manuscripts omit
     the whole span (apparatus, Line 494-877: om.; disputed_lines.md
     #2). Translated as fully integral, in the same line-for-line verse
     as the narrative; see commentary.

[21] (2.528-530) The disambiguation of the two Ajaxes. Zenodotus
     athetized 528 and Aristarchus 529-530 (apparatus) — the "linen
     corslet" (λινοθώρηξ) bothered critics, linen being light armor at
     odds with heroic bronze. Kept: the lesser Ajax's smallness and
     spear-skill are both plot-relevant later.

[22] (2.546-551) Erechtheus, the primeval Athenian king, is "borne by
     the grain-giving earth" and raised by Athena in her own temple —
     the earliest statement of the Athenian autochthony myth, and one of
     the very few references to hero-cult practice (annual bull and ram
     offerings) in the Iliad.

[23] (2.557-558) The most notorious couplet in the poem. Aristarchus
     athetized 558, and ancient tradition (Strabo 9.1.10, Diogenes
     Laertius 1.48) reports that Solon or Pisistratus inserted it to
     support Athens's claim to Salamis against Megara — the Megarians
     circulated a rival version continuing Ajax's line with Megarian
     towns instead (quoted in the apparatus at Line 557-558). Whether
     or not the accusation is true, Ajax — the war's second-best
     fighter — receives a strikingly minimal two-line entry. Translated
     as printed, with the controversy recorded here (disputed_lines.md
     #2a).

[24] (2.594-600) Thamyris the Thracian bard boasted he could outsing
     the Muses; they "maimed" him (πηρόν — later tradition specifies
     blinding, but the word is unspecific), stripped his song, and made
     him forget his lyre-craft. A dark in-poem parable of song as the
     Muses' revocable gift, placed pointedly just after the poet's own
     humble invocation of them.

[25] (2.637) "ships with cheeks of vermilion" (μιλτοπάρῃοι) — Odysseus'
     ships have bows painted with red ochre; the word transfers the
     "cheek" of the human face-epithets to the ship's prow.

[26] (2.671-675) The Nireus entry: his name opens three consecutive
     lines (anaphora unique in the Catalogue) — beautiful, well-born,
     and militarily negligible. Zenodotus athetized 673-675 and omitted
     674 (apparatus). The rhetorical polish of the entry made it famous;
     it is kept at full strength.

[27] (2.686-694) Zenodotus athetized this Achilles-paragraph (apparatus,
     Line 686-694). It is the Catalogue's load-bearing link to the
     poem's plot: the fifty ships are mustered, and their leader lies
     apart "in wrath over the girl" — with the quiet promise "but he
     was soon to rise."

[28] (2.698-702) Protesilaus, "far the first of the Achaeans" ashore,
     killed as he leapt from his ship (an oracle had promised death to
     the first to land — the poem alludes without telling). His "house
     but half-built" (δόμος ἡμιτελής) is ambiguous: an unfinished hall,
     or a marriage cut off half-made; the wife with torn cheeks favors
     hearing both.

[29] (2.716-725) Philoctetes, master of Heracles' bow, abandoned on
     Lemnos with the festering water-snake wound. "Soon the Argives
     were to remember" him: prophecy held that Troy could not fall
     without the bow — the story of Sophocles' Philoctetes. One of the
     Catalogue's several forward windows beyond the Iliad's own frame.

[30] (2.782-783) Typhoeus, the monstrous challenger of Zeus, lies
     lashed by his thunderbolts "among the Arimi" (location already
     disputed in antiquity — Cilicia, Lydia, and volcanic Ischia were
     all proposed). The marching army makes the earth groan like Zeus's
     cosmic punishment: the grandest of the muster-similes.

[31] (2.786) "wind-footed swift Iris" (ποδήνεμος ὠκέα Ἶρις) — the
     rainbow goddess as messenger of Zeus; her fixed epithet, always
     so rendered.

[32] (2.813-814) Men call the mound Batieia; the gods call it "the tomb
     of Myrine" (an Amazon, in later tradition). Second instance of
     divine-versus-human double naming (cf. Briareus/Aegaeon, Book 1
     note [33]) — the gods' name preserves the older, true history of
     the landmark.

[33] (2.816) "Hector of the flashing helmet" (κορυθαίολος Ἕκτωρ) — his
     defining epithet, first appearance in the poem; literally "with
     glancing/shimmering helm." Always so rendered.

[34] (2.860-861) Aristarchus athetized these lines (and obelized the
     parallel pair at 874-875): they anticipate, in the future tense of
     the narrator, Achilles' massacre in the river Scamander — which
     belongs to Book 21 — and ancient critics disliked the Catalogue
     "spoiling" it. Kept: the Trojan catalogue's habit of glancing at
     its captains' deaths is part of its elegiac texture.

[35] (2.867) "Carians of barbarous speech" (βαρβαροφώνων) — the first
     occurrence in Greek literature of any "barbar-" word. Not yet the
     later blanket term "barbarian": in the whole poem only the Carians
     get this label, and the Trojans themselves speak with the Achaeans
     unhindered. Rendered "of barbarous speech" to keep the word's
     echoic, language-centered sense.
